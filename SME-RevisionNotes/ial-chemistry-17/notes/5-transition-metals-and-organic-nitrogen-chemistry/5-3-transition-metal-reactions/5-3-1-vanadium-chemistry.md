## Vanadium - Colours & Oxidation States

- Vanadium is a transition metal which has variable oxidation states
- The table below shows the important ones you need to be aware of

![colours-and-oxidation-states](../../../assets/752c0ac6dfa6-colours-and-oxidation-states.png)

- The variation in oxidation states of transition metal ions is illustrated by the reaction of zinc with ammonium vanadate(V) (also known as ammonium metavanadate) under acidic conditions
- Vanadium had four common oxidation states, from +2 to +5
- Zinc is a reducing agent that is capable to reducing vanadium(V) to vanadium(II) in a sequence of steps accompanied by vibrant colour changes

![Colours of vanadium, downloadable AS & A Level Biology revision notes](../../../assets/c417fb7347f9-colours-of-vanadium.png)

***The reduction of vanadate(V) ions by zinc in acidic conditions is one of the most colourful reactions in chemistry***

## Vanadium - Interconversions of Ions

- For vanadium, we need to consider the following standard electrode potential values

![6-3-1-vanadium-table-2](../../../assets/a728cef61239-6-3-1-vanadium-table-2.png)

- The half equations are arranged from the most negative *E*<sup>θ </sup>at the top to the most positive *E*<sup>θ </sup>at the bottom

  - The strongest oxidising agent is the reactant from the electrode reaction with the most positive standard electrode potential
  
    - In this case, the most positive value is + 1.00 V, which means that VO<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> is the strongest oxidising agent
  - The strongest reducing agent is the product from the electrode reaction with the most negative standard electrode potential
  
    - In this case, the most negative value is – 1.18 V, which means that V is the strongest reducing agent
- For all of the following reactions, we will use zinc as the reducing agent

#### Reduction of V from +5 to +4 by Zn

- Vanadium is being reduced from an oxidation number of +5 to +4 in half equation 5
- So, the two half equations we need to consider are:

  - 2. Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn (s)     *E*<sup>θ</sup> = – 0.76 V
  - 5. VO<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> (aq) + 2H<sup>+</sup> (aq) + e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ VO<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O (l)     *E*<sup>θ</sup> = + 1.00 V
- Half equation 5 has the most positive standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the reduction reaction
  - VO<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> (aq) + 2H<sup>+</sup> (aq) + e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ VO<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O (l)
- Half equation 2 has the least positive standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the oxidation reaction and needs to be reversed
  - Zn (s) $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup>
- We can obtain the overall equation by combining the reduction equation (half equation 5) and the oxidation equation (the reversed half equation 2)

  - **Remember: **When combining half equations, they must have the same number of electrons in both equations | **2VO**<sub>**2**</sub><sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** ** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **2VO**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) ** |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Zn (s)** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **Zn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** ** |
  - The equal number of electrons on both sides of the equation cancel out to give the overall equation:

**2VO**<sub>**2**</sub><sup>**+ **</sup>**(aq) + 4H**<sup>**+ **</sup>**(aq) + Zn (s) **$\rightleftharpoons$** 2VO**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + Zn**<sup>**2+ **</sup>**(aq) + 2H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l)**

#### Reduction of V from +3 to +2 by Zn

- Vanadium is being reduced from an oxidation number of +3 to +2 in half equation 3
- So, the two half equations we need to consider are:

  - 2. Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn (s)     *E*<sup>θ</sup> = – 0.76 V
  - 3. V<sup>3+</sup> (aq) + e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ V<sup>2+</sup> (aq)      *E*<sup>θ</sup> = – 0.26 V
- Half equation 3 has the most positive / least negative standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the reduction reaction
  - V<sup>3+</sup> (aq) + e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ V<sup>2+</sup> (aq)
- Half equation 2 has the least positive standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the oxidation reaction and needs to be reversed
  - Zn (s) $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup>
- We can obtain the overall equation by combining the reduction equation (half equation 5) and the oxidation equation (the reversed half equation 2)

  - **Remember: **When combining half equations, they must have the same number of electrons in both equations | **2V**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** ** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **2V**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) ** |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Zn (s)** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **Zn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** ** |
  - The equal number of electrons on both sides of the equation cancel out to give the overall equation:

**2V**<sup>**3+ **</sup>**(aq) + Zn (s) **$\rightleftharpoons$** 2V**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + Zn**<sup>**2+ **</sup>**(aq) **

#### Reduction of V from +2 to 0 by Zn

- Vanadium is being reduced from an oxidation number of +3 to +2 in half equation 1
- So, the two half equations we need to consider are:

  - 2. Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn (s)     *E*<sup>θ</sup> = – 0.76 V
  - 1. V<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ V (s)      *E*<sup>θ</sup> = – 1.18 V
- Half equation 1 has the most negative standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the oxidation reaction and needs to be reversed
  - V (s) $\rightleftharpoons$ V<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup>
- Half equation 2 has the most positive / least negative standard electrode potential, *E*<sup>θ</sup>, value

  - Therefore, this is the reduction reaction
  - Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> $\rightleftharpoons$ Zn (s)
- We can obtain the overall equation by combining the reduction equation (half equation 5) and the oxidation equation (the reversed half equation 2)

  - When combining these half equations, there is no need to change them as they have the same number of electrons in both equations | **V (s) ** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **V**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** ** |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Zn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>**  ** | $\rightleftharpoons$** ** | **Zn (s)** |
  - The equal number of electrons on both sides of the equation cancel out to give the overall equation:

**V**<sup>** **</sup>**(s) + Zn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) **$\rightleftharpoons$** V**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + Zn**<sup>** **</sup>**(s) **

- Zn is not electron releasing with respect to V<sup>2+</sup>

  - This means this reaction is not thermodynamically feasible
