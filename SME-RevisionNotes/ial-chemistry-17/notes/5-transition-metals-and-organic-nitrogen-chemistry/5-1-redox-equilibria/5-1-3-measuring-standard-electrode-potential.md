## Measuring Standard Electrode Potential

- There are three different types of half-cells that can be connected to a standard hydrogen electrode to measure standard electrode potential

  - A metal / metal ion half-cell
  - A non-metal / non-metal ion half-cell
  - An ion / ion half-cell (the ions are in different oxidation states)

#### Metal / metal-ion half-cell

![Metal_Metal Ion Half-Cell, downloadable AS & A Level Chemistry revision notes](../../../assets/3b2c7c9ae25b-5-4-2-metal-metal-ion-half-cell.png)

***Example of a metal / metal ion half-cell connected to a standard hydrogen electrode***

- An example of a metal/metal ion half-cell is the Ag<sup>+</sup>/ Ag half-cell

  - Ag is the metal
  - Ag<sup>+</sup> is the metal ion
- This half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode** and the two half-equations are:

**Ag**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ Ag (s)        *****E***<sup>**ꝋ **</sup>**= + 0.80 V**

**2H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ H**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g)        *****E***<sup>**ꝋ **</sup>**= 0.00 V **

- Since the Ag<sup>+</sup>/ Ag half-cell has a more positive *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value, this is the **positive pole **and the H<sup>+</sup>/H<sub>2</sub> half-cell is the **negative** pole
- The **standard cell potential (***E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ</sup>) is ***E***<sub>***cell***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = (+ 0.80) - (0.00) = + 0.80 V**
- The Ag<sup>+ </sup>ions are more likely to get **reduced **than the H<sup>+</sup> ions as it has a greater *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value

  - Reduction occurs at the **positive electrode**
  - Oxidation occurs at the **negative electrode**

#### Non-metal / non-metal ion half-cell

- In a **non-metal / non-metal ion **half-cell, **platinum **wire or foil is used as an electrode to make electrical contact with the solution

  - Like graphite, platinum is inert and does not take part in the reaction
  - The redox equilibrium is established on the platinum surface
- An example of a non-metal / non-metal ion is the Br<sub>2 </sub>/ Br<sup>-</sup> half-cell

  - Br<sub>2 </sub>is the non-metal
  - Br<sup>-</sup> is the non-metal ion
- The half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode** and the two half-equations are:

**Br**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ 2Br**<sup>**-**</sup>** (aq)        *****E***<sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = +1.09 V**

**2H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ H**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g)        *****E***<sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = 0.00 V   **

- The Br<sub>2 </sub>/ Br<sup>-</sup> half-cell is the **positive pole** and the H<sup>+ </sup>/ H<sub>2</sub> is the **negative** pole
- The *E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ </sup>is: ***E***<sub>***cell***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = (+ 1.09) - (0.00) = + 1.09 V**
- The Br<sub>2</sub> molecules are more likely to get **reduced **than H<sup>+</sup> as they have a greater *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value

![Non-Metal_Non-Metal Ion Half-Cell, downloadable AS & A Level Chemistry revision notes](../../../assets/882d9c603402-5-4-2-non-metal-non-metal-ion-half-cell.png)

***Example of a non-metal / non-metal ion half-cell connected to a standard hydrogen electrode***

#### Ion / Ion half-cell

- A **platinum electrode **is again used to form a half-cell of ions that are in **different oxidation states**
- An example of such a half-cell is the MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>- </sup>/ Mn<sup>2+</sup> half-cell

  - MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup> is an ion containing Mn with oxidation state +7
  - The Mn<sup>2+ </sup>ion contains Mn with oxidation state +2
- This half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode** and the two half-equations are:

**MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**-**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l)       *****E***<sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = +1.52 V**

**2H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**- **</sup>**⇌ H**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g)       *****E***<sup>**ꝋ **</sup>**= 0.00 V   **

- The H<sup>+</sup> ions are also present in the half-cell as they are required to convert MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>- </sup>into Mn<sup>2+ </sup>ions
- The MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>- </sup>/ Mn<sup>2+</sup> half-cell is the **positive pole** and the H<sup>+ </sup>/ H<sub>2</sub> is the **negative** pole
- The *E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ</sup> is ***E***<sub>***cell***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = (+ 1.52) - (0.00) = + 1.52 V**

***Ions in solution half cell***

#### The Salt Bridge

- The salt bridge completes the circuit in a voltaic (electrochemical) cell by allowing ions to move between the two half-cells
- It helps to **balance** the charges as electrons flow through the external wire
- A metal wire must not be used as a salt bridge

  - Metals allow electrons to flow, but a salt bridge **must carry ions**, not electrons
- **Potassium chloride** and **potassium nitrate** are commonly used to make the salt bridge because **chloride** and **nitrate** ions are:

  - Highly **soluble** in water
  - **Unreactive** with most ions in the half-cells
  - Unlikely to form **precipitates**
  - This ensures smooth ion flow and prevents disruption of the redox reactions

## Electromotive Force

#### Standard cell potential

- Once the ***E***<sup>ꝋ </sup>of a half-cell is known, the **potential difference** or **voltage** or** emf** of an **electrochemical cell **made up of any two half-cells can be calculated

  - These could be **any** half-cells and neither have to be a standard hydrogen electrode
- The **standard cell potential (***E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ</sup>) can be calculated by **subtracting **the **less** **positive** *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>from the **more positive ***E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value

  - The half-cell with the more positive *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value will be the **positive** pole
  
    - By convention this is shown on the right hand side in a conventional cell diagram, so is termed ** *****E***<sub>***right***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>
  - The half-cell with the less positive *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value will be the **negative **pole
  
    - By convention this is shown on the left hand side in a conventional cell diagram, so is termed ** *****E***<sub>***left***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>

***E***<sub>***cell***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = *****E***<sub>***right***</sub><sup>**ꝋ **</sup>**- *****E***<sub>***left***</sub><sup>**ꝋ   **</sup>

- Since oxidation is always on the left and reduction on the right, you can also use this version

***E***<sub>***cell***</sub><sup>**ꝋ**</sup>** = *****E***<sub>***reduction***</sub><sup>**ꝋ **</sup>**- *****E***<sub>***oxidation***</sub>

> **Worked Example**
> **Calculating the standard cell potential**
> 
> Calculate the standard cell potential for the electrochemical cell below and explain why the Cu<sup>2+ </sup>/ Cu half-cell is the positive pole. The half-equations are as follows:
> 
> Cu<sup>2+</sup>(aq) + 2e<sup>- </sup>⇌ Cu(s)      *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>= +0.34 V
> 
> Zn<sup>2+</sup>(aq) + 2e<sup>- </sup>⇌ Zn(s)      *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>= −0.76 V
> 
> ![Electrochemistry Calculations - Electrochemical Cell, downloadable AS & A Level Chemistry revision notes](assets/e3fb9206d8bf-5-4-electrochemistry-calculations-electrochemica.png)
> 
> **Answer**
> 
> **Step 1: **Calculate the standard cell potential. The copper is more positive so must be the right hand side.
> 
> *E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ</sup> = *E*<sub>*right*</sub><sup>ꝋ </sup>- *E*<sub>*left*</sub><sup>ꝋ   </sup>
> 
> *E*<sub>*cell*</sub><sup>ꝋ</sup> = (+0.34) - (-0.76)
> 
> = +1.10 V
> 
> The voltmeter will therefore give a value of +1.10 V
> 
> **Step 2: **Determine the positive and negative poles
> 
> The Cu<sup>2+ </sup>/ Cu  half-cell is the **positive** pole as its *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>is more positive than the *E*<sup>ꝋ </sup>value of the Zn<sup>2+ </sup>/ Zn half-cell
