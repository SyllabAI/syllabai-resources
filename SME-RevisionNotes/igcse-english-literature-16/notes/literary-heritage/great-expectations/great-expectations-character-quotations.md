# Great Expectations: Character Quotations

GCSE English Literature exam questions usually focus on a theme, a character or a relationship between two or more characters. Examiners reward responses that track the development of characters or themes through the novella.

When revising, try to consider quotes in terms of their narrative effects — how characters are presented, what attitudes or relationships are presented and why these ideas have been shown to the reader.

We’ve included the best Great Expectations quotes — with detailed analysis — to help you to revise and organised them by the following characters:

- **Pip**
- **Estella**
- **Miss Havisham**
- **Abel Magwitch **

> **Exam Hint**
> Examiners have said that a common feature of successful Great Expectations essays is that they start with a thesis statement or argument which broadly outlines the answer to the question. It is best to consider the whole novel in this initial argument as you are discussing the development of a theme or character throughout the text.
> 
> Something that will help you to use references in a cohesive and precise analysis is to keep your focus on the most important part. Here, we have included the “key word or phrase” in longer quotations so that you can remember the most important part.

## Great Expectations: Character quotations: Pip

> **Spec point** — `spcpt_RKxkkGkbZvhkXDvC`

## Pip

> **blockquote**
> **“But it’s so new here, and so strange, and so fine,—and melancholy—” **- Pip, Chapter 8

| ![Illustration of Pip in profile, wearing a high-collared shirt and a beige coat.](assets/21e09e039167-great-expectations-pip-2.png)<br><br>*Pip* | **Key word or phrase to memorise:  **“so new” and “so melancholy” | **What the quotation means: **Pip expresses his discomfort to Miss Havisham, explaining that he cannot play for her because the place is so unfamiliar, so odd, so beautiful, and also sad | **Theme: ***Social class* |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens builds sympathy for the small, scared Pip as he meets Miss Havisham:<br><br>  - His discomfort is conveyed through the use of a list<br>  - <u>[Repetition](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/repetition-definition/)</u> of “so” stresses his extreme reaction to her grand house<br>- Dickens presents differences in *social class* through contrasting settings:<br><br>  - Pip finds Miss Havisham’s mansion unfamiliar in its grandness, implied with “fine” and linked with “strange”<br>  - However, Pip also implies he is not used to the dark “melancholy” mood<br>  - Pip’s home, in contrast, is small, loud and warm |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“I wish you could put me right”** - Pip, Chapter 17

| ![Illustration of Pip in profile, wearing a high-collared shirt and a beige coat.](assets/21e09e039167-great-expectations-pip-2.png)<br><br>*Pip * | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“put me right” | **What the quotation means: **Pip expresses his longing for Biddy to make him love her and forget his desire to be a gentleman** ** | **Theme: **Ambition and self-improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens contrasts the relationships between Pip and Biddy and Pip and Estella:<br><br>  - Biddy, as Estella’s *<u>foil</u>*, is a good role model and companion for Pip<br>  - Pip acknowledges in “put me right” that he may be on the wrong course<br>  - This <u>[foreshadows](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/foreshadowing-definition/)</u> Pip’s eventual realisation that emotional integrity matters more than class status<br>- Nevertheless, the vague “wish” suggests Pip is more focused on his future:<br><br>  - This portrays his determined ambition to be a gentleman |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“There were days once, I know, that I did for a while forget; but I never shall forget these” ** - Pip, Chapter 57

| ![Illustration of Pip in profile, wearing a high-collared shirt and a beige coat.](assets/21e09e039167-great-expectations-pip-2.png)<br><br>*Pip* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“I did for a while forget” and “but I shall never forget these” | **What the quotation means: **Pip tells Joe that when he was focused on becoming a gentleman, he forgot the positive elements of a simple life with Joe, but now he has changed he will never forget his roots and the good times they have shared ** ** | **Theme: **Guilt and shame |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens’ <u>[bildungsroman](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-literature/bildungsroman-definition/)</u> presents Pip’s shame as he realises his earlier mistakes:<br><br>  - The repetition of “forget” refers to Pip’s misguided snobbery<br>  - The <u>conjunction</u> “but” signals the contrast of “a while” to “never”<br>- Pip’s admission to Joe shows him as self-aware and regretful about his actions:<br><br>  - His return to Joe marks his enlightenment as Dickens returns Pip to Joe and his childhood home |   |   |   |

## Great Expectations: Character quotations: Estella

> **Spec point** — `spcpt_ddDhfDRCMRQkX28k`

## Estella

> **blockquote**
> **“It meant, when it was given, that whoever had this house, could want nothing else. They must have been easily satisfied in those days, I should think” ** - Estella, Chapter 8

| ![Illustration of Estella in profile with brown hair in an intricate bun, wearing a pale pink dress.](assets/2ce0c9ede00e-great-expectations-estella-2.png)<br><br>*Estella* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“easily satisfied” | **What the quotation means: **Estella tells Pip that Miss Havisham’s mansion is called “Satis House” (which means “house of the satisfied”), but that she believes that the people who named it must have had low standards if they found this house to be satisfying ** ** | **Theme: ***Social class* |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens introduces Estella as cold and snobbish to present ideas about *social class*:<br><br>  - Her snobbish words portray her as spoilt and arrogant<br>- Dickens plays on the word “satisfied” as a [symbol](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/symbol/) for the characters’ dissatisfaction:<br><br>  - Dickens presents class divides as damaging for all parties<br>  - While Miss Havisham and Estella are wealthy, they are also miserable<br>  - Pip’s desire for wealth and status  does not bring him satisfaction |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“I have not bestowed my tenderness anywhere. I have never had any such thing” **- Estella, Chapter 29

| ![Illustration of Estella in profile with brown hair in an intricate bun, wearing a pale pink dress.](assets/2ce0c9ede00e-great-expectations-estella-2.png)<br><br>*Estella* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“tenderness” and “never had any such thing” | **What the quotation means: **When they are reunited, Estella tells Pip that his feelings for her are *futile* as she has never given her kindness to anyone, and does not even know what it is** ** | **Theme: **Ambition and self-improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens uses Estella to present the danger of Miss Havisham’s need for vengeance:<br><br>  - Estella stresses to Pip that she has always** **repressed any compassion, emphasising this with “never”<br>  - She stresses how unfamiliar the feeling is to her with “any such thing”<br>  - She is presented as a victim despite her cold and callous [characterisation](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-literature/characterisation-definition/)<br>- Dickens portrays the damage of Miss Havisham’s *malice* on Estella:<br><br>  - Estella’s emotional development is slower and shaped by greater trauma, but she eventually gains insight into her suffering |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“Everything else has gone from me, little by little, but I have kept this. It was the subject of the only determined resistance I made in all the wretched years” **- Estella, Chapter 59

| ![Illustration of a woman in profile with brown hair in an intricate bun, wearing a pale pink dress, set within a circular frame on a white background.](assets/2ce0c9ede00e-great-expectations-estella-2.png)<br><br>*Estella* | **Key word or phrase to memorise:  **“my only determined resistance” | **What the quotation means: **Estella tells Pip that she owns “Satis House” and that it is all she has left, suggesting that not only is it her only possession, but also the only thing that proves she has fought for something in all her pitiful years | **Theme: **Guilt and shame |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens illustrates Estella’s guilt about her lack of defiance against Miss Havisham:<br><br>  - Estella links “wretched” with “only determined resistance” to imply that she has been too passive and weak<br>- Dickens presents Estella as humbled, and thus, developed as a character:<br><br>  - Her development is implied by her <u>[ironic](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/irony-definition/)</u> words that the house is the only thing that brings her satisfaction<br>  - This contradicts Estella’s earlier detachment and pride in emotional distance, revealing regret and vulnerability<br>  - The cyclical nature of this [alludes](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/allusion-definition/) to Estella’s continued isolation |   |   |   |

## Great Expectations: Character quotations: Miss Havisham

## Miss Havisham

> **blockquote**
> **“I have a sick fancy that I want to see some play” **- Miss Havisham, Chapter 8

| ![Illustration of Miss Havisham as a woman with a serious expression is wearing a bridal gown, veil, and pearl necklace.](assets/5f98aa3084db-great-expectations-miss-havisham.png)<br><br>*Miss Havisham* | **Key word or phrase to memorise:  **“sick fancy” | **What the quotation means: **Miss Havisham gives a strange command for Pip to play in front of her and explains that it is because she has a kind of illness that makes her a little delusional or erratic | **Theme: **Integrity and reputation |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens introduces Miss Havisham as *fanciful* and unstable:<br><br>  - She says that she is “sick” and bored<br>  - Though she is cruel, she is direct and straightforward<br>- Dickens’ ambiguous and mysterious Miss Havisham is a villain and a victim:<br><br>  - Her reputed wealth isolates her<br>  - She tells Pip that she is “done” with adults to explain her desire for play<br>  - She uses Pip in her revenge plot against men, though she later regrets the emotional harm caused |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“Educating for a lady; far out of reach; prettier than ever; admired by all who see her. Do you feel that you have lost her?” **- Miss Havisham, Chapter 15

| ![A woman with a serious expression is wearing a bridal gown, veil, and pearl necklace within a circular frame. The illustration has a simple, pastel color palette.](assets/5f98aa3084db-great-expectations-miss-havisham.png)<br><br>*Miss Havisham* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“a lady” and “far out of reach” | **What the quotation means: **Miss Havisham expresses her pleasure that her plot to break Pip’s heart by making him fall in love with Estella is working, and asks Pip if he feels he has “lost” Estella now that she is a “lady” and, thus, out of his grasp | **Theme: ***Social class* |
|---|---|---|---|
| - The pressure Pip feels to improve his status and wealth is a key idea in the novel:<br><br>  - Here, Dickens illustrates how Miss Havisham uses social inequalities to carry out her plot to hurt Pip<br>  - Although her desire for revenge is directed at men, Miss Havisham also uses *social class* hierarchies to manipulate Pip and Estella<br>  - Miss Havisham plays on the idea of Estella being “far out of reach” as she is “abroad” “educating for a lady” |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“‘You stock and stone!’ exclaimed Miss Havisham. ‘You cold, cold heart!’”**- Miss Havisham, Chapter 38

| ![Illustration of Miss Havisham as a woman with a serious expression is wearing a bridal gown, veil, and pearl necklace.](assets/5f98aa3084db-great-expectations-miss-havisham.png)<br><br>*Miss Havisham* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“cold, cold heart” | **What the quotation means: **Miss Havisham is shocked to be the subject of Estella’s *insolent*** ***disdain* and exclaims that Estella is made of “stone” and is cruel and cold-hearted | **Theme: **Ambition and self-development |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens exposes Miss Havisham’s hypocrisies when Estella turns on her:<br><br>  - Miss Havisham’s shock at Estella’s “cold, cold heart” is [ironic](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/irony-definition/)<br>  - Dickens depicts how making Estella cold-hearted works against her<br>- Great Expectations explores ideas about the ability to reflect and change:<br><br>  - Miss Havisham is unable to show remorse when Estella confronts her<br>  - She refuses to accept that she raised Estella to be “hard” and “proud” |   |   |   |

## Abel Magwitch

> **blockquote**
> **“I wish I was a frog. Or a eel!” **- Abel Magwitch, Chapter 1

| ![Illustrated head-and-shoulders portrait of Magwitch from Great Expectations, a bald man in a grey coat, shown in profile.](assets/fccb532639a8-great-expectations-magwitch.png)<br><br>*Abel Magwitch* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“frog” and “eel” | **What the quotation means: **After Magwitch has threatened Pip in the churchyard, Pip wishes him a good night. Magwitch replies sarcastically that he would rather be a “frog” or an “eel” as the night ahead will be so wet and cold he would be better off being a small water creature | **Theme: ***Social class* |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Dickens’ [characterisation](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-literature/characterisation-definition/) of Magwitch examines ideas about social injustice:<br><br>  - Magwitch is both a villain and victim<br>  - He is introduced as a violent criminal, as well as a desperate man<br>- Dickens creates sympathy for Magwitch, despite his mistreatment of Pip:<br><br>  - Though threatening, Magwitch’s surreal humour and exaggeration [foreshadow](https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/english-language/foreshadowing-definition/) his deeper humanity<br>  - Dickens suggests that his lower class status makes him vulnerable<br>  - His vain “wish” to be a small animal conveys his limited autonomy |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“Noble, Pip! And I have never forgot it!”** - Abel Magwitch, Chapter 39

| ![Illustration of Magwitch as a prisoner in profile.](assets/fccb532639a8-great-expectations-magwitch.png)<br><br>*Abel Magwitch* | **Key word or phrase to memorise: **“Noble” and “never forgot it” | **What the quotation means: **Magwitch seeks out Pip to tell him that he is his benefactor, and begins by explaining that Pip was “noble” and principled when he helped him in the marshes and he has never forgotten his kindness | **Theme: **Integrity and reputation |
|---|---|---|---|
| - The novel examines nobility assumed by wealth compared with noble behaviour:<br><br>  - Magwitch’s reputation as a criminal makes him the subject of prejudice<br>  - However, Dickens implies he has integrity as he repays an old debt<br>  - Dickens illustrates the impact of the kindness shown in “never forgot it”<br>- His praise of Pip is perhaps *ironic* as Pip is less than “noble” at this point in the novel:<br><br>  - Dickens portrays Pip as snobbish and dismissive of Magwitch here<br>  - Perhaps this draws attention to the nobility of Magwitch’s actions |   |   |   |

> **blockquote**
> **“It's best as a gentleman should not be knowed to belong to me now” **- Abel Magwitch, Chapter 54

| ![Illustration of Magwitch as a prisoner in profile.](assets/fccb532639a8-great-expectations-magwitch.png)<br><br>*Abel Magwitch* | **Key word or phrase to memorise:  **“not be knowed to belong to me” | **What the quotation means: **When Magwitch is arrested and about to depart in a boat, he tells Pip that he is happy that he has seen Pip as a gentleman and that it is better that he leaves as his companionship would damage his reputation | **Theme: **Ambition and self-improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| - Ironically, Magwitch himself does not develop much as a character:<br><br>  - His perspective on wealth and status stays the same and his ambition, to see Pip as a gentleman, satisfies him<br>  - He cannot see that Pip’s status has not brought him happiness or character<br>  - Perhaps Dickens implies that his victimisation makes him accept the status quo |   |   |   |
