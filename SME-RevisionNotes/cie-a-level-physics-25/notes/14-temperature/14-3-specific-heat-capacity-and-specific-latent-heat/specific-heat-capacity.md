# Specific Heat Capacity

## Defining Specific Heat Capacity

> **Spec point** — `spcpt_ykhdS62Rv2NM4jFj`

## Defining specific heat capacity

- The specific heat capacity of a substance is defined as:

**The amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C**

- This quantity determines the amount of energy needed to change the temperature of a substance
- The specific heat capacity is measured in units of **joules per kilogram** **per *****kelvin***** **(J kg<sup>-1 </sup>K<sup>-1</sup>) or **joules per kilogram per Celsius** (J kg<sup>-1 </sup>°C<sup>-1</sup>) and has the symbol ***c***

  - Different substances have different specific heat capacities
  - Specific heat capacity is mainly used in liquids and solids

***Low v high specific heat capacity***

- From the definition of specific heat capacity, it follows that:

  - The heavier the material, the more thermal energy that will be required to raise its temperature
  - The larger the change in temperature, the higher the thermal energy will be required to achieve this change

### Calculating specific heat capacity

- The amount of thermal energy *Q* needed to raise the temperature by *∆θ* for a mass *m* with specific heat capacity *c* is equal to:

**∆*****Q***** = *****mc*****∆*****θ***

- Where:

  - ∆*Q* = change in thermal energy (J)
  - *m* = mass of the substance you are heating up (kg)
  - *c* = specific heat capacity of the substance (J kg<sup>-1 </sup>K<sup>-1 </sup>or J kg<sup>-1 </sup>°C<sup>-1</sup>)
  - ∆*θ* = change in temperature (K or °C)

- If a substance has a **low** specific heat capacity, it heats up and cools down quickly (for equal masses heated at the same rate)
- If a substance has a **high** specific heat capacity, it heats up and cools down slowly (for equal masses heated at the same rate)
- The specific heat capacity of different substances determines how useful they would be for a specific purpose eg. choosing the best material for kitchen appliances

- Metals conduct thermal energy well because of their free electrons; this is a separate property from specific heat capacity

> **Worked Example**
> A kettle is rated at 1.7 kW. A mass of 650 g of a liquid at 25°C is poured into a kettle. When the kettle is switched on, it takes 3.5 minutes to start boiling.
> 
> Calculate the specific heat capacity of the liquid.
> 
> **Step 1: Calculate the energy from the power and time**
> 
> Energy = Power × Time
> 
> Power = 1.7 kW = 1.7 × 10<sup>3</sup> W
> 
> Time = 3.5 minutes = 3.5 × 60 = 210 s
> 
> Energy = 1.7 × 10<sup>3</sup> × 210 = 3.57 × 10<sup>5</sup> J
> 
> **Step 2:** **Thermal energy equation**
> 
> ∆Q = mc∆θ
> 
> **Step 3:** **Rearrange for specific heat capacity**
> 
> $c=\frac{\DeltaQ}{m\Deltaθ}$
> 
> **Step 4: Substitute in values**
> 
> m = 650 g = 650 × 10<sup>-3</sup> kg
> 
> ∆*θ* = 100 - 25 = 75 °C
> 
> $c=\frac{3.57\times10^{5}}{650\times10^{-3}\times75}$ = 7323.07... = 7300 J kg<sup>-1 </sup>°C<sup>-1</sup> (2 s.f)

> **Exam Hint**
> The difference in temperature ∆θ will be exactly the same whether the temperature is given in Celsius or kelvin. Therefore, there is no need to convert between the two since the **difference** in temperature will be the same for both units.
