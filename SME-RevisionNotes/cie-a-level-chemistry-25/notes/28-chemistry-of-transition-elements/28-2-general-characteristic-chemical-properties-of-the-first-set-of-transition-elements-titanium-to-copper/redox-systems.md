# Redox Systems

## The Permanganate & Oxalate Redox System

- The oxidation states of transition element ions can change during **redox reactions**

  - A species will either be oxidised or reduced, depending on what reaction is occurring
- To find the concentration of specific ions in solution, a titration can be performed
- There are three particular redox titrations that need to be learnt:

  - Iron (II) (Fe<sup>2+</sup>) and permanganate (MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>) in acid solution given suitable data
  - Permanganate (MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>) and ethanedioate (C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>) in acid solution given suitable data
  - Copper (II) (Cu<sup>2+</sup>) and iodide (I<sup>-</sup>) given suitable data

- The first redox titration involving transition element ions, that needs to be learned, is the titration of permanganate (MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>) and ethanedioate, sometimes known as oxalate (C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>) in acid solution given suitable data

### Reaction of MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup> & C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> in acid

- The reaction of MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> with ethanedioate, C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup> is an example of a redox reaction in which the ethanedioate ions (C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup>) get **oxidised **by manganate(VII) (MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup>) ions
- A titration reaction can be carried out to find the concentration of the **toxic **ethanedioate ions
- As before, the endpoint is when all of the ethanedioate ions have reacted with the MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> ions, and the first permanent pink colour appears in the flask

  - At this point, the MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> is very slightly in **excess**
- The two half-reactions that are involved in this redox reaction are as follows:

**C**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**2–**</sup>** (aq) → 2CO**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** **

- The C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup> (aq) loses 2 electrons to form 2CO<sub>2</sub> (g)

  - The oxidation number of carbon changes from +3 in C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup> (aq) to +4 in CO<sub>2</sub> (g)
  - Since there is an increase in oxidation number, this is the oxidation reaction

**MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- The oxidation number of manganese changes from +7 in MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> (aq) to +2 in** **Mn<sup>2+</sup> (aq)

  - Since there is a decrease in oxidation number, this is the reduction reaction

- The half equations are combined to get the overall equation:

Oxidation:   **C**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**2–**</sup>** (aq) → 2CO**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- Both half equations must have the same number of electrons, so:

  - The oxidation half equation is multiplied by 5
  - The reduction half equation is multiplied by 2

Oxidation:   **5C**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**2–**</sup>** (aq) → 10CO**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g) + 10e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **2MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 16H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 10e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → 2Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- The reactants and products from each half equation can be combined together:

**5C**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**2–**</sup>** (aq) + 2MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 16H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 10e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → 2Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) + 10CO**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g) + 10e**<sup>**–**</sup>** **

- Any species that appear on both sides of the overall equation can be cancelled out

  - In this case, there are 10e<sup>–</sup> on both sides, which can be cancelled:

**5C**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**2–**</sup>** (aq) + 2MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 16H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) → 2Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) + 10CO**<sub>**2**</sub>** (g)  **

- This is an example of an **autocatalysis reaction**
- This means that the reaction is catalysed by one of the products as it forms
- In this reaction, the Mn<sup>2+</sup> ions formed act as the **autocatalyst**
- The more Mn<sup>2+</sup> formed, the faster the reaction gets, which then forms even more Mn<sup>2+</sup> ions and speeds the reaction up even further
- Transition element ions can act as autocatalysts because they can change their oxidation states during a reaction

## The Ferrous & Permanganate Redox System

- The second redox titration involving transition element ions, that needs to be learned, is the titration of permanganate (MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>) and iron(II) ions  (Fe<sup>2+</sup>)

### Reaction of MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> & Fe<sup>2+</sup> in acid

- The concentration of Fe<sup>2+</sup> ions can be determined by titrating a known **volume** of Fe(II) ions with a known **concentration** of MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> ions
- During the reaction of MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> with Fe<sup>2+</sup>, the **purple **colour of the manganate(VII) ions disappears
- The end-point is when all of the Fe<sup>2+</sup> ions have reacted with the MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> ions, and the first trace of a permanent **pink** colour appears in the flask

  - At this point, the MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> is very slightly in **excess**
- The two half-reactions that are involved in this redox reaction are as follows:

**Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) → Fe**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) + e**<sup>**–**</sup>

- The Fe<sup>2+</sup> (aq) loses an electron to form Fe<sup>3+</sup> (aq)

  - The oxidation number of iron changes from +2 in Fe<sup>2+</sup> (aq) to +3 in Fe<sup>3+</sup> (aq)
  - So, this is the oxidation reaction

**MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- The oxidation number of manganese changes from +7 in MnO<sub>4</sub><sup>–</sup> (aq) to +2 in** **Mn<sup>2+</sup> (aq)

  - Since there is a decrease in oxidation number, this is the reduction reaction

- The half equations are combined to get the overall equation:

Oxidation:   **Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) → Fe**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) + e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- Both half equations must have the same number of electrons, so:

  - The oxidation half equation is multiplied by 5
  - The reduction half equation does not need any changes

Oxidation:   **5Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) → 5Fe**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) **

- The reactants and products from each half equation can be combined together:

**5Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) + 5Fe**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) + 5e**<sup>**–**</sup>

- Any species that appear on both sides of the overall equation can be cancelled out

  - In this case, there are 5e<sup>–</sup> on both sides, which can be cancelled:

**5Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + MnO**<sub>**4**</sub><sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 8H**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) → Mn**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4H**<sub>**2**</sub>**O (l) + 5Fe**<sup>**3+**</sup>** (aq) **

## The Cupric & Iodide Redox Systems

- The third redox titration involving transition metal ions, that needs to be learnt, is the titration between copper(II) ions (Cu<sup>2+</sup>) - sometimes known as cupric ions - and iodide ions (I<sup>-</sup>)

### Reaction of Cu<sup>2+</sup> & I<sup>-</sup>

- The reaction of Cu<sup>2+</sup> with I<sup>-</sup> is an example of a redox reaction in which the copper ions (Cu<sup>2+</sup>) **oxidise** the iodide ions (I<sup>-</sup>) and as a result are themselves **reduced**
- The two half-reactions that are involved in this redox reaction are as follows:

**2I**<sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) → I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>

- The 2I<sup>–</sup> (aq) loses an electron each to form I<sub>2</sub> (aq)

  - The oxidation number of iodine changes from -1 in I<sup>–</sup> (aq) to 0 in I<sub>2</sub> (aq)
  - So, this is the oxidation reaction

**Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Cu**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) **

- The Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) gains an electron to form Cu<sup>+</sup> (aq)

  - The oxidation number of copper changes from +2 in Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) to +1 in** **Cu<sup>+</sup> (aq)
  - Since there is a decrease in oxidation number, this is the reduction reaction

- The half equations are combined to get the overall equation:

Oxidation:   **2I**<sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) → I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → Cu**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) **

- Both half equations must have the same number of electrons, so:

  - The oxidation half equation does not need any changes
  - The reduction half equation is multiplied by 2

Oxidation:   **2I**<sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) → I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>

Reduction:   **2Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → 2Cu**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) **

- The reactants and products from each half equation can be combined together:

**2I**<sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 2Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>** → 2Cu**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2e**<sup>**–**</sup>

- Any species that appear on both sides of the overall equation can be cancelled out

  - In this case, there are 2e<sup>–</sup> on both sides, which can be cancelled:

**2I**<sup>**–**</sup>** (aq) + 2Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) → 2Cu**<sup>**+**</sup>** (aq) + I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) **

- When **excess **iodide ions are reacted with Cu(II), a precipitate of copper(I) iodide and **iodine** is formed:

**Reaction 1**

**2Cu**<sup>**2+**</sup>** (aq) + 4I**<sup>**-**</sup>** (aq) →  I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2CuI (s)**

- A titration reaction can be carried out to find an unknown concentration of the copper(II) solution
- This is done by finding the amount of iodine which is liberated during the reaction, through a titration:
- A known concentration of **sodium thiosulfate solution** is added to the mixture formed in Reaction 1 from a burette

  - The I<sub>2</sub> formed in Reaction 1 will react with the thiosulfate ions

**Reaction 2**

**I**<sub>**2**</sub>** (aq) + 2S**<sub>**2**</sub>**O**<sub>**3**</sub><sup>**2- **</sup>**(aq) →  2I**<sup>**-**</sup>** (aq) + S**<sub>**4**</sub>**O**<sub>**6**</sub><sup>**2-**</sup>** (aq)**

- As the iodine is used up, the **brownish** colour of the solution gets **lighter**
- When most of the iodine colour is gone, starch is added, which turns deep **blue/black** with the remaining I<sub>2</sub> (aq)
- Titrate further until the blue/black colour disappears, i.e. when all of the iodine has reacted

  - By knowing the number of moles of thiosulfate ions added in the titration, you can use the molar ratios from the reaction equations and work backwards to calculate the number of moles of Cu(II)
- Look at Reaction 2 It can be concluded that half the number of moles of I<sub>2</sub> reacts when compared to the moles of thiosulfate that react
- Now look at Reaction 1

  - The number of moles of I<sub>2</sub> which react in Reaction 2, is the moles formed in Reaction 1
  - The number of moles of Cu(II) is twice that of I<sub>2</sub> (aq), i.e. the same number of moles as thiosulfate ions added in the titration
- Divide the number of moles of Cu(II) by the volume in dm<sup>3</sup> to get the concentration of Cu(II)
