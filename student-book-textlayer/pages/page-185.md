BASES
We saw in Chapter 16 that bases are substances that neutralise acids by
combining with the hydrogen ions in them to produce water. When we are
referring to a base at this level, we usually mean a metal oxide, a metal
hydroxide or ammonia.

REACTING ACIDS WITH BASES

REACTING DILUTE SULFURIC ACID
WITH COPPER(II) OXIDE

Metal oxides, such as copper(II) oxide and magnesium oxide, are bases.

The copper(II) oxide (black powder) reacts with hot dilute sulfuric acid to
produce a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate.
CuO(s) + H, so. (aq) ..... CuSO, (aq) + H20(I)
All the metal oxide and acid combinations that you will meet at International
GCSE behave in exactly the same way as the reaction between copper(II)
oxide and dilute sulfuric acid, that is, they produce a salt and water. Most need
to be heated for the reaction to start.
Copper(II) oxide is an ionic compound containing the 0 2- ion. What has
happened in this reaction is that the H+ ions from the acid have combined with
the 0 2- ions to form water (H20).
The general equation for the reaction of a metal oxide (base) with an acid is
metal oxide + acid -+ salt + water
This is a neutralisation reaction - the base neutralises the acid (water is formed).

BASES AND ALKALIS
Some metal oxides are soluble in water and react with it to form solutions of
metal hydroxides, for example:
Na20(s) + H20(1) --+ 2NaOH(aq)
All the Group 1 oxides do this reaction, so for potassium oxide we would get:
K 20 (s) + H20(1) -+ 2KOH(aq)
Most other metal oxides are not soluble in water.
Calcium oxide dissolves slightly to form calcium hydroxide:
CaO(s) + H20(1) -+ Ca(OH),(aq)
Another alkali you will meet is a solution of ammonia (NH:,). The ammonia
reacts with the water to form ammonium ions and hydroxide ions:
NH3(aq) + H 2 0(1) s= NH4 •(aq) + OH-(aq)
All these solutions contain hydroxide ions. We learnt in the last chapter that we
can define alkalis as solutions that are a source of hydroxide (OH-) ions, so all
of these solutions are alkalis.
