THE ADDITION OF BROMINE
Bromine adds to alkenes without any need for heat, light or a catalyst. The
reaction is often carried out using bromine water (aqueous bromine solution)
as, for example, in Figure 25.2, with ethene.

THE TEST FOR UNSATURATED
COMPOUNDS
HINT

You must be careful with the words you
use here: the final mixture is colourless
and not clear. Do not use the word
clear. Clear means not cloudy, you can
see through it. The bromine water is
clear and any other solution can also

be described as clear.

Any compound with a carbon-carbon double bond will react with bromine in a
similar way to ethene. This is used to test for a carbon--airbon double bond. If
you shake an unknown organic compound with bromine water and the orange
bromine water is decolorised (the colour changes from orange to colourless), the
compound contains a carbon-carbon double bond. If your unknown compound
is a gas, you can simply bubble it through bromine water with the same effect.
The left-hand tube in Figure 25.3 shows the effect of shaking a liquid alkene
with bromine water. The organic layer (containing the alkene) is on top. You
can see that the bromine water has been completely decolorised, showing the
presence of the carbon-carbon double bond.
The right-hand tube in Figure 25.3 shows what happens if you use a liquid
alkane, which doesn't have a carbon-carbon double bond. The colour of the
bromine is still there. Alkanes do not decolorise bromine water because they
do not contain a carbon-carbon double bond.
The addition reaction between bromine and propene is very similar to that
of ethene. Part of the carbon-carbon double bond breaks and two bromine
atoms add across the bond, as shown in Figure 25.4.
