## Hull’s Drive Reduction Theory of Motivation

### Biological drives

* Biological drives are states of readiness that motivate an action, created by a biological need.

* A biological need occurs when the body lacks something necessary for normal functioning. This creates an uncomfortable internal state, which produces a drive that motivates behaviour to restore balance.

**Example:**


→ The body lacks water → **thirst** develops → thirst creates a **drive to drink** → drinking reduces the drive.

The basic idea is that motivation can come from the body’s need to return to a balanced internal state.

### Sympathetic nervous system (SNS)

* The sympathetic nervous system is a network of nerves that activates the body’s “fight or flight” response when the brain reacts to stress, danger or physical activity.

* Activation increases physiological arousal, preparing the body for action.

**Examples of effects include:**

* Increased heart rate

* Increased breathing rate

* Greater alertness

* Increased blood flow to muscles

**Example:** 

* If someone suddenly sees danger, sympathetic activation prepares them to respond quickly.

### Parasympathetic nervous system (PNS)

* The parasympathetic nervous system is a network of nerves that activates the body’s “rest and digest” response when the body is resting, particularly after eating.

* It helps the body return towards a calmer state after physiological activation.

**Example:** 

* After eating and resting, parasympathetic activity helps the body settle and supports digestion.

### Biological instincts and motivation

* Biological instincts can contribute to motivation by helping organisms respond to important features of their environment.

**Stimulus/environmental cues**

* Environmental cues can release or trigger an instinctive response.

* For example, a particular environmental stimulus may signal that a biological response is needed, triggering behaviour without requiring conscious decision-making.

**Fixed-action patterns (FAPs)**

* Fixed-action patterns are innate, predictable sequences of behaviour that are triggered by particular stimuli.

* They are important because some instinctive behaviours help organisms survive.

**Examples can include:**

* Behaviours involved in obtaining food

* Responding to threats

* Behaviours involved in protecting offspring

The key idea is that some motivated behaviours are influenced by innate biological mechanisms, rather than being entirely learned from experience.

### Hull’s drive reduction theory

* Clark Hull proposed that motivation is largely driven by the need to maintain homeostasis.

* Homeostasis is the organism’s internal regulation of physiological conditions to maintain a state of balance.

* When the body moves away from its optimal internal state, a biological need develops. This produces a drive, which motivates behaviour designed to reduce the drive and restore balance.

**Basic sequence:**

Physiological imbalance → biological need → drive → motivated behaviour → drive reduction → homeostasis

**Example:**

Low water levels → need for water → thirst → drinking → thirst decreases → internal balance restored

### Primary drives

**Primary drives** are motivations based on basic biological needs that are necessary for survival.

**Examples include:**

* Hunger

* Thirst

* The need for sleep

* Avoiding excessive physical discomfort

Primary drives are not dependent on learning because they arise from biological requirements.

**Example:** 

* Hunger motivates eating because the body needs energy and nutrients.

### Secondary drives

**Secondary drives** are motivations that are learned through experience and become associated with primary needs or desirable outcomes.

* Unlike primary drives, they are not directly necessary for biological survival.

**Example:** 

* Money can become a secondary drive because it can be used to obtain food, shelter and other things that satisfy primary needs.

* This means that something originally unrelated to biological survival can become motivating through learning.

### Physiological arousal and motivation

* Hull's theory links motivation to changes in physiological arousal.

* When a biological need creates a drive, the body may become physiologically activated, motivating the organism to take action. Once the appropriate behaviour occurs and the need is reduced, arousal can decrease and the body moves back towards homeostasis.

* However, motivation is not simply about producing as much arousal as possible. The aim is to maintain an optimal level of arousal.

### Optimal arousal

* Optimal arousal refers to a level of physiological activation that is appropriate for effective functioning.

* Too little arousal can mean the person is insufficiently activated or alert, whereas excessive arousal can interfere with effective behaviour.

* Therefore, motivation can involve moving the body towards an appropriate level of arousal, rather than simply increasing arousal.

**Example:**

* A student who is extremely tired may have too little arousal to concentrate effectively. Becoming more alert may improve performance.

* However, if the student becomes extremely anxious and physiologically over-aroused before an exam, performance may also suffer.

**Application tip:** In a novel scenario, identify what biological imbalance or drive is present, explain the motivated behaviour, and then link the behaviour to drive reduction and restoration of homeostasis.
