## Non-experimental Method: Observations

### What is an observation?

* An **observation** is a method of collecting data by watching participants and recording their behaviour.

* Unlike an experiment, the researcher does not manipulate an IV to investigate cause and effect.

* The researcher observes behaviour as it occurs.

**Example:**

* A researcher watches students during a lesson and records how often they raise their hands.

**Organising the observational setting**

When conducting an observation, the researcher must carefully organise the **observational setting** so that relevant behaviour can be observed and recorded consistently.

This can involve deciding:

* Where the observation will take place

* When the observation will take place

* How long the observation will last

* Where the observer will be positioned

* What participants will be doing during the observation

* How behaviours will be recorded

The setting should be planned so that the researcher can collect data relevant to the research aim while keeping the procedure consistent.

**Example:**

* If a researcher wants to investigate classroom participation, they might decide to observe the same type of classroom for the same length of time and record behaviour from a consistent position.

**Choosing behavioural categories**

* In a structured observation, the researcher must choose behavioural categories before collecting data.

* Behavioural categories are specific activities or behaviours that the researcher records.

The categories should be:

* Clearly defined

* Observable

* Measurable

* Relevant to the research aim

* Mutually exclusive where possible, so the observer can decide which category a behaviour belongs to

Behavioural categories must be **operationalised** so that different observers can identify the same behaviour consistently.

**Example:**

* Instead of recording “aggressive behaviour”, the researcher could operationalise it as:

  * “Any instance of hitting, kicking or pushing another person.”

  * This makes the behaviour easier to observe and record consistently.

### Structured observation

* A **structured observation** involves observing a **fixed set of behaviours** that the researcher selects in advance.

* The researcher decides beforehand which behaviours will be recorded.

* Structured observations use **behavioural categories**.

* **Behavioural categories** are specific activities or behaviours that are recorded during a structured observation.

* The categories should be **operationalised** so that the researcher knows exactly what behaviour counts.

Structured observations commonly use:

* Tally charts

* Checklists

**Example:**

A researcher wants to investigate classroom participation.

Before observing, they create behavioural categories:

* Raises hand

* Asks a question

* Answers a question

* Talks to another student

The researcher then records each behaviour using a tally chart.

### Operationalising behavioural categories

* Behavioural categories need to be operationalised so that they are **specific, observable and measurable**.

* This prevents different observers from interpreting the same behaviour differently.

**Example:**

Vague category:

“Distracted”

Operationalised category:

“Looks away from the teacher or learning material for **5 seconds or more**.”

The operationalised definition tells the researcher exactly when to record the behaviour.

### Tally charts and checklists

* A tally chart records the frequency of predefined behaviours.

* The observer adds a tally each time the behaviour occurs and counts the tallies at the end.

* A checklist records whether predefined behaviours occur.

### Unstructured observation

* An unstructured observation involves observing a complete range of possible behaviours rather than selecting specific behaviours in advance.

* The researcher does not use a fixed set of behavioural categories.

* This allows the researcher to record a wider range of behaviour.

**Example:**

* Instead of deciding beforehand that only hand-raising and talking will be recorded, the researcher observes the classroom and records the different behaviours that occur.

### Covert observation

A covert observation occurs when the participants are unaware that they are being observed.

The observer's role is not known or obvious because the observers may be hidden or disguised.

**Example:**

* A researcher observes how people behave in a public setting without making their role as a researcher obvious.

### Overt observation

An overt observation occurs when participants know that they are being observed.

The observer's role is obvious to the participants.

**Example:**

* A researcher tells a class that they are observing classroom behaviour and then records students' behaviour during the lesson.

**Application tip:**
For an observation scenario, first identify **what behaviour is being recorded**.

Then ask:

* Are behaviours selected in advance? → **Structured**

* Is the researcher recording the full range of behaviour? → **Unstructured**

* Do participants know they are being observed? → **Overt**

* Are participants unaware? → **Covert**

* Are specific behaviours clearly defined? → **Operationalised behavioural categories**
