## Validity and Reliability

### Validity

**Validity** is the extent to which the researcher is investigating what they aimed to investigate.

A study has high validity when the method actually measures or investigates what the researcher intended.

**Example:**

* A researcher wants to investigate memory but gives participants a test that mainly measures reading ability.

* This would have low validity because the researcher is not accurately investigating memory.

### Subjectivity

Subjectivity means that research is impacted by personal opinions and feelings.

This can introduce bias into the research.

**Example:**

* An observer believes that one participant is more confident and therefore records their behaviour more positively.

* The observer's personal opinion has influenced the data, making the observation more subjective.

### Objectivity

Objectivity means that research is not impacted by personal opinions and feelings.

Objective research aims to be unbiased.

**Example:**

* An observer uses clearly defined behavioural categories and records behaviour according to the same rules for every participant.

* This makes the observation more objective.

### Improving validity

Validity can be improved by making sure the research method actually measures what the researcher intends to investigate.

This can involve:

* Clearly operationalising variables

* Using appropriate measures

* Controlling extraneous variables

* Using appropriate research methods

* Standardising the procedure

**Example:**

* If a researcher wants to measure memory, they should use a task that actually measures memory rather than another ability.

### Application to novel scenarios

Ask:

**“Is the researcher actually investigating what they intended to investigate?”**

If yes → higher validity

If another factor or an inappropriate measure means they are not investigating the intended variable → lower validity

### Reliability

**Reliability** is the consistency of the procedure or measures used to collect data.

A reliable procedure should produce similar results when the same method is used consistently.

**Example:**

* If two observers watch the same behaviour and record very different results, the observation has low reliability.

* If they consistently record the same behaviours, the observation has higher reliability.

### Inter-rater reliability

Inter-rater reliability is the consistency and agreement between raters judging the same responses.

A rater is someone who judges or scores participants' responses.

**Example:**

* Two researchers independently score participants' responses to an essay question.

* If they give similar scores, there is **high inter-rater reliability**.

### Inter-observer reliability

**Inter-observer reliability** is the consistency and agreement between observers observing the same behaviours.

**Example:**

* Two observers watch the same classroom and record how many times a student raises their hand.

* If both observers record similar numbers, there is **high inter-observer reliability**.

### Improving reliability

Reliability can be improved by:

* Standardising the procedure

* Giving all participants the same instructions

* Using clear operationalised measures

* Training observers or raters

* Using clearly defined behavioural categories

**Example:**

* Two observers are more likely to agree if “aggressive behaviour” is clearly operationalised so both know exactly what behaviour to record.

### Application to novel scenarios

Ask:

**“Would the procedure or measurement produce consistent results?”**

If different researchers or observers produce similar results → high reliability

If results vary substantially between researchers or observations → low reliability
