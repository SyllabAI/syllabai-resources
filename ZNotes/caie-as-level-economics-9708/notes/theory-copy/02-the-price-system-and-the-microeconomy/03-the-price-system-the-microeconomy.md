## **Price Elasticity, Income Elasticity & Cross Elasticity of Demand**

### Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

* Definition → Measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the price of a product.

* Formula →

PED=%ΔQd%ΔPPED=\\frac\{\\%\\Delta Q\_d\}\{\\%\\Delta P\}PED=%ΔP%ΔQd​​

* PED \> 1 – Price Elastic: Quantity demanded changes by a greater percentage than the change in price.

* PED \< 1 – Price Inelastic: Quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than the change in price.

* PED = 1 – Unitary Elastic: Quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as the price.

* PED = 0 – Perfectly Inelastic: Quantity demanded remains unchanged despite changes in price.

* PED = ∞ – Perfectly Elastic: An extremely small change in price can cause demand to fall to zero.

### Example: Price of Bread Increases

* Price Increase → 15%

* Change in Quantity Demanded → −20%

* Calculation →

PED=−20%15%=−1.33PED=\\frac\{-20\\%\}\{15\\%\}=-1.33PED=15%−20%​=−1.33

* Interpretation → The negative sign indicates the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

* Magnitude → 1.33, which is greater than 1.

* Conclusion → Demand is relatively price elastic.

Important: When classifying PED, ignore the negative sign and consider its absolute value.

## 2. Factors Influencing Price Elasticity of Demand

### 1) Necessity

* Necessities → Tend to have relatively inelastic demand because consumers continue purchasing them even when prices rise.

* Luxury Goods → Tend to have relatively elastic demand because they are not essential and can be avoided when prices increase.

### 2) Availability of Substitutes

* Many Substitutes → Demand is relatively elastic because consumers can switch to alternative products when the price rises.

* Few Substitutes → Demand is relatively inelastic because consumers have limited alternatives.

* Example → Bread generally has relatively inelastic demand because consumers may have fewer suitable alternatives.

### 3) Addictiveness

* Highly Addictive Goods → Tend to have relatively inelastic demand because consumers may continue purchasing them despite price increases.

* Example → Cigarettes, as addicted consumers may maintain their consumption even when prices rise.

### 4) Proportion of Income Spent

* Small Proportion of Income → Demand tends to be relatively inelastic because a price change has little impact on the consumer's budget.

* Large Proportion of Income → Demand tends to be relatively elastic because a price change has a greater effect on affordability.

### 5) Durability of the Good

* Durable Goods → Demand tends to be relatively elastic because consumers can delay replacing the product when its price rises.

* Less Durable Goods → Demand tends to be relatively inelastic because they need to be replaced or purchased more frequently.

### 6) Peak and Off-Peak Demand

* Peak Periods → Demand is relatively inelastic because consumers may have fewer opportunities to change their travel time.

* Off-Peak Periods → Demand tends to be relatively elastic because consumers can more easily adjust when they travel.

## 3. Point Price Elasticity of Demand

* Definition → Measures the price elasticity of demand at a particular point on the demand curve rather than across a range of prices.

* As Price Rises → Point PED becomes more elastic, as consumers become more responsive to price changes.

* As Price Falls → Point PED becomes more inelastic.

## 4. Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

* Definition → Measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in consumer income.

* Formula →

YED=%ΔQd%ΔYYED=\\frac\{\\%\\Delta Q\_d\}\{\\%\\Delta Y\}YED=%ΔY%ΔQd​​

* YED \< 0 – Inferior Good: Demand falls as income rises because consumers may switch to higher-quality alternatives.

* YED \> 0 – Normal Good: Demand rises as income increases because consumers can afford to purchase more.

* YED \> 1 – Luxury Good: Demand increases by a greater percentage than income.

## 5. Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)

* Definition → Measures how responsive the quantity demanded of one good (X) is to a change in the price of another good (Y).

* Formula →

XED=%ΔQd of X%ΔP of YXED=\\frac\{\\%\\Delta Q\_d\\text\{ of X\}\}\{\\%\\Delta P\\text\{ of Y\}\}XED=%ΔP of Y%ΔQd​ of X​

* XED \< 0 – Complements: When the price of good Y rises, demand for good X falls because the two goods are consumed together.

* XED \> 0 – Substitutes: When the price of good Y rises, demand for good X increases as consumers switch to the alternative.

* XED = 0 – Unrelated Goods: A change in the price of good Y has no effect on the demand for good X.
