## Gunderson et al. (2013): **Parent praise to 1- to 3-year-olds predicts children's motivational frameworks 5 years later**

### ★ **Background:**

* Research has shown the way parent praise children can have an impact on their ideas, reasoning, beliefs, and behaviour

* Though many artificial-setting experiments were able to prove this, Gunderson et al. (2013) aimed to study whether these findings would be consistent in a natural environment.

***Process and person praise***

* Children receive two types of praise affecting their motivational framework (how they understand ideas and facts when deciding)
* Person praise (praising the individual) leads to an entity motivational framework
  * **Entity Framework**: Believing behaviour and ability are fixed and based on a child's nature
* Process praise (praising the child's behaviour) leads to an incremental motivational framework
  * **Incremental framework**: Believing behaviour and ability can be changed with effort

***Previous experimental findings***

* The idea of differing types of praise was drawn from Dweck’s mindset theory

  * Fun fact: Dweck was involved in this study as well!

* Though the abundant amount of experimental evidence for this theory, they utilised artificial environments, which may not explain behaviour in natural settings (lack ecological validity)

* So Gunderson et al. wanted to study if these same findings can be found when studying in a natural environment

***Praise and gender***

* Previous studies showed:

  * boys adopt an incremental framework, the belief ability can be changed with effort

    * suggests boys receive more process praise

  * girls adopt an entity framework, the belief ability is fixed

    * suggests girls receive more person praise

### ★ **Aims:**

To investigate whether:

* there is an impact of differing types of parental praise given to children given in a natural setting
* parents give girls less process praise and more person praise compared to boys
* parents’ usage of process or person praise in early childhood predicts a child’s reasoning 5 years later about motivation and causation of behaviour (ability or effort)

### ★ **Sample:**

* 53 children from Chicago (29 boys, 24 girls)
* Selected from a larger pool of 63 families involved in a language development study
* The sample mirrors the demographics of Chicago, including income, race, and ethnicity

### ★ **Procedure:**

* **Double-blind technique:**
  * Both the researcher and parent weren’t aware that the recordings would be studied for praise
* **Longitudinal Study:**
  * Assessment points at 14 months, 26 months, and 38 months
  * Five years later, motivation was reviewed
  * Questionnaires covered motivation, morality, beliefs, and intelligence
  * Participants and observers were unaware of the study's proper focus (they assumed it was about language development)
* **Parent-Child Interactions:**
  * Parents conducted typical day activities during assessments
  * Caregiver-child interactions were videotaped in 90-minute sessions
* **Follow-Up at 7–8 Years Old:**
  * Children answered questionnaires on intelligence and moral behaviour
  * 18 items covered children's motivational frameworks regarding intelligence and socio-moral views

### ★ **Results:**

**Parental Praise Pattern:**

* Only 3% of parental comments to children constituted praise

**Types of Praise:**

* Process praise represented 18% of all praise
* Person praise constituted 16% of all praise
* Other forms of praise comprised the remaining 66%

**Gender Differences in Process Praise:**

* Boys received more process praise (24.4%) than girls (10.3%)

**Relationship between Parental Praise and Children's Framework:**

* Process praise fostered an incremental framework, where children believed effort could lead to improvement
* No clear link was observed between parents giving person praise and children developing an entity motivational framework
* Early personal praise did not appear to result in fixed frameworks later in a child's life

### ★ **Conclusions:**

1. **A strong correlation was observed**: Parents using process praise align with children adopting an incremental motivational framework, reflecting a belief in changeable abilities
2. **No evidence was found**: Parental use of personal praise does not lead children to develop an entity motivational framework, implying that fixed abilities are not associated with this form of praise
3. **Gender**: Males were more likely to receive process praise in comparison to females

**STRENGTHS**

|

**WEAKNESSES**

||
||

**Natural experiment:** conducted in a natural environment where behaviour of the children and parents are normally seen, so there is high ecological validity, and results can be applied and explain real-world scenarios regarding parental praise

|

**Ethical concerns:** use of deception with parents, so they were unaware of the aims, which raises ethical issues as this means valid informed consent was not given/taken

||
||

**Double blind trial:** both parents and observers were unaware of the actual aims of the study, so could reduce demand characteristics within the parents, and observer/researcher bias within the observers

|

**Observer effect:** parents are aware they are being observed, which may probe them to alter their behaviour according to what they assume the aim is (demand characteristics) or what is seen as “good” (social desirability bias), so results from observations may be invalid

||
||

**Longitudinal design:** studies children from early to late childhood, which means researchers can observe the direct changes from a baseline (no parental praise received) to post-intervention (parental praise received)

|

**Sampling bias:** sample consisted of only 53 child-parent pairs from Chicago, which can be considered a small sample size so it may not cover a wide range of differences, and ethnocentric as it does not study child-parent pairs from other cultures so it cannot be generalised to individuals from differing cultures

||
||

**Collected quantitative data:** collected percentages of process and person praise given by parents to children, which means direct and objective comparisons can be made, free from subjective researcher intepretation

|

**Incomplete findings and conclusions:** findings show gender differences in amount of process praise received between boys and girls, but is not explained why

||
||

**Findings are consistent:** findings of this natural setting study matched findings from artificial researches such as the ones conducted by Dweck, meaning there is reliability (consistency) in outcomes when effort-based praise is given

|

**Link does not indicate causation:** though there is a link that process praise promotes an incremental framework (belief that ability is malleable), it does not mean it is the cause (could just be a coincidence), and cannot be scientifically proven
