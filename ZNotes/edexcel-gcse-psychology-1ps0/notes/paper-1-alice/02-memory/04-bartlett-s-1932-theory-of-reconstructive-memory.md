## Bartlett’s (1932) Theory of Reconstructive Memory

### **The link between memory and schemas**

* Bartlett believed memories are never stored exactly like a computer, but rather a notepad

  * We jot down brief outlines on a notepad, and elaborate on them when we need to

  * Memory works in this same way:

    * we remember a brief outline or a distinct theme of the memory

    * when we recall them, we elaborate using the knowledge of the theme and our existing knowledge

  * Memory is not an exact copy, but rather a (subjective) interpretation known as **active reconstruction**

* Memory is reconstructed/shaped by existing **schemas** (mental frameworks) we gain from life experiences, and develop/change them over time

  * *Learning link:* in Piaget’s Cognitive Theory of Development, he proposes children need to accommodate new experiences by changing schemas, so the same way we may alter our schema due to new experiences over time

### 4 ways schemas influence memory

Schemas impact memory particularly through:

* **Omissions:** Leaving out unfamiliar or unpleasant information
* **Transformation:** Changing details to make them rational or familiar
* **Rationalisation:** Adding details to justify something initially incongruent
* **Familiarisation:** Altering details to align them more closely with our schemas

**Real-world application:** the theory helps us understand why memories can become distorted or inaccurate, and can explain wrongful prosecutions in the justice system; this theory has encouraged the development of *cognitive interviews*, a technique to help eyewitnesses avoid bias and maximise accuracy in their testimonies

|

**Subjective analysis on memory reconstruction:** the theory was based on Bartlett’s own analysis and interpretation of the stories reproduced/recalled by the participants, so critics argue this could mean different people may interpret it differently, questioning the overall validity of the theory, weighing it down as unscientific

||
||

**Ecologically valid methods:** Bartlett’s War of the Ghosts (1932) (KEY STUDY) used methods that can be considered ecologically valid (making participants recall the folklore hours, days, and even years later), which helps supports the theory’s applicability

|

**Lack of standardisation:** the research Bartlett conducted to support his theory lacks proper standardised procedures and controls – he engaged in informal conversations with his participants to explore each of their unique memories and schemas (which was of interest to him); this can weaken the research that was used as the baseline of this theory

||
||

**Research evidence and further application:** Loftus and Palmer (1974) found when asking participants to observe a staged car accident and estimate the speed of the car involved, participants estimated higher speeds when the question used ‘strong’ verbs (“smashed”), compared to using ‘soft’ verbs (“hit”); demonstrates the power of how schemas influence memory (rationalisation during recall), and contributes to the development of *cognitive interviews*

|

**Conflicting evidence:** Yuille & Cutshall (1986) found eyewitnesses of a real-life shooting were not impacted by leading questions that may have triggered their schema to alter the memory, so it suggests reconstructive memory does not always occur as much as the theory emphasised

||
||

**May contribute to positive psychology:** when we know memories can be impacted by schemas, it may help us understand why two or more people who experienced the same event recall it differently; it could be a genuine false memory and does not mean one is necessarily lying, so prevents immediate accusations in some cases

|

**Cannot be measured objectively:** the variable “schema” cannot be quantified or measured in one specific way as schemas are unique to every individual; this means the conclusion “schemas affect memory” cannot be properly falsified, and researchers that may attempt to build on this theory may get inconsistent results
