## Freudian Theory of Dreaming (Freud, 1900)

* Sigmund Freud, a medical doctor in the early 1900s, believed in the significance of dreams for understanding mental health.
* Freud proposed that anxieties, when discussed, could be released, contributing to better mental well-being.

### **Components of the Mind**

* Freud introduced the concept of the unconscious mind, an inaccessible region housing hidden wishes and desires.
* Distinctions:
  * Conscious Mind: Being consciously aware.
  * Pre-conscious/Subconscious: Being made consciously aware through thinking.

### **Iceberg Model**

* Illustrates the structure of the mind:
  * ID (Pleasure Principle): "I want" - driven by instinctual desires.
  * Ego (Reality Principle): Balances the conflicting drives of the ID and the superego.
  * Superego (Moral Principle): Concerned with societal norms and morality.

### **Content of Dreams**

* Freud posited that dreams reveal unconscious thoughts through different aspects:
  * Manifest Content: The surface-level story of a dream.
  * Latent Content: The hidden, symbolic meaning behind the manifest content.

### **Dreamwork**

* Dreamwork involves mechanisms to keep unconscious thoughts hidden during dreaming:
  * Condensation: Merging multiple ideas into a coherent story.
  * Displacement: Elevating the significance of less important elements.
  * Secondary Elaboration: Combining many ideas into one.

### **Symbols**

* Freud emphasised the use of symbols in dream interpretation.
* Symbols have varied meanings for different individuals; psychoanalysts consider personal histories for accurate interpretation.


|Strengths|Weaknesses|
|:---|:---|
|Release of Anxieties: Freud's approach suggests that discussing anxieties can contribute to mental well-being.|Lack of Scientific Basis: Freud's ideas lack empirical evidence, making them difficult to validate scientifically.|
|Comprehensive Model: The three components of the mind (ID, Ego, Superego) provide a comprehensive understanding of mental processes.|Complex Interpretation: Interpretation relies heavily on the psychoanalyst's understanding of a person's history, introducing subjectivity.|
