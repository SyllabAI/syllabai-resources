## How Thought and Language Structures Affect Views of the World

* **Linguistic relativism:** the idea that our language affects our view of the world.
* **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis:** the way our language is structured affects our view of the world.
* **Linguistic determinism:** the idea that our language limits our view of the world.

### **Linguistic Relativism**

* Linguistic relativism posits that language influences how we perceive and interpret the world.
* Known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, was proposed by Roger Brown and Eric Lenneberg in 1954.
* The distinction between 'weak Whorf' (linguistic relativism) and 'strong Whorf' (linguistic determinism).
* **Evidence for Linguistic Relativity:**
  * **Mandarin Language and Time:**
    * Lera Boroditsky (2001) showed that language about time affects its perception.
    * Mandarin Chinese, with a vertical time orientation, influences thinking differently than English, which is horizontally oriented.
  * **Hopi Language and Time:**
    * Benjamin Lee Whorf (1941) suggested the Hopi language lacked a word for 'time.'
    * Ekkehart Malotki (1983) challenged this, finding that the Hopi do talk about time, countering Whorf's claim.

### **Linguistic Determinism**

* The strong form of linguistic relativity is where language shapes and limits our thinking entirely.
* Implies that thought is impossible without language.
* **Discounting Linguistic Determinism:**
  * **The Dani Language:**
    * Boroditsky (2001) discussed the Dani language with two colour words.
    * Despite linguistic determinism predicting limited colour perception, Dani people were able to learn and think about additional colours.
* **Whorf's View on Determinism:**
  * **Whorf's Original Ideas:**
    * Boroditsky (2001) suggests that Whorf's original view on linguistic determinism has been abandoned.
    * Wouter Beek (2012) argues that Whorf never proposed linguistic determinism but rather a softer form—linguistic relativity.
* **Myth about Inuit and Snow Words:**
  * **Pinker's Critique:**
    * Steven Pinker (1995) challenges the idea that the Inuit have numerous words for snow.
    * The claim that more words lead to different thinking about snow is debated, with varying estimates on Inuit snow vocabulary.
