## Charlton et al. (2000)

**Aim(s):**

* Investigate the impact of television on the social behaviour of young children.

**Procedure:**

* **Background**: St. Helena, with a recorded population of 5,644 in 1994, lacked broadcast television until that year.
* **Sample:** A random sample from the school population (approximately 160 children aged 3-8) was used.
* Video recordings of free-play behaviour were conducted four months before and several years after the introduction of television in 1994 and 2000, respectively.
* 256 video recordings in 1994 and 344 minutes in 2000 were analyzed.
* Playground cameras recorded daily routines, and data was coded using eight selected items from the PBOS for pro-social and anti-social behaviours.
* Inter-coder reliability ensured consistency through independent coders comparing and discussing coding for every 60-second interval.
* Behaviour was measured using the number of anti-social and pro-social behaviours in 30-minute time frames pre- and post-television introduction.

**Results(s):**

* A mean number of eight PBOS anti- and pro-social behaviours was analyzed in eight 30-minute time frames.
* Only 9 out of 64 comparisons were statistically significant.
* 2 significant decreases in anti-social behaviour post-television.
* 5 significant decreases in pro-social behavior post-television.
* 2 significant increases in pro-social behaviour post-television.
* Boys exhibited nearly four times more anti-social acts than girls.
* Pro-social behaviour occurred approximately twice as much as anti-social behaviour.

**Conclusions:**

* No conclusions were drawn regarding individual television viewing and behaviour changes.
* Over 5.3 years, little change was observed in pro- or anti-social behaviour.
* Close-knit community vigilance may contribute to children's good behaviour.
* Television did not appear to influence behaviours like hitting, fighting, pushing, or kicking.
* Boys' increased anti-social play is not linked to television.


|**Strengths**|**Weaknesses**|
|:---|:---|
|Naturalistic study, providing real-world insight into the impact of television on children's behaviour.|Lack of control over extraneous variables, making it challenging to establish causation.|
|Longitudinal design spanning 5.3 years, allowing for examining behavioural changes over time.|Limited generalizability as the study focused on a specific island community with unique characteristics.|
|Random sampling from the school population enhances the study's representativeness.|Lack of consideration for individual differences in television viewing habits and content preferences.|
|Video recordings provide objective data on behaviour, reducing potential observer bias.|Reliance on observational data may not capture all relevant factors influencing behaviour.|
|Inter-coder reliability is employed to ensure consistent and accurate coding of behaviours.|Limited statistical significance in some comparisons may question the robustness of the findings.|
|Considering the close-knit community's influence on children's behaviour adds depth to the interpretation.|Failure to draw specific conclusions about individual television viewing and behaviour changes limits the study's explanatory power.|
|Recognition of potential gender differences in anti-social behaviour provides valuable insights.|The absence of a clear link between boys' increased anti-social play and television calls for further investigation.|
