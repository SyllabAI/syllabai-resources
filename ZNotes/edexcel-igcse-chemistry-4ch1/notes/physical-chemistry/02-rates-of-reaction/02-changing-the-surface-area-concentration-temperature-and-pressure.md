## Changing the Surface Area, Concentration, Temperature and Pressure of The Reactants

### Surface Area

* The more finely divided the solid is, the faster the reaction takes place. The surface area in contact with the gas or liquid is much greater, so more particles are exposed on the solid’s surface.
* The ***frequency*** of successful collisions increases as the surface area increases.
* **Example**: Catalytic converters have a large surface area to allow harmful gases like carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides to be converted to carbon dioxide and nitrogen, which are less harmful to the environment. The large surface area speeds up the reaction, as the gases are in contact with the converter for only a short amount of time.

**Experiment: effect of surface area**

* Add $1 \ g$ of magnesium ribbon to $50 \ cm^3$ of dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask connected to a gas syringe.
* Record the volume of hydrogen collected every $10 \ s$.
* Repeat with $1 \ g$ of magnesium powder, keeping the volume, concentration and temperature of the acid the same.
* **Result**: The powder produces gas faster (steeper curve), but the same total volume of gas is produced.

### Concentration

* If we halve the concentration of the acid used (and the acid is the limiting reactant), the amount of carbon dioxide produced would halve as well, as only half the number of acid particles would be present in the reaction.
* ***Increasing*** the concentration ***increases*** the rate of the reaction, as there are more particles in the same volume, so there are more successful collisions per unit time.

**Experiment: effect of concentration**

* Place a conical flask on a piece of paper marked with a black cross.
* Add $50 \ cm^3$ of sodium thiosulfate solution to the flask, then add $5 \ cm^3$ of dilute hydrochloric acid and start a stopwatch.
* A yellow precipitate of sulfur forms, making the solution cloudy. Stop the stopwatch when the cross can no longer be seen.
* Repeat using different concentrations of sodium thiosulfate solution (dilute it with water, keeping the total volume the same).
* **Result**: The higher the concentration, the shorter the time taken for the cross to disappear, so the faster the rate.

### Temperature

* Changing the temperature still produces the same amount of carbon dioxide at the end, but the reaction is faster (if the temperature is increased) and levels off sooner on the graph. The same mass of gas is given off, as the amounts of reactants are the same as before.
* Increasing the temperature gives the particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more often. More of the particles also have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy, so the frequency of successful collisions per second increases.

**Experiment: effect of temperature**

* Carry out the sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid "disappearing cross" experiment as above, keeping the concentrations and volumes the same.
* Warm the sodium thiosulfate solution to different temperatures (e.g. $20 \ ^\circ C$, $30 \ ^\circ C$, $40 \ ^\circ C$, $50 \ ^\circ C$) using a water bath before adding the acid.
* Time how long it takes for the cross to disappear at each temperature.
* **Result**: The higher the temperature, the shorter the time taken, so the faster the rate.

### Pressure

* If we take a fixed mass of gas and decrease the volume of its container, the pressure increases, as the particles hit the walls of the container more often.
* This forces the gas particles closer together, so they collide more often and the rate of reaction increases.
