## Experiments

### Investigating the Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

This method can be used for aqueous sodium chloride, dilute sulfuric acid and copper(II) sulfate solution.

**Method**:

1. Take a glass electrolysis cell (or a beaker), close it with a rubber bung, place two inert graphite electrodes through it and connect them to a d.c. power supply (battery).

2. Pour the solution being tested into the cell, so that the electrodes are covered.

3. Fill two small test tubes with the solution and invert one over each electrode, so that any gas produced is collected.

4. Switch on the power supply and observe what happens at each electrode.

5. Test any gases collected (see the table below) and note any solid formed on the electrodes.

**Safety**:

* Carry out the electrolysis of sodium chloride solution in a fume cupboard, as chlorine gas is toxic.

* Wear eye protection, and use a low voltage d.c. supply.

**Results**:

#|
|| **Solution** | **Cathode ($-$)** | **Anode ($+$)** | **Observations** ||
|| Sodium chloride, $NaCl(aq)$ | Hydrogen, $H_2$ | Chlorine, $Cl_2$ | Bubbles at both electrodes; pale green gas at the anode ||
|| Dilute sulfuric acid, $H_2SO_4(aq)$ | Hydrogen, $H_2$ | Oxygen, $O_2$ | Bubbles at both electrodes; twice the volume of gas at the cathode ||
|| Copper(II) sulfate, $CuSO_4(aq)$ | Copper, $Cu$ | Oxygen, $O_2$ | Pink-brown solid on the cathode; bubbles at the anode; blue colour fades ||
|#

*Products of electrolysis of three aqueous solutions using inert electrodes.*

**Tests for the gases**:

#|
|| **Gas** | **Test** | **Result** ||
|| Hydrogen | Hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the tube | Burns with a squeaky pop ||
|| Oxygen | Insert a glowing splint into the tube | The splint relights ||
|| Chlorine | Hold damp blue litmus paper at the mouth of the tube | Turns red, then bleaches white ||
|#

*Tests used to identify the gases produced during electrolysis.*

### Quantitative Electrolysis

* **Purpose**: To investigate how the **current** affects the **amount of product** formed in a fixed time. The more current that flows, the more electrons are transferred at the electrodes each second, so more product is formed. The volume of gas produced is directly proportional to the current (for the same time).

* **Independent variable**: Current. **Dependent variable**: Volume of hydrogen collected. **Control variables**: Time, concentration and volume of solution, distance between the electrodes.

**Method**:

1. Take a glass electrolysis cell, close it with a rubber bung, place the electrodes through it, and connect it to the battery with a variable resistor and an ammeter in the circuit.

2. Pour $50\ cm^3$ of concentrated sodium chloride solution into the cell.

3. Fill a burette with the solution and invert it over the cathode to collect the hydrogen gas.

4. Take the initial reading on the burette.

5. Set the current to $0.2\ A$ using the variable resistor and switch on.

6. Start the timer, and record the volume of gas collected after a fixed time (e.g. 5 minutes).

7. Repeat for different currents ($0.4\ A$, $0.6\ A$, $0.8\ A$ and $1.0\ A$).

8. Repeat the whole experiment and calculate the mean volume for each current, to get more reliable results.

9. Record the data in a table and plot a graph of volume of gas against current. A straight line through the origin shows that the volume is directly proportional to the current.
