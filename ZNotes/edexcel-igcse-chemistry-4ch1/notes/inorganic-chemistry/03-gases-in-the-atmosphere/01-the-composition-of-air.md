*Approximate composition of dry air. The percentages add up to about 100%. Air also contains a variable amount of water vapour.*

* For IGCSE, you can remember these as roughly **78% nitrogen**, **21% oxygen**, **0.9% argon** and **0.04% carbon dioxide**.

### Experiments to Find the Percentage of Oxygen in Air

* These experiments all work in the same way: a substance that reacts with **oxygen** (but not with nitrogen or the other gases in air) is left in contact with a known volume of air. The oxygen is used up, so the volume of gas decreases.
* The decrease in volume is equal to the volume of oxygen that was in the air.

$$\text{percentage of oxygen} = \frac{\text{decrease in volume}}{\text{starting volume of air}} \times 100
$$

**Method 1: Heated Copper**

1. Set up two gas syringes connected by a silica (heat-resistant glass) tube containing copper turnings. One syringe contains $100\ cm^3$ of air and the other is empty.
2. Heat the copper strongly with a Bunsen burner.
3. Push the air backwards and forwards over the hot copper using the syringes. The copper reacts with the oxygen, so the volume of gas decreases.
4. The pink-brown copper turns **black**, as copper(II) oxide forms.
5. Keep going until the volume stops changing, which shows all the oxygen has reacted.
6. Let the apparatus cool to room temperature before taking the final reading, as hot gas takes up a larger volume.

$$2Cu(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2CuO(s)

$$

**Method 2: Iron (Rusting)**

1. Push some **damp iron wool** (or iron filings) into the bottom of a measuring cylinder or graduated tube.
2. Turn the tube upside down, with its open end in a trough of water. Record the starting volume of air in the tube.
3. Leave it for about a week. The iron rusts, using up the oxygen in the trapped air, so the **water rises** up the tube to take its place.
4. When the water level stops changing, record the final volume of gas.

$$4Fe(s) + 3O_2(g) + 2xH_2O(l) \rightarrow 2Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O(s)

$$

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/aeaa32994e1b277e298d55c9.webp =804x)

* This method is slow, but it doesn't need heating, and the iron keeps reacting until all the oxygen is used up.

**Method 3: Phosphorus**

1. Place a small piece of **phosphorus** on an evaporating dish floating on water, inside a bell jar. The bell jar is marked into five equal parts, and its open end stands in a trough of water.
2. Light the phosphorus (for example, by touching it with a hot wire through the bung) and put the bung in straight away.
3. The phosphorus burns, using up the oxygen. The phosphorus oxide formed dissolves in the water.
4. Once the bell jar has cooled, the water has risen by about **one-fifth** of the volume, showing that about $21\%$ of air is oxygen.

$$P_4(s) + 5O_2(g) \rightarrow P_4O_{10}(s)

$$

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/735cba6c28514db416415a03.webp =822x)

* **Safety**: Phosphorus catches fire easily in air and is toxic, so this experiment is done by a teacher as a demonstration, in a fume cupboard.

**Why Results Can Be Lower Than 21%**

* Not all of the oxygen has reacted (the experiment was stopped too early).
* The gas was not cooled to room temperature before the final volume was measured.
* The apparatus was not airtight, so air leaked in.

### Worked Examples: Calculating the Percentage of Oxygen in Air

* $100\ cm^3$ of air is passed backwards and forwards over heated copper until the volume stops changing. The copper reacts with the oxygen in the air. The final volume of gas is $79\ cm^3$.

* Volume of oxygen used up $= 100 - 79 = 21\ cm^3$

$$\text{percentage of oxygen} = \frac{21}{100} \times 100 = 21\%
$$

* A measuring cylinder contains $50.0\ cm^3$ of air and some damp iron wool. After one week, the volume of gas is $39.5\ cm^3$.

* Decrease in volume $= 50.0 - 39.5 = 10.5\ cm^3$

$$\text{percentage of oxygen} = \frac{10.5}{50.0} \times 100 = 21\%
$$
