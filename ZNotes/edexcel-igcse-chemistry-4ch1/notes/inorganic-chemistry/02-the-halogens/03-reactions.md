## Reactions

### Hydrogen and Halogens

* Halogens react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides.

  $H_2(g) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2HCl(g)$

* Hydrogen halides are all acidic, poisonous gases.

* When dissolved in water, they form acids.

  $HCl(g) \xrightarrow{\text{dissolved in water}} HCl(aq)$

### Alkali Metals and Halogens

* Halogens react with alkali metals to form salts.

  $2Na(s) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2NaCl(s)$

* In this reaction, sodium burns in chlorine with a yellow flame to produce white sodium chloride.

### Displacement Reactions

* Chlorine is more reactive than bromine, which is more reactive than iodine.

* A more reactive halogen will **displace** a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt.

* e.g. chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution:

  $Cl_2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + Br_2(aq)$

* The more reactive halogen forms the halide ion (it gains electrons), while the less reactive halide ion is turned back into the element (it loses electrons).

### Displacement as Evidence for the Reactivity Trend

* Chlorine displaces both bromine and iodine, so it is the **most reactive** of the three.

* Bromine displaces iodine but not chlorine, so it is **less reactive than chlorine** but **more reactive than iodine**.

* Iodine displaces neither, so it is the **least reactive**.

* These results show that **reactivity decreases going down Group 7**, which matches the explanation in terms of electronic configuration: the further the outer shell is from the nucleus, the harder it is to gain an electron.
