## How to Answer All Question Types

### **1. Factual/Retrieval Questions**

**What it's testing:**
Can you identify the *exact* information directly stated in the passage?

**How to answer:**

* Underline the keywords in the question.

* Go back to the passage and scan for exact matches or close synonyms.

* Rephrase words around the main info if possible (if not, copy the minimal phrase that answers the question).

**Common mistakes:**

* Copying the whole sentence (you’ll lose marks for giving extra irrelevant info).

* Not checking if your answer makes grammatical sense when read independently. (Do **not** use parts of sentences in quotes, unless specifically asked to).

### **2. Inference Questions**

**What it's testing:**
Can you understand something *not directly said*?

**How to answer:**

* Ask yourself: “What is the writer *trying to show me* without saying it outright?”

* Use context clues (adjectives, tone, contradictions).

* Frame your answer like this:

  * *The writer implies that...*

  * *It can be inferred that...*

  * *This suggests that the writer believes/thinks/feels...*

* Your answer must be justified by a sentence or phrase in the passage.

  * *This is shown by the phrase...*

**Common mistakes:**

* Making wild guesses not supported by the text. (**Always** show evidence from the text and explain **why** it was used by the author).

* Confusing opinion with inference - **your views on the text ≠ inference.** (Opinion expresses a personal belief or feeling, while inference is a logical conclusion drawn from evidence within the text. **Use the context and overall tone of the text to your benefit**).

### **3. Word Meaning in Context**

**What it's testing:**
Do you know what the word means *right here* in this sentence, and not generally?

**How to answer:**

* Read the full sentence (and one before/after).

* Swap the word with a synonym that keeps the meaning intact.

* Use logic: “Would this synonym change the tone or meaning?” If yes, try again.

**Common mistakes:**

* Giving dictionary meanings that don’t fit the passage. (**Context** is the most important part)

* Choosing a synonym that slightly changes the meaning (especially emotional tone).

* Writers often use words **metaphorically** or **symbolically** - don’t rely on literal meaning alone.

### **4. Writer’s Attitude and Tone**

**What it's testing:**
Can you *detect* how the writer feels about the subject?

**How to answer:**

* Highlight any emotive language, figurative/loaded wording, bias or neutrality, shifts in tone across the text. (If your answer is persuasive and well-justified, almost any interpretation is acceptable).

* Choose precise tone adjectives -  e.g., “skeptical” is better than just “negative.” (Depending on the context it’s used in.)

**Common mistakes:**

* Using vague words like “nice” or “bad.” It is crucial to broaden your vocab.

* Ignoring sarcasm or irony (these are harder to pinpoint, and often come with exaggeration or contrast).

* Not exploring the layers of every text evidence. (Talk about every possibility of what the words suggest, what they convey to you, and if they were intended that way.)

### **5. Comparison Questions**

**What it's testing:**
Can you *clearly* *identify* similarities or differences between two texts, views, or characters?

**How to answer:**

* Find matching or contrasting points (tone, evidence, opinions, intentions).

* Use balanced phrasing: “Text A argues that…, whereas Text B suggests…”

* Stick to 1 comparison per point - don’t crowd your writing with points that can stand alone, or be mentioned in another part of your answer.

**Common mistakes:**

* Only describing one text.

* Only writing about the differences. (Analyse the similarities, talk about why that is important, especially if other points contradict the other text)

* Being vague (e.g., “they are both different” - how? why?).

### **6. Evaluative Questions (Effectiveness, Bias, Persuasiveness)**

**What it's testing:**
Can you assess how *well* the writer argued their point? Did they use strong logic, good evidence, or emotional manipulation?

**How to answer:**

* Judge the content: Is it balanced or biased?

* Judge the technique: Is it persuasive? (The question may also ask you to evaluate on different grounds - underline and understand what the question expects of you before beginning)

* Use PAFT — Purpose, Audience, Format, Tone. (Identify what each one is ideally meant to be for that specific prompt, then decide if the writer achieved it effectively.)

**Common mistakes:**

* Describing the text instead of evaluating it. (Don’t focus on what the content is saying; focus on how well the content achieves the intended purpose.)

  * For example, the **purpose** of a parenting tips article is to inform; the **audience** is parents or expectant parents; the **format** is usually formal or semi-formal; and the **tone** is supportive, reassuring, and informative.

* Saying “the writer is biased” without giving evidence, or vice versa.

### **7. Summary Questions**

**What it's testing:**
Can you identify and condense the *main ideas* while leaving out extra stuff?

**How to answer:**

* Go straight to the relevant paragraph(s). (You will typically be provided with relevant lines to refer to).

* Identify only the core points - no examples or illustrations.

* Rewrite the ideas in your own words, in a clear and simple sentence if possible.

* Aim for 120 words, unless stated otherwise.

**Common mistakes:**

* Including examples, repetition, or irrelevant info.

* Writing too much when the question asks for 2-3 ideas. (Highlight keywords and ideas before you begin writing.)
