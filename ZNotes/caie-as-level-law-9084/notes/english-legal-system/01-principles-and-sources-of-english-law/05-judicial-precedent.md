## Judicial Precedent

### **Doctrine of Judicial Precedent**

The doctrine of judicial precedent, or stare decisis, forms a fundamental aspect of the common law legal system. It establishes the importance of previous judicial decisions as authoritative guidance for future cases.


1. **Ratio Decidendi, Obiter Dicta, and Law Reporting:**
   * Ratio Decidendi: The legal reasoning or the essential principle upon which a decision is based. It forms the binding part of a precedent.
   * Obiter Dicta: Statements made by the court that are not directly relevant to the decision. While not binding, they can be persuasive.
   * Law Reporting: Officially record and publish judicial decisions, making them accessible for reference.
2. **Binding, Original, and Persuasive Precedents:**
   * Binding Precedents: Decisions from higher courts or courts at the same level are binding on lower courts.
   * Original Precedents: Decisions that create new legal principles and serve as a foundation for future cases.
   * Persuasive Precedents: Decisions from other jurisdictions or lower courts that are not binding but may be considered for their persuasive value.
3. **Hierarchy of the Courts:**
   * Supreme Court: The highest court in the UK legal system, followed by the Court of Appeal, the High Court, and various specialized courts and tribunals.
4. **Supreme Court and Use of the Practice Statement:**
   * Practice Statement: A tool the Supreme Court uses to depart from its own previous decisions when it considers it right to do so.
   * Conditions: The court may exercise this discretion when it appears necessary to do justice, there is a conflict between its previous decisions, or the law needs development.
5. **Court of Appeal and Exceptions in Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co (1944):**
   * Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co (1944): Established that a lower court is not bound by its own decisions and may depart if satisfied that the previous decision was given per incuriam (without considering relevant statutory provisions or precedent).
   * Exceptions: The Court of Appeal is generally bound by its own decisions unless there are exceptional circumstances, a conflicting decision, or the decision was given per incuriam.
6. **Avoidance Techniques – Overruling, Reversing, and Distinguishing:**
   * Overruling: Occurs when a higher court decides that a previous decision was wrongly decided and expressly states that it should no longer be followed.
   * Reversing happens when an appellate court overturns a lower court's decision on appeal.
   * Distinguishing: The court finds that the material facts of the present case are different from the facts of the precedent, making the precedent inapplicable.

### **Significance of the Doctrine**

* Legal Certainty: Promotes consistency and predictability in legal decisions.
* Incremental Development: This allows the law to evolve gradually by creating and refining legal principles.
* Judicial Efficiency: Provides a framework for resolving cases by referring to established legal principles.

### **Critiques and Challenges**

* Rigidity: Some argue that the doctrine can be inflexible and prevent the law from adapting to changing societal values.
* Uncertainty: The hierarchy of the courts can create uncertainty, especially when a lower court is unsure of which precedent to follow.
* Overemphasis on Precedent: Critics suggest that strict adherence to precedent may hinder the pursuit of justice in certain cases.

In conclusion, the doctrine of judicial precedent is a cornerstone of the common law system, balancing the need for stability with the necessity for legal evolution. It provides a structured framework for the development of legal principles while also allowing for flexibility through exceptions and avoidance techniques when justice demands deviation from established norms.
