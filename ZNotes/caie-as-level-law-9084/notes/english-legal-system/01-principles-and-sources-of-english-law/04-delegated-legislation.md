## Delegated Legislation

### **Types of Delegated Legislation**

Delegated legislation refers to law-making by authorities other than Parliament. It allows detailed rules and regulations to be created under the authority of an existing Act of Parliament. Three common types of delegated legislation are Orders in Council, Statutory Instruments, and Bylaws.


1. **Orders in Council:**
   * Definition: These are laws issued by the Monarch on the advice of the Privy Council without the need for a new Act of Parliament.
   * Purpose: Orders in Council are often used for matters requiring swift action or those concerning national security.
   * Example: The declaration of a state of emergency.
2. **Statutory Instruments (SIs):**
   * Definition: These are laws made by government ministers under the authority of a specific Act of Parliament.
   * Purpose: SIs are used to provide detailed regulations and rules that support the primary legislation.
   * Example: Environmental regulations issued under the Environmental Protection Act.
3. **Bylaws:**
   * Definition: Local authorities, such as city councils or transport authorities, can create bylaws to address specific local issues.
   * Purpose: Bylaws are often used to regulate matters not adequately covered by national legislation.
   * Example: Parking regulations in a specific city.

### **The Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006**

* Overview: The Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006 (LRRA) is a piece of primary legislation that grants significant powers to government ministers to amend or repeal existing legislation through Orders in Council.
* **Key Provisions:**
  * Henry VIII Clauses: The LRRA is often criticized for including Henry VIII clauses, which permit the modification of primary legislation without requiring a new Act of Parliament.
  * Simplified Procedures: The Act aims to streamline legislative processes to reduce bureaucratic obstacles.

### **Parliamentary Controls on Delegated Legislation**

Parliamentary oversight is crucial to ensure accountability and prevent potential abuses of power in the creation of delegated legislation. Several controls are in place:


1. **Pre-drafting Consultation:**
   * Purpose: Ministers may consult relevant stakeholders and experts before formally drafting delegated legislation.
   * Advantages: This ensures that diverse perspectives are considered, potentially leading to more effective and balanced regulations.
2. **Scrutiny Committees:**
   * Role: Committees, such as the House of Commons Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments, review proposed delegated legislation.
   * Functions: Scrutiny committees examine the legal and procedural aspects of the legislation to ensure it aligns with parliamentary intent.
3. **Affirmative Resolution:**
   * Definition: Some forms of delegated legislation require approval through a specific parliamentary resolution.
   * Process: The legislation must be expressly approved by both Houses of Parliament before becoming law.
   * Advantages: Provides a more rigorous level of scrutiny for significant or controversial regulations.
4. **Negative Resolution:**
   * Definition: Legislation automatically becomes law unless either House objects within a specified timeframe.
   * Process: If objections are raised, the delegated legislation may be annulled.
   * Advantages: Allows for a less burdensome process for routine or uncontroversial regulations.

### **Court Controls on Delegated Legislation**

To ensure that delegated legislation stays within the boundaries set by Parliament, the courts play a crucial role through various controls:


1. **Judicial Review:**
   * Purpose: Courts can review the legality of delegated legislation to ensure it complies with statutory authority and constitutional principles.
   * Criteria: Judicial review examines delegated legislation's legality, procedural fairness, and rationality.
2. **Locus Standi:**
   * Definition: The legal standing or right to bring a case before the court.
   * Requirement: Individuals or groups challenging delegated legislation must demonstrate a sufficient interest in the matter.
3. **Procedural Ultra Vires:**
   * Definition: Refers to actions taken beyond the legal authority granted by Parliament.
   * Court Action: Courts can intervene if the procedures for creating the delegated legislation are flawed or irregular.
4. **Substantive Ultra Vires:**
   * Definition: Refers to delegated legislation exceeding the powers conferred by the parent Act.
   * Court Intervention: Courts can declare such legislation invalid if it goes beyond the intended scope granted by Parliament.
5. **Unreasonableness:**
   * Definition: Courts can intervene if the content of delegated legislation is deemed unreasonable or irrational.
   * Subjective Test: This involves evaluating whether a reasonable authority could have made the same decision.
