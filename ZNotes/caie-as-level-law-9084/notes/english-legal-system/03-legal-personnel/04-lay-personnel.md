## Lay Personnel

### **Role of Lay Magistrates in Civil and Criminal Cases**

**Qualifications:**

* Lay Magistrates:
  * Lay magistrates, also known as justices of the peace, are not required to have formal legal qualifications.
  * Selected based on personal qualities, community engagement, and an understanding of local issues.
  * The emphasis is on diversity to ensure a broad representation of the community.

### **Selection and Appointment**

* Lay Magistrates:
  * Appointed by the Lord Chancellor, usually following a recommendation from local advisory committees.
  * The selection process aims to ensure a balanced and diverse bench that reflects the demographics of the local population.
  * Candidates are interviewed, and their suitability is assessed based on their understanding of justice and community awareness.

### **Training**

* Lay Magistrates:
  * Lay magistrates undergo comprehensive training, which includes legal knowledge, court procedures, and practical aspects of the role.
  * The initial training is followed by ongoing development to keep magistrates informed about changes in the law and legal procedures.
  * Training helps ensure that lay magistrates can confidently and effectively contribute to the justice system.

### **Jury Role in Criminal Courts**

* Qualifications for Jury Service:
  * Jurors are selected from the electoral register.
  * Eligibility criteria include being 18–75 years old and having been a resident for at least five years since the age of 13.
  * The random selection process aims to provide a cross-section of the community.

### **Vettings and Challenges**

* Jury Service:
  * Jurors undergo vetting to ascertain their suitability and impartiality.
  * Legal representatives have the opportunity to challenge potential jurors, either without cause (peremptory challenges) or with cause (challenge for cause).
  * The goal is to ensure a fair and unbiased jury for the trial.

### **Alternatives to the Use of the Jury**

* **Magistrates' Court:**
  * Lay magistrates typically preside over cases in the Magistrates' Court, dealing with less serious criminal offences.
  * They may sit alone or as a panel of three, and their decisions are based on the law and their understanding of community standards.
* **Crown Court:**
  * Serious criminal cases in the Crown Court often involve a jury.
  * However, in certain circumstances, such as complex fraud trials, a judge alone, without a jury, may preside.
  * This ensures that the trial process is tailored to the specific nature of the case.

### **Summary**

* Qualifications:
  * Lay Magistrates: No formal legal qualifications; emphasis on diverse qualities.
  * Jurors: Random selection from the electoral register; eligibility criteria in place.
* Selection and Appointment:
  * Lay Magistrates: Appointed by the Lord Chancellor based on local advisory committee recommendations.
  * Jurors: Randomly selected, aiming for a representative cross-section.
* Training:
  * Lay Magistrates: Comprehensive initial and ongoing training.
  * Jurors: No specific training.
* Jury Role in Criminal Courts:
  * Jurors are selected randomly to participate in criminal trials.
* Vetting and Challenges:
  * Jurors are vetted for impartiality; legal representatives can challenge them.
  * Lay magistrates are selected based on suitability and community awareness.
* Alternatives to the Use of the Jury:
  * Lay Magistrates handle less serious cases in Magistrates' Court.
  * Judges may preside without a jury in certain serious cases in the Crown Court.
