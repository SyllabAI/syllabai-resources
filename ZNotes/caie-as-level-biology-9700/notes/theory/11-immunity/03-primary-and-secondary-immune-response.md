## Primary and secondary immune response

### Primary immune response

* First time seeing the pathogen; Takes longer to respond and creates memory cells in the end

* Flow of events :

   1. Pathogen enters the body: A pathogen carrying a specific antigen enters the body.

   2. Phagocytosis: A macrophage engulfs the pathogen and digests it.

   3. Antigen presentation: The macrophage displays the pathogen's antigen on its cell-surface membrane.

   4. T-helper cell activation: A T-helper cell with a complementary receptor binds to the presented antigen and becomes activated.

   5. B-lymphocyte activation: The activated T-helper cell releases cytokines, which activate a B-lymphocyte with a complementary antibody/receptor to the same antigen.

   6. Clonal selection and expansion: The activated B-lymphocyte undergoes mitosis, producing many genetically identical B cells.

   7. Plasma cells form: Some of these B cells differentiate into plasma cells.

   8. Antibody production: Plasma cells produce and secrete specific antibodies complementary to the pathogen's antigen.

   9. T-killer cell activation: T-helper cells also activate T-killer cells, which can destroy infected body cells displaying the relevant antigen.

  10. Memory cells form: Some B cells and T cells become memory cells, allowing a faster and stronger response if the same antigen enters the body again.

### Secondary immune response

* Exposure to the same pathogen/antigen again

* Very fast (immediate recognition by memory cells)

* Produces a much higher concentration of antibodies rapidly.

* Pathogen is cleared before symptoms develop.

### Non-specific immune response

**Inflammation**

* Tissues become **hot, red, and swollen** due to a chemical substance released from cells called **histamine**.

### Specific immune response

The specific immune response can be divided into :

* Cell-mediated immunity

* Humoral immunity.

**1. Cell-mediated immunity**

#### Antigen Presentation

* A host cell, e.g. a macrophage that engulfed a pathogen, or an infected host cell, displays foreign antigens on its cell membrane, becoming an Antigen-Presenting Cell (APC).

#### T-helper cells

* T-helper cells bind to the presented antigen on APCs and release **cytokines** to direct the rest of the immune response.

#### T-killer cells

* T-killer cells with complementary receptors bind specifically to foreign antigens on infected host cells.

#### Clonal Expansion

* Cytokines cause activated T-cells to divide by mitosis, forming active effector T-cells and Memory T-cells.

#### Destruction

* T-killer cells release proteins such as **perforin** that punch holes in the membrane of infected cells, causing them to lyse and die, thereby destroying the host environment required by the pathogen.

**2. Humoral immunity**

* #### Recognition

  * A B-cell recognises a specific non-self antigen using its surface membrane receptors.

* #### Activation

  * The B-cell engulfs the antigen and presents it on its surface.

  * A T-helper cell recognises this antigen and releases **cytokines** to fully activate the B-cell.

* #### Clonal Expansion & Differentiation

  * The activated B-cell divides by **mitosis** into:

    * Plasma cells: Short-lived cells that produce and secrete large quantities of specific antibodies into body fluids.

    * Memory B-cells: Long-lived cells that remain in circulation to provide rapid immunity upon re-exposure.

#### Action of Antibodies

* Antibodies bind to foreign antigens to neutralise them, clump them together (agglutination), or mark them for destruction by phagocytes.
