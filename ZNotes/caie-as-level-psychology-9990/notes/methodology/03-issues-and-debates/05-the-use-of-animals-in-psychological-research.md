## The Use of Animals in Psychological Research

### Why Psychologists Use Animals for Research?

* **To Study Biological Baselines**: Animals share foundational evolutionary traits, brain structures, and hormonal pathways with humans. This allows psychologists to study basic behaviours (like learning, stress, or gendered play) without human cultural biases.

* **Controlling the Environment:** Researchers can perfectly manage an animal’s entire life experience such as their diet, social interactions, and living conditions from birth. This level of experimental control is impossible with human participants.

* **Faster Longitudinal Studies:** Many animals have significantly shorter lifespans and breeding cycles than humans. This enables psychologists to observe developmental changes, or the effects of traits across multiple generations, in a relatively short timeframe.

### Benefits of Animal Research

* **Fewer Demand Characteristics:** Animals do not try to guess the aim of the experiment, alter their behavior to please the researcher, or suffer from social desirability bias. This ensures high internal validity in behavioural observations.

* **Developing Humane Interventions:** Discoveries made through animal research often lead to better methods for animal management, conservation, and veterinary care, directly improving captive animal welfare worldwide.

* **Procedures Exempt from Human Testing:** It allows researchers to investigate physiological or behavioural mechanisms that would be completely illegal or deeply unethical to test on humans. For example, isolation studies, strict dietary control, or invasive surgeries.

### Ethical Concerns and Issues

* While humans and animals share biological similarities, human behavior is highly mediated by complex language, abstract thought, advanced culture, and social norms.

* Findings from animal studies might lack population validity when applied to humans. Assuming an animal's cognitive processes are exactly like a human's can lead to inaccurate conclusions.

* Animals must be housed in conditions that mimic their natural social structures. Furthermore, if researchers use food deprivation to motivate an animal during a task, the level of deprivation must be kept to the absolute minimum necessary to avoid physical harm or metabolic distress.

* Researchers are ethically required to choose a species that is least likely to suffer. They must consider whether the animals were bred in captivity or taken from the wild, as wild-caught animals suffer significantly higher distress when placed in a lab environment.

### Examples from Core Study

* **Hassett et al. (Monkey Toy Preferences Study)**

  * An observational task assessing toy choice and preferences in monkeys.

    * **Why animals were used**: Hassett et al. needed a sample completely free from human societal gender socialization, advertisements, and parental conditioning to see if toy preferences were purely biological (nature).

    * **Generalisability** **Issue**: While the study showed clear biological preferences in monkeys, it is difficult to generalize these findings directly to human children, whose toy choices are deeply influenced by complex cultural norms, language, and gender expectations that monkeys do not experience.

* **Fagen et al. (Elephant Trunk Wash Study)**

  * An elephant trunk wash procedure study using positive reinforcement training.

    * **Why animals were used:** The research aimed to improve the welfare of the animals themselves. By using food rewards (Positive Reinforcement Training), the researchers taught young Asian elephants to voluntarily participate in veterinary tests.

    * **Ethical Considerations:** Instead of using traditional, dominant physical punishment or heavy sedation (which causes severe distress), the researchers refined the procedure to eliminate harm, prioritizing the animals' physical and psychological well-being.
