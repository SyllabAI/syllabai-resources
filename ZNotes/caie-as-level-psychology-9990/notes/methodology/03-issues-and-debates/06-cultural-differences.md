## Cultural Differences (Ethnocentrism)

This debate explores the extent to which psychological findings can be generalized across diverse geographical and social cultures, balanced against the problematic tendency of researchers to interpret all human behaviour through the exclusive lens of their own cultural background.

* **Ethnocentrism:** A bias where a researcher treats Western or localized behaviour as the universal human "norm”, viewing any cross-cultural variations as "abnormal" or deficient.

* **Individualism vs. Collectivism:** Individualist cultures (e.g., USA, UK) prioritize personal autonomy, unique identity, and individual success. Collectivist cultures (e.g., Japan, global indigenous societies) prioritize group harmony, interdependence, and collective duty over personal desire.

* **Imposed Etic:** This occurs when a researcher takes a psychological tool, test, or theory rooted in their own specific culture and inappropriately applies it globally, falsely assuming the behaviour is universally understood.

### Ethnocentric Example from Study

* **Milgram (Obedience Study)**

  * Conducted entirely at Yale University using 40 white, male American participants from the New Haven area.

  * The study assumes that its baseline 65% obedience rate reflects a universal human vulnerability to destructive authority. In reality, obedience levels are heavily mediated by a society's Power Distance Index (PDI) which tells how acceptable structural hierarchy is to its members.

  * We cannot generalize these findings globally. Subsequent cross-cultural replications revealed that obedience dropped below 30% in highly individualist, low-PDI cultures like Australia, while soaring above 80% in high-PDI societies like Germany, proving that obedience is culturally constructed rather than a human universal.

* **Baron-Cohen et al. (The "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" Study)**

  * Conducted using British adults and undergraduate students from the University of Cambridge.

  * The "Eyes Test" relies heavily on Caucasian facial features and the highly specific socio-cultural decoding of Western emotional expressions.

  * Applying this test to non-Western populations creates an imposed etic. In East Asian cultures, where eye-contact norms and outward emotional displays differ structurally from the UK, neurotypical participants often score lower. This is not due to a deficit in Theory of Mind, but simply a cultural variance in eye-gaze decoding, invalidating the test as a pure, objective cognitive measure.

### Cross-Cultural Attempt Example from Study

* **Piliavin et al. (Subway Samaritan Study)**

  * Conducted in a real-world setting on the New York subway, capturing a naturally occurring, racially diverse mix of Black and White passengers.

  * While the study achieved excellent racial diversity within an American context, the behavioural setting remains bound to a Western, highly capitalist urban environment.

  * The resulting Cost-Reward Model views helping behaviour as a calculated, individualistic choice. However, in deeply collectivist cultures, assisting a struggling stranger is often an automatic, non-calculative moral obligation to the wider community. This means the model's validity drops significantly outside of major Western cities.
