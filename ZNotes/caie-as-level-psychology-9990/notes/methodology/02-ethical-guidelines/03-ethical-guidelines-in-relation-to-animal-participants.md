## Ethical Guidelines in Relation to Animal Participants

* Based on the **British Psychological Society’s guidelines for Psychologists** working with animals (2012)

  Replacement

  |
  
  Replace the use of animals with alternative non-animal research methods whenever possible, such as computer simulations, observations, or existing data.

  |
  
  * Computer simulations of learning instead of live animals.

  * Human volunteers in virtual reality experiments.

  * Cell cultures instead of live animal tissue.

  ||
  ||
  
  Numbers

  |
  
  According to the Animal Scientific Procedures Act (1986),
  psychologists should always use the smallest number of animals that still accomplish the research aims and goals. Researchers should use the smallest number of animals necessary to achieve valid results.

  |
  
  * Instead of using 100 rats, researchers perform statistical calculations showing only 30 are needed.

  * Sharing data between research teams to avoid repeating similar experiments.

  ||
  ||
  
  Species and strain

  |
  
  Psychologists should choose a species that is scientifically and ethically suitable for the intended use. Knowledge of the animal’s previous experience is also essential.

  The chosen animal should:

  * Be capable of answering the research question.

  * Experience the least possible suffering.

  * Not be chosen simply because it is convenient or inexpensive.

  Researchers must also consider:

  * Natural behaviour

  * Intelligence

  * Social needs

  * Health

  * Previous breeding history

  |
  
  Researchers should justify why a particular species is most suitable for investigating a research question.

  **Good practice**

  * A researcher studying memory uses laboratory mice because the biological mechanisms are already well understood and many previous studies allow comparison.

  **Poor practice**

  * Using primates when rats could answer the same research question would be considered unethical because primates have more complex cognitive and social needs.

  ||
  ||
  
  Procedures

  |
  
  Psychologists should consider research that enriches rather than harms. Procedures that may cause discomfort, injury, stress, etc., need a Project Licence, which can be gained after a cost-benefit analysis.

  |
  
  psychologist studying learning trains rats to press a lever for food. Ethically guidelines are followed by keeping sessions short, allowing rats to receive breaks, keeping cages clean, and stopping the test if a rat becomes ill or distressed.

  ||
  ||
  
  Pain and distress

  |
  
  Research causing physiological or psychological pain or distress should be avoided. Whatever procedure is used, any adverse effects on animals must be recognised and assessed, and immediate action taken wherever necessary.

  |
  
  Suppose a mouse develops an infection following surgery.

  Researchers should:

  * Treat the infection immediately.

  * Provide pain relief.

  * Remove the mouse from the study if necessary.

  ||
  ||
  
  Housing

  |
  
  For social species, housing should allow interaction with other animals where appropriate. Caging should
  recreate aspects of the natural environment that are important to
  the welfare of the animal and must take into account the social
  behaviour of the species.

  Animals should be monitored daily for signs of illness or stress.

  |
  
  Different animals require different housing conditions.

  * Rats are housed in groups because they are social animals.

  * Monkeys receive climbing equipment and puzzles to reduce boredom.

  ||
  ||
  
  Reward, deprivation and aversive stimuli

  |
  
  The researcher should consider the animal’s usual eating and drinking habits and metabolic requirements. When using deprivation and aversive stimuli, the researcher should ensure that there is no alternative way of motivating the animal that is consistent with the aims of the experiment and that the levels of deprivation are no greater than that needed to achieve the experiment's goals.

  |
  
  A rat is trained to press a button for food.

  Ethically:

  * The rat is only slightly hungry (around 85–90% of its free-feeding weight under veterinary supervision).

  * It still receives adequate nutrition each day.

  **Unethical example**

  Starving a rat for several days simply to increase motivation.

  ||
  ||
  
  Anaesthesia,  &

  |
  
  Animals should be protected
  from pain relating to surgery and euthanized if suffering lasting
  pain. Procedures that are likely to cause pain and discomfort should
  be performed only on animals that have been adequately
  anaesthetised by qualified personnel.

  |
  
  A neuroscientist implanting electrodes into a rat's brain ensure that the rat is fully anaesthetised before surgery, has vital signs monitored throughout, is not awakened until surgery is complete.

  ||
  ||
  
  Analgesia

  |
  
  Analgesia (pain killers) should be given after painful procedures unless they would seriously interfere with the research and continued until recovery.

  |
  
  After surgery, the rat receives pain medication as needed, followed by veterinary monitoring and daily health checks

  ||
  ||
  
  Euthanasia

  |
  
  In cases where the animal may suffer severely, cannot recover, or needs to be humanely killed at the end of the experiment to prevent prolonged suffering, euthanasia (mercy killing) is carreid out.

  |
  
  At the end of a neuroscience study, mice are humanely euthanised using approved methods before brain tissue is examined.

  This avoids prolonged suffering.
