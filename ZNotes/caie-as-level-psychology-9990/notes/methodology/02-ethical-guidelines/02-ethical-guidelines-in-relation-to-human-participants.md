Informed Consent

|

Participants must be given sufficient information about the study's purpose, risks, and procedures to make a conscious decision to join.

|

Providing a detailed information sheet and signature form before a memory test begins.

|

**Milgram (1963) Obedience Study:** Fails this; participants volunteered for a "study on memory and learning," not an obedience task.

||
||

Protection from Harm

|

Participants should not be exposed to physical or psychological risks greater than those encountered in normal daily life.

|

Ensuring a stressful task (like a public speaking simulation) features regular check-ins and a calm environment.

|

**Bandura (1961) Bobo Doll:** Criticized for potentially inducing long-term aggressive behavior patterns in young children.

||
||

Minimising Harm & Maximising Benefit

|

Researchers must design studies to yield maximum educational/scientific value while strictly reducing any possible distress or negative impacts.

|

Screening out highly anxious individuals from a high-stress trial to protect them while advancing anxiety research safely.

|

**Loftus and Palmer (1974):** High benefit (proved eye-witness testimony flaws) with virtually zero harm (just watching car crash films).

||
||

Right to Withdraw

|

Participants must know they can leave the study and have their data destroyed at any point without penalty or pressure.

|

Informing a participant they can stop a computer task and leave whenever they want, keeping their compensation.

|

**Milgram (1963):** The experimenter used verbal prods ("You have no choice, you must go on") which directly violated this right.

||
||

Deception

|

Participants should not be deliberately misinformed. If unavoidable for the study's validity, distress must be minimized.

|

Telling a participant a task measures "reaction time" when it actually measures how social media notifications distract them.

|

**Asch (1951) Conformity:** Deceived participants into thinking the confederates were fellow participants taking a vision test.

||
||

Debriefing

|

A post-study explanation revealing the true aims and consequences, ensuring participants leave in the same psychological state they arrived.

|

Revealing the hidden camera at the end of a social experiment and discussing how the participant feels about it.

|

**Milgram (1963):** Conducted extensive debriefings, introducing participants to the unharmed "learner" to restore peace of mind.

||
||

Confidentiality

|

A requirement to keep all participant data, results, and personal information secure and unreleased outside the research team.

|

Storing survey responses under participant numbers (e.g., Participant 042) rather than actual names.

|

**Case Studies (e.g., Freud's Little Hans):** Used pseudonyms to protect the identity of individuals, though distinct details sometimes challenged complete anonymity.

||
||

Privacy

|

Avoiding the invasion of a participant's personal physical or psychological space without their explicit knowledge or comfort.

|

Observing behavior only in public places where people naturally expect to be seen, rather than hidden spaces.

|

**Rosenhan (1973):** Pseudo-patients observed real psychiatric patients and staff in private ward settings without their explicit awareness.
