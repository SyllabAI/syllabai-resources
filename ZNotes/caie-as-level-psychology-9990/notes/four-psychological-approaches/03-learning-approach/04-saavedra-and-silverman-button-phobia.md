## Saavedra and Silverman (Button Phobia)

**Title**: Case Study: Disgust and a Specific Phobia of Buttons.

**Year**: 2002

### **Psychology being investigated**:

* **Classical conditioning** is a learning process where a neutral stimulus is paired with an unconditioned trauma. This creates a permanent association, turning the object into a conditioned trigger for an automatic fear response.

* **Evaluative learning** is a form of classical conditioning in which a person comes to perceive or evaluate a previously neutral object negatively. Negative evaluation is usually acquired through association with an unpleasant emotional response

  * The person may develop a negative emotional response even if they do not consciously expect harm.

  * An individual may negatively evaluate a specific object or event without anticipating the threat of an objective contaminant.

  * This elicits a feeling of disgust rather than fear.

* **Phobia** is an irrational, intense, and persistent fear of a specific object or situation that is completely disproportionate to any actual danger. In this study, it manifested as evaluative learning based on disgust rather than physical harm.

### **Aims**

* To investigate the cause of button phobia in a child.
* To attempt to treat a child’s phobia via targeting both disgust and fear responses.

### **Method**

* **Case study research method**:
  * Interviews, questionnaires, scales

  * Observations: Data collection techniques

  * Quantitative data: Distress ratings and Severity ratings

  * Qualitative data: Questions about why the boy found buttons disgusting
* **Sample**: A 9-year-old Hispanic American boy who was part of the Child Anxiety and Phobia Program at Florida International University. He came with an avoidance of buttons.
* **Sampling Technique**: Opportunity Sampling
* **Procedure**:
  * The boy and the mother gave informed consent to participate in the assessment and intervention procedures. Written consent was also provided to publish this study.

  * The boy met the DSM-IV criteria for a specific phobia of buttons.

  * The phobia began when the boy was 5 years old in kindergarten, during an art project involving buttons.

  * The boy ran out of buttons, so he was asked to come to the front of the class and take them. His hand slipped when he reached the bowl, and the buttons fell on him. He described this experience as distressful.

  * The duration of the phobia was 4 years.

  * He did not express significant stressors or events that could be related to the phobia’s onset during this period.
* **Behavioural Exposures:**
  * The child was treated using an exposure-based treatment programme, which is a behavioural therapy where a person is gradually exposed to a feared stimulus in a controlled way until anxiety reduces through habituation and counterconditioning.

  * Contingency management was also used, which is a technique where positive reinforcement is given when the desired behaviour is shown, increasing the likelihood it will be repeated. Here, the mother praised and rewarded the child for successfully completing exposure tasks.

  * Treatment sessions lasted about 30 minutes with the boy alone and 20 minutes with the boy and his mother.

  * The most difficult were small, clear plastic buttons.

  * He had 4 sessions of behavioural exposure to buttons using this hierarchy.

* **Disgust Imagery and Cognitions:**
  * After the behavioural exposure, it was planned to have 7 sessions looking into the boy’s disgusted imagery and cognitions with a view of helping him to change these over time.

  * Further probing revealed that the boy found buttons disgusting upon contact with his body.

  * He also expressed that buttons emitted unpleasant odours.

  * These 7 sessions explored the images, thoughts, and interpretations that made buttons seem disgusting to the boy.

  * He was prompted to imagine buttons falling on him, express how they looked, felt, and smelled, and elaborate on how these imagery exposures made him feel.

  * Although the boy indicated that buttons were disgusting and gross, even with intense probing, it was difficult for him to describe what about the buttons rendered them disgusting and gross.

### **Results**

* By session 4, the boy had successfully completed all in-vivo exposure tasks up to those with the highest distress ratings.
* Even though he could handle more and more buttons, his distress rating increased dramatically from sessions 2 to 3 and 3 to 4.
* In session 4, the boy’s subjective ratings that had been 6 or 7 before the treatment were now higher.
* This phenomenon supports evaluative learning, where a stimulus retains a negative emotional value (e.g. disgust or fear) even after behaviour improves. Although the child showed better contact with buttons, his distress did not fully reduce, suggesting that emotional evaluation is harder to change than behaviour.
* Disgust-related imagery exposures and cognitions appeared to be successful in reducing the boy’s subjective ratings of distress.
* In the imagery sessions, he had to imagine hundreds of buttons falling on him; before the cognitive restructuring, he rated the experience as 8. This decreased to 5 midway through the session and ended up as 3.
* In a session where he had to imagine hugging his mother while she was wearing a shirt with many buttons, the distress ratings went from 7 to 4 to 3.
* He was followed up 6 and 12 months after treatment, and he no longer met the specific criteria for phobia of buttons.

### **Conclusions**

* The findings suggest that disgust plays a key role in the development and maintenance of a phobia but a mixture of behavioural exposure and cognitive restructuring helped to eliminate the feelings of disgust.

### **Ethical Issues**

* The participant experienced severe distress during exposure to buttons, but protection was provided through gradual exposure, therapeutic support, and parental involvement throughout the treatment.
* Informed consent was taken from the mother and the boy.

### **Strengths**

* Qualitative and quantitative data were both acquired in this study.
* As this was a case study focusing on one individual, detailed and in-depth data could be collected. This increased the validity of the findings by allowing researchers to closely understand the development and treatment of the child’s phobia over time.
* The study was conducted in a therapeutic setting and hence had ecological validity.

### **Weaknesses**

* The study only involved one child, so the findings may not apply to other people with phobias. Hence, it has a low generalisability.
* The study relied partly on self-reported fear and disgust ratings, which may be affected by demand characteristics or social desirability. Therefore, it has a low reliability
* It is difficult to know whether improvement was caused only by the treatment, as there was no comparison group.

### **Issues and Debates**

* Application to Everyday Life: The study can be applied to the treatment of other phobias using exposure-based therapies. It also suggests that treatment may need to target both fear and disgust responses, not just avoidance, as negative emotional evaluations may persist even after behavioural improvement. This can help therapists develop more effective treatments for phobias.
* Nature vs Nurture: The phobia supports nurture, as it developed through a negative learning experience with buttons. However, individual vulnerability may also influence whether a phobia develops, suggesting both nature and nurture play a role.
