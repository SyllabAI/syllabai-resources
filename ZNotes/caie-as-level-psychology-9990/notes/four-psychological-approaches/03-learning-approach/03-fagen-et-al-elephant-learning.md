## Fagen et al. (Elephant Learning)

**Title**: Positive reinforcement training for a trunk wash in Nepal’s working elephants: demonstrating alternatives to traditional elephant training techniques

**Year**: 2014

### **Psychology being investigated**:

* **Operant conditioning:** Learning through the consequences of our actions. Behavior that is rewarded is more likely to be repeated.
* **Primary Positive Reinforcement (PPR)**: Something that is naturally rewarding and satisfies a basic biological or psychological need, making a behaviour more likely to happen again. For example, food and water.
* **Secondary Positive Reinforcement (SPR):** A secondary positive reinforcer is something that becomes rewarding through learning or association rather than being naturally rewarding on its own. In this study, the mahout would blow a whistle every time the elephant successfully performed a behaviour and then rewarded it with a banana. At first, the banana itself is the reward (primary positive reinforcer) and after repeated training, hearing the whistle itself became a reward (secondary positive reinforcement).
* **Shaping:** Involves reinforcing successful approximations of the desired behaviour.
* **Behavior chaining:** When different behaviors learned are chained together in a sequence and performed.

### **Background**

* Mahouts' traditional method of training elephants relies on punishment, which uses pain or fear to shape behaviour.
* Therefore, in 2011, the Nepalese government introduced annual Tuberculosis testing of captive elephants to reduce transmission, and researchers were encouraged to develop new training methods based on positive reinforcement, similar to the one published by Fagen et al.
* Particularly, Desmond and Laule (1991) found that positive reinforcement methods have been shown to improve the psychological well-being of elephants.
* SPR has been used successfully with several animal species, including pandas, primates and antelopes.

### **Aim**

* To investigate if free-contact traditionally trained elephants can participate in a trunk wash through training using positive reinforcement.

### **Method**

* **Research Method:**

  * Controlled Observation (Structured observation conducted in a natural/applied setting)

  * Common characteristics of the sample:  all docile, not currently pregnant, their mahout was willing to participate, traditionally trained and no previous experience with SPR training.

* **Sample:** 4 female juvenile elephants aged 5-7 years and 1 adult aged approximately 50 years.

* **Procedure:**

  * Elephants trained in the morning (7:30 to 10 am) and evening (4 to 7 pm)

  * Sessions were never more than two days apart.

  * To complete the trunk wash, elephants had to complete the 5 behaviors stated above in Table (v).

  * Elephants were trained using capturing, luring, shaping and secondary reinforcers

  * Overtime, syringe went from aversive stimulus to conditioned stimulus.

  * Trainer then encouraged elephant to accept syringe being inside the trunk so that fluid droplets can be injected inside.

  * The amount of fluid was gradually increased until the full 60 ml was accepted

* Measured variables included:

  1. Minutes of training

  2. Number of cues given by the trainer

  3. Success rate for each behaviour

  4. Success rate for the full sequence

* Elephants were graded on their ability to complete the sequence of behaviors, with approximately 80 percent being the passing grade.

* Individual behaviors scored with approximately 90 percent as the passing grade.

* If a sequence failed, they were tested for a shorted sequence.

### **Results**

* 4 juvenile elephants learned full trunk wash in 25-35 sessions.
* Session durations ranged between 10-13 minutes with an average of 12 minutes.
* Elephant 5, the oldest elephant, was never tested for the full trunk wash because she failed to learn the complete behavioural sequence. This may suggest the influence of individual differences in learning ability, possibly due to age, previous training history, or lower responsiveness to reinforcement compared to the younger elephants.
* Success rate of individual behaviors increased from 39% in session 10 to 89.3% in session 35.

### **Strengths**

* Use of behavioral checklist with detailed exact descriptions which increases reliability.
* The study was conducted in a naturalistic setting, which increases ecological validity

### **Weakness**

* The small sample size of elephants were all females and from one setting, which limits generalisability.
* Measurement of elephant’s performance was subjective since it was open to interpretation.

### **Ethics**

* The study used positive reinforcement, such as banana rewards and whistle cues, instead of punishment-based training methods.
* Researchers aimed to minimise stress and harm to the elephants throughout training.
* Traditional aversive elephant training methods were avoided, improving animal welfare.
* Desensitisation and counter-conditioning were used to reduce fear of the syringe gradually.
* The trunk wash training improved veterinary care by helping elephants cooperate voluntarily with medical procedures.

### **Issues and Debate**

* Application to everyday life-

  1. Animal welfare-Fagen’s training techniques improved captive elephant welfare by using positive reinforcement instead of stressful or forceful traditional training methods. The techniques can also be replicated to teach other animals behaviours safely and humanely.

  2. Public health-The trunk wash procedure helped elephants cooperate voluntarily with tuberculosis (TB) testing, improving diagnosis and treatment. This may also help reduce the spread of TB between elephants and humans.

* Individual vs Situational- the 5 elephants have individual differences in the speed at which they learnt the trunk wash.
