## Bandura et al. (Aggression)

**Title**: Transmission of Aggression Through Imitation of Aggressive Models.

**Year**: 1961

### **Psychology being investigated**:

* **Social Learning Theory:** Social behaviour is learned primarily by observing and imitating others. It is “learning indirectly by observing others”. The four meditational processes to it are:
  * **Attention:** Observers must pay attention to the behaviour of the model. The model must have some feature that attracts the observer.

  * **Retention**: Observers must store the behaviour in their long-term memory so that the information can be used again (when the observer wants to imitate the behaviour).

  * **Reproduction:** Observers must feel capable of imitating the retained, observed behaviour.

  * **Motivation**: Observers who experience vicarious reinforcement are more likely to imitate the behaviour. This is when the model has been rewarded for performing the observed behaviour. Vicarious punishment can also happen when the model is punished for the observed behaviour. Therefore, the observer is less likely to imitate it.

### **Background**

* Learning behaviour by imitating others is called observational learning.
* Several studies have demonstrated that children are influenced by witnessing adult behaviour in the same situation and in the presence of the adult who modelled the behaviour.
* The study investigated imitation of aggressive behaviour, defined as any physical or verbal behaviour intentionally aimed towards other people or objects with the intent to cause harm,  and whether imitation varied according to the sex of the child and the sex of the model

### **Aims**

* Overall aim: To investigate observational learning of aggression.
* Specific aims:
  * To see whether children would reproduce aggressive behaviour when the model was absent.

  * To look for gender differences in the learning of aggression.

### **Method**

* **Research Method**: Laboratory experiment

* **Observations Method**: Technique to record behaviour

* **Experimental Design**: Independent groups, Matched pairs design

  * **Independent Variables**: Children were matched for pre-existing aggression levels before being allocated to conditions, so the study used a matched-pairs feature within an independent measures design

    (1) behaviour of the model – aggressive or non-aggressive

    (2) sex of the model

    (3) sex of the child

  * **Dependent Variables**: amount of behaviour observed in 8 categories (imitative physical aggression, imitative verbal aggression, imitative non-aggressive verbal responses, mallet aggression, sits on bobo doll, non-imitative physical and verbal aggression, aggressive gun play, non-aggressive play)

* **Sample**: 72 participants- 36 boys and 36 girls from Stanford University Nursery School. Their ages ranged from 37 months to 69 months, with a mean age of 52 months. 2 adults, a male and a female, served as the role models in the experiment. 1 female experimenter conducted the study for all 72 participants.

* **Sampling Technique**: Opportunity Sampling

* **Procedure:**

  * Participants were divided into 8 experimental groups, 6 children each, and 1 control group of 24 participants who watched no model.

  * Children in each condition were matched for their levels of physical aggression, verbal aggression, aggression towards inanimate objects and aggressive inhibition.

  * The experimenter and a nursery teacher rated 51 participants independently on a 5-point scale. A very good agreement of 0.89 of inter-rater reliability was achieved.

* **Modelling the Behaviour**

  * Each child was brought individually to a playroom by the experimenter. She then invited the model, who was outside the room.

  * The experimenter took the child to one corner of the room, which was their play area. She demonstrated how the child could design a picture with potato prints and the stickers provided.

  * The experimenter then escorted the model to the room's opposite corner, which contained a small table and chair, a tinker toy set, a mallet and a 5-foot inflated Bobo doll. She explained that these were the materials provided for the model to play with and then left.

  * In the **non-aggressive condition**, the model assembled the tinker toys quietly, subduedly, ignoring the Bobo doll.

  * In the **aggressive condition**, the model began assembling the tinker toys, but after a minute, he turned to the Bobo and played with it.

  * The model punched the Bobo, sat on it and punched it repeatedly, hit it with a mallet on the head and aggressively kicked it about in the room.

  * The model also included verbal aggression such as “sock him in the nose,” “Pow,” “throw him in the air”, and two non-aggressive comments such as “he keeps coming back for more” and “he sure is a tough fella”.

  * This lasted for about 10 minutes.

  * Two more observers (the models) observed the child play and had an inter-rater reliability of 0.90.

  * Three types of aggression were recorded:

    (3) partially imitative aggression.

### **Results**

* There was a significant difference in levels of imitative aggression between the group that witnessed aggressive behaviour and the other two groups.
* There was a significant difference in levels of imitative physical and verbal aggression.
* Significantly more non-aggressive play was recorded in the non-aggressive model condition.
* Children who had witnessed an aggressive model were significantly more aggressive themselves.
* Overall, very little difference was present between aggression in the control group and the non-aggressive modelling condition.
* Boys who observed aggressive models showed an average imitation score of **38.2**, compared to **1.5** in the non-aggressive condition.
* Boys were significantly more physical aggressive than girls. Although girls displayed slightly more verbal aggression, this difference was not statistically significant.

### **Conclusions**

* Witnessing aggression in a model can be enough to produce aggression by an observer.
* Children selectively imitate gender-specific behaviours.

### **Ethical Issues**

* The principle of protection from harm may have been violated because children were exposed to aggressive models, which could have influenced their behaviour negatively.

* Informed consent was taken from the nursery teacher- This raises ethical issues as parents/guardians are supposed to give consent for children, and the children themselves must participate in the experiment willingly.

* Although parental consent may have been obtained, the children themselves may not have been capable of giving fully informed assent due to their age. This raises ethical concerns about voluntary participation.

* The children should have been debriefed after the experiment to ensure they understood that aggressive behaviour should not be copied. Debriefing would also help reduce any potential long-term effects caused by observing aggressive models.

* Because the participants were very young children, they may not have fully understood that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time.

### **Strengths**

* The study has high levels of standardisation and, hence, a higher reliability.
* The study has many controls, such as standardised model behaviour, fixed toy placement, standardised observation time, and matched aggression ratings and this increases its validity.
* There was a high inter-observer/ inter-rater reliability.
* The study may have had a relatively low risk of demand characteristics because young children were unlikely to fully understand the aim of the experiment. However, demand characteristics cannot be completely ruled out because the aggressive model and the presence of the Bobo doll may have acted as cues encouraging aggressive behaviour
* Using a matched pairs design reduced the effects of the participant variable.
* The use of quantitative data allows for easier statistical analyses.

### **Weaknesses**

* The study lacks ecological validity and mundane realism, for example, aggression toward an inflatable doll in a laboratory may not reflect real-life aggression toward people.
* It raised ethical concerns because children were exposed to aggressive behaviour, which may have influenced their behaviour negatively and violated protection from harm.
* The sample was limited because all participants came from a single nursery school associated with Stanford University. This reduces generalisability because the findings may not represent children from different cultural or social backgrounds.

### **Issues and Debates**

* **Application to Everyday Life**: The study helps explain how children may imitate behaviours observed in parents, teachers, celebrities, social media influencers, or television characters. It also explains how exposure to violent media may increase aggressive behaviour. Social learning theory can also be applied positively, as teachers and parents can model prosocial behaviours such as cooperation, kindness, and respect to encourage similar behaviour in children.
* **Individual and Situational Explanation**: This study supports the situational side of the debate as the situation that the children found themselves in caused the imitated aggressive behaviour.
* **Nature versus Nurture**: this supports the nurture side of the
  debate as the environment they found themselves in caused the
  imitated aggressive behaviour.
* **The use of young children** may have reduced demand characteristics because they were less likely to understand the true aim of the study, increasing the validity of the behaviour observed. However, exposing children to aggressive role models may have encouraged aggressive behaviour, raising ethical concerns about protection from harm.
