## **Explanations for Differences in Educational Achievement and Experience Based on Gender**

### Differential Gendered Socialisation and Social Control

**++Gender Socialisation and Education++**

* Boys and girls are socialised into gender roles before starting school.

* Children develop ideas about behaviour and attitudes appropriate to their sex from the culture around them.

* **Girls**

  * May be influenced to prioritise marriage and motherhood over a career.

  * May see qualifications as unnecessary and not work hard in school.

  * Some may use education to secure a high-status husband, so marriage and education are not always alternatives.

* **Boys**

  * Likely to see their future in terms of work.

  * May not feel pressure to gain qualifications if male jobs are manual and easily accessible.

**++Changes in Modern Industrial Societies++**

* Shift away from traditional gender roles

  * Girls increasingly have career aims and are motivated to succeed academically.

  * More opportunities and successful female role models influence girls’ attitudes.

  * Girls now see careers as achievable alongside or instead of marriage.

* Fewer traditional male manual jobs

  * Makes educational qualifications more important for careers in ICT, business, and finance.

  * In countries with mostly manual male jobs, boys may lack motivation to work hard for exams.

**++Gendered Subject Choices++**

* Socialisation influences subject choice at GCSE and higher levels.

* **Boys**

  * More likely to choose technical subjects: mathematics, physics, computing.

  * Careers often linked to these subjects: accountancy, programming, and engineering.

* **Girls**

  * More likely to choose humanities and social sciences: history, sociology, literature.

  * Careers often involve working with people: teaching, nursing.

### Teacher Expectations

* Boys and girls may be treated differently by teachers.

* **Boys**

  * Poor study habits and misbehaviour are often seen as 'normal'.

  * Behaviour tolerated due to gender stereotypes: boys expected to be outgoing, confident, and dominant.

* **Girls**

  * Similar misbehaviour is less tolerated.

  * Some teachers may have lower academic expectations and are less likely to encourage hard study.

### Cultural and Social Factors

**Gender Differences in Achievement**

* Material and cultural deprivation are less important for gender differences than for social class or ethnicity.

* In families with limited resources, boys’ education may be prioritised over girls’.

**Motivation, Attitudes, and Peer Pressure**

* Girls are generally more motivated and work more consistently over time.

  * Spend more time on homework, concentrate better, and are better organised.

  * Benefit more from coursework and continuous assessment than exams.

* Girls tend to mature earlier, so by age 16, they are more likely to recognise the importance of studying hard.

* Gender socialisation influences attitudes to effort.

  * Girls often underestimate their ability and believe hard work is necessary.

  * Boys may overestimate their ability and believe effort is less necessary.

  * Studying hard is more accepted in female peer groups, while some boys face pressure not to study because it is seen as unmasculine.

**Learning Styles and Schoolwork**

* Most schoolwork is based on reading and writing.

* Boys may be disadvantaged because they often prefer active and practical learning.

* Reading is often seen as a female activity due to early socialisation

* In some countries, girls have less access to education than boys.

### In-school Factors, e.g. pro- and anti-school sub-cultures, gendered sub-cultures

**Hidden Curriculum and Gender**

* Gender divisions are built into everyday school practices.

  * Registers, seating plans, and uniforms often separate boys and girls.

* Classroom interactions and resources can be gendered.

  * Boys may dominate practical activities such as science experiments.

  * Textbooks in science and technology often show boys more than girls.

  * These subjects may also be taught mainly by male teachers.

* This can discourage girls from subjects like science and technology

**Policies, Opportunities, and Role Models**

* Schools in most countries now aim to provide equal opportunities for boys and girls.

* Some countries run programmes to encourage girls to aim higher, especially in science and technology.

  * These include extra school resources and events with successful female role models.

* Teaching is female-dominated, but senior leadership roles remain male-dominated.

  * Boys are more likely to see male teachers in positions of authority.

  * Girls may gain the impression that leadership roles are less accessible to women.

**Peer Groups and Gendered Sub-Cultures**

* **Female** peer groups are more likely to be pro-school.

  * Girls often support one another academically and share school values.

* **Male** peer groups are more likely to be anti-school.

  * Masculine culture may value peer respect over academic success.

  * Boys may fear losing status if they work hard, as effort is associated with girls.

**Changing Gender Norms and Resistance**

* Research by Carolyn Jackson found some working-class girls adopting behaviours traditionally linked to boys.

  * Fighting, swearing, and aggression.

* This involves a minority but suggests that working hard may be seen as uncool by some girls.

* Not working can act as self-protection.

  * Failure can be explained as a lack of effort rather than a lack of ability.

* This may reflect pressure on both genders to succeed, especially among working-class learners.

### Labelling Theory

* Teachers make assumptions about students based on gender, as well as social class and ethnicity.

  * This shapes the type of attention boys and girls receive.

* **Girls**

  * Praised for appearance, behaviour, and neatness of work

  * Less likely to act out problems visibly

* **Boys**

  * Individuality and creativity are valued

  * More likely to be labelled as problems

  * The majority of school exclusions are boys

* **Effects of labelling**

  * Negative labels can become a self-fulfilling prophecy

  * Students may internalise labels, leading to underachievement

### Feminist Explanations, e.g. Patriarchy

* Focus on girls’ experiences and achievements within a patriarchal education system.

* Over the last 30 years, political attention has shifted to boys’ underachievement because girls now outperform boys in many countries.

* Continuing disadvantages for girls include:

  * Lack of confidence in their ability

  * Boys dominating classrooms

  * Receiving less teachers’ time than boys

  * Gendered subject choices

  * Teachers’ assumptions about ability

* Overcoming these issues could improve girls’ education and enhance life chances, particularly in employment.
