## The Development and Impact of Welfare States on Life Chances

* **Welfare states**

  * Most modern states are welfare states to some extent, meaning the government takes responsibility for citizens’ security and well-being.

  * Welfare is mainly funded through taxation on workers, with additional support from NGOs, charities, religious groups and informal social networks.

* **Reasons for welfare states**

  * *Moral reasons*: It is seen as wrong for some people to live in poverty while others prosper; charity and support for the poor are valued across major religions.

  * *Political reasons*: Welfare helps prevent anger, social unrest and class conflict. Functionalists argue that it maintains social stability, and governments may use welfare to gain public support.

* **Different welfare models**

  * *Scandinavian model* (e.g. Sweden, Norway, Denmark):

    * High taxes, especially on the wealthy.

    * Generous benefits and services.

    * Results in relatively low inequality.

  * *USA model*:

    * Based on individualism and self-reliance.

    * Provides only basic welfare as a safety net.

    * Does little to reduce inequality.

### **Free and Universal Education**

* Most governments now provide basic education for all children, whereas in the past, many children either never went to school or left after a few years.

* Schooling is usually free, but hidden costs (uniforms, books, transport, fees) can still prevent poorer families from accessing education.

* Universal primary education exists in all countries, but many poorer countries lack the resources to provide universal secondary education.

  * As a result, many children in developing countries leave school after the primary level

* Free universal education improves life chances in many ways:

  * They earn qualifications, skills and experience which are vital for employment later on in life

  * They won’t be illiterate, reducing the chances of exploitation as a result of not understanding their own rights

  * They will understand health and nutrition, so they are able to look after themselves and their families.

* Free universal primary education has only recently become normal for all countries

  * Because this is still recent, many adults in LEDCs are still illiterate

* Schooling is often prevented by wars or other disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic or the Sudan Civil War

### Unemployment Benefits

* Many countries try to help unemployed people who cannot find a job

* Unemployment benefits are payments made to people while they are unemployed

  * But for this, they need to register as unemployed and actively look for a job

* Unemployment benefits are quite low and difficult to live on, to discourage people from deliberately avoiding work

  * But they can help people survive during difficult times

### Pensions

* Pensions are regular payments given to people once they reach the retirement age set by the government.

  * Before pensions, older people had to continue working or rely on family support.

* Pensions are funded through taxation on working people.

  * In some countries, this includes a separate social security tax

* State pensions aim to prevent poverty, not to provide a high income.

  * Many people also have private pensions paid into by themselves and their employers to increase retirement income.

* Pensions are becoming more expensive for governments.

  * Life expectancy has increased, so people receive pensions for longer.

  * Some governments have raised the retirement age to reduce costs.

* Pensions improve life chances by reducing the risk of poverty in old age.

  * Some older people remain at risk, especially those needing expensive care 

* Women are more likely to receive smaller pension**s**.

  * Because many women had career breaks or unpaid domestic work, and made fewer tax contributions.

* e.g., UK State Pension and USA Social Security

### Low-cost Housing

* Governments may attempt to improve the life chances of disadvantaged people by providing better housing at a low cost to people who struggle to buy a house or pay rent.
  * e.g., Brazil’s Minha Casa Minha Vida programme or UK council housing

### Free Healthcare

* Access to healthcare affects life chances because medical treatment can be very expensive.

  * Poorer people may not be able to afford doctors or hospital treatment, and even middle-class people may struggle

* Some governments provide free healthcare as part of the welfare state to improve life chances.

  * This is funded through taxation and allows everyone to receive treatment without worrying about cost.

  * e.g., the UK’s National Health Service (NHS).

* Countries differ in how much healthcare they provide for free.

  * Some only offer basic or emergency care, which may not be fully free.

  * Countries like the USA and China rely heavily on private health insurance, which many poorer people cannot afford.

* Private healthcare exists even where free healthcare is available.

  * Those who can pay may receive faster or better-quality treatment, creating inequality in health outcomes.

### **Universal Basic Income**

* Universal basic income (UBI) is an alternative to traditional welfare, where the government pays everyone a fixed amount of money regularly.

  * It has only been tested in a few countries on a small scale.

  * The payment acts as a safety net, so no one falls into poverty.

  * People can still choose to work to earn more, but work is not compulsory.

* **Supporters of UBI** argue it reduces poverty and inequality.

  * It removes the benefit trap, where people avoid low-paid work because they would lose benefits.

  * It could reduce government spending in the long term by cutting administration costs and lowering costs linked to poverty, such as healthcare, crime and policing.

* **Opponents of UBI** argue it may reduce motivation to work.

  * They believe welfare should encourage people into employment rather than provide unconditional income.

* **Example of UBI:** [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://progressandpovertyinstitute.org/exploring-universal-basic-income-and-its-implementation/), which pays residents an annual dividend ($1,312 in 2023) from state oil revenues
