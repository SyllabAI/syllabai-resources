## Equations

### **Writing Chemical Equations**

* Chemical equations show the molar amount of reactants that react to form a molar amount of product
* When writing chemical equations, we must:
  * use chemical symbols instead of names
  * ensure our chemical equations are balanced
  * include state symbols where needed
  * use the correct type of arrow

### **Using Chemical Symbols**

* For simple compounds, the chemical formula should be used instead of the name
* For example:
  * water is $H_2O$
  * iron(II) sulphate is $FeSO_4$
  * calcium carbonate is $CaCO_3$
* The chemical formula of each element can be found in the periodic table

### **Balancing Chemical Equations**

* Chemical equations should be balanced to ensure that the number of atoms of each element in the reactants is equal to the number of atoms of each element in the products
* This is done by adding coefficients that denote the number of the particular molecule participating in a reaction
* **For example:**
  * $H_2 SO_4\ +\ NaOH\ \rightarrow\ Na_2 SO_4\ +H_2O$
  * There are two sodium atoms on the right (product) side. Thus, $NaOH$ should be multiplied by 2 to ensure that the amount of sodium atoms is equal on the left and right sides of the reaction
  * Thus: $H_2 SO_4\ +\ 2NaOH\ \rightarrow\ Na_2 SO_4\ +H_2 O$
  * Now, the sodium atoms are equal; counting the number of oxygen ($O$) and hydrogen ($H$) atoms would show that the left side of the reaction has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom more than the right side. Thus, $H_2O$ should also be doubled
  * This results in the final equation: $H_2 SO_4\ +\ 2NaOH\ \rightarrow\ Na_2 SO_4\ +2H_2 O$ with both sides having the same number of atoms of each element

### **State Symbols**

* When required, state symbols should be added to the right of each molecule in a chemical formula to denote its state of matter during the reaction:
  * ($s$) is used to denote solid
  * ($l$) is used to denote liquid
  * ($g$) is used to denote gas
  * ($aq$) is used to denote an aqueous state, that is, if the molecule is dissolved in water
* An example of state symbols being used in a reaction:
  * $2HCl(aq)\ +\ CaCO_3 (s)\ \rightarrow\ CaCl_2 (aq)\ +\ H_2 O(l)\ +\ CO_2 (g)$

### **Arrows in Equations**

* The reactions above all involve the $\rightarrow$ symbol as the reactions all go to completion. However, some reactions, especially reactions in organic chemistry, will not complete as the products will react to form the reactants when the concentration of the products is high. Thus, these reactions will involve the $\leftrightharpoons$ symbol to show that the reaction will not go to completion and is reversible.
* Example:
  * $N_2 (g)\ +\ 3H_2 (g)\ \leftrightharpoons\ 2NH_3 (g)$

### **Writing Ionic Equations**

* The ionic equation shows only the ions involved in a reaction and does not show any ions that do not participate in a reaction, otherwise known as a spectator ion

* **Steps to writing an ionic equation:**

  1. write out chemical formulas of ionic compounds as ions if they are in an aqueous state
  2. remove the ions if they are present on both sides of the reaction

* **Example:**

  * $NaOH(aq)\ +\ HNO_3 (aq)\ \rightarrow\ NaNO_3 (aq)\ +\ H_2 O(l)$
  * The chemical compounds can be written as ions: $Na^{+} (aq) + HO^{-} (aq) + H^{+} (aq) + NO_{3}^{-} (aq) \rightarrow\ \ Na^{+} (aq) + NO_{3}^{-} (aq) + H_2 O(l)$
  * Notice $H_2O$ is not broken down as it is not an ionic compound in an aqueous state
  * Hence, after removing $Na^{+}$ and $NO_{3}^{-}$ as they appear at both sides of the reaction, we are left with: $H^{+} (aq) + HO^{-} (aq)\ \rightarrow\ H_2 O(l)$

### **Writing Ionic Half-Equations**

* Ionic half-equations are used when referring to redox reactions, where reduction and oxidation occur simultaneously
* Example: $2H^{+} (aq) + 2e^{-}\ \rightarrow\ H_2 (g)$
