## Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

* High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry can be used to deduce the atomic structure and molecular mass of a compound. It is a more refined version of mass spectrometry.
* Nuclear Magnetic Resonance is present in two types, carbon 13 NMR and proton NMR they both allow you to deduce the structure of a molecule as different bonds absorb different energies, so they are displayed on different peaks.
* TMS peak (tetramethylsilane) has a peak of 0 so it is used to compare the other peaks to it as it has four identical carbon environments
* Carbon 13 NMR analyses the different number of carbon environments in a molecule after it is dissolved in a solvent. Some compounds are symmetrical, so they will appear under the same peak, so the molecular formula is needed to deduce the compound fully.
* H 1 NMR (proton NMR) analyses a molecule's different hydrogen peaks, also measured against TMS. These compounds must be dissolved in $CCl_{4}$ as they do not contain hydrogen; therefore, they won’t appear on the spectrum. Compounds containing isotopes of hydrogen, such as deuterium, can also be used.
* The peaks in proton NMR show the relative intensity of each hydrogen environment. These peaks may also have splitting patterns to show where each hydrogen environment is located in the molecule. These peaks follow the n+1 rule, telling you how many hydrogens are on the adjacent carbon molecule. The n is the number of hydrogens on the adjacent carbon. Singlets have no hydrogens on the adjacent carbon; doublets have one hydrogen on the adjacent carbon; triplets have two hydrogens on the adjacent carbon; quartets have three hydrogens on the adjacent carbon, and so on.
