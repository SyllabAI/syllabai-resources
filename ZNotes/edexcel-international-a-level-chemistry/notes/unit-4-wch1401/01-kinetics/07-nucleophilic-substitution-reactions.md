## Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

* Two nucleophilic substitution reactions can occur: $S_{n}1$ and $S_{n}2$.
* $S_{n}1$ has only one reactant in the rate equation: rate =k\[A\] | This reaction is a two-step reaction, and two different enantiomers can be formed
* $S_{n}2$  has two reactants in the rate equation: rate = k\[A\]\[B\] | This reaction is a one-step reaction that has an intermediate. Only one product is formed.
* \[A\] is the halogenoalkane | \[B\] is the nucleophile
* In  $S_{n}1$ , the halogen group leaves the halogenoalkane first, creating a carbocation where the nucleophile can attack. The rate-determining step is the slowest step, which is creating the carbocation. The nucleophile attracts regions with a positive charge and attacks very quickly.
* In  $S_{n}2$, the halogen group leaves simultaneously as the nucleophile reacts, making an unstable intermediate. This is the only step in the reaction, so it is the rate-determining step, so there are two reactants in the rate equation.
* Tertiary halogenoalkanes react via  $S_{n}1$  as they form the most stable carbocations.
* Primary halogenoalkanes react via  $S_{n}2$ as smaller groups are attached to the carbocation, making it easily accessible. Secondary halogenoalkanes can react by either mechanism ( $S_{n}1$  or  $S_{n}2$ )
