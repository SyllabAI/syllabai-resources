# Redox Chemistry and Groups 1, 2 and 7

## Oxidation and Reduction

* Oxidation is the loss of electrons (OIL).
* Reduction is the gain of electrons (RIG).
* $Fe_{2}O_{3} + 3CO ​ \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_{2}$
  * This is an example of an equation where oxidation and reduction of two **different** species are taking place simultaneously.
  * This is called a redox reaction.
  * Here, $CO$ is oxidised to $CO_{2}$, and $Fe_{2}O_{3}$ is reduced to Fe
* The species being **oxidised** is the **reducing agent,** and the species being **reduced** is the **oxidising agent**.
* Common oxidising agents include: $O_2$, $H^{+}$, halogens, $Fe^{3+}$, acidified $KMnO_{4}$, acidified $K_{2}Cr_{2}O_{7}$, $H_{2}SO_{4}$, etc.
* Common reducing agents include: metals, $Fe^{2+}$, $Na_{2}S_{2}O_{2}$

### **Oxidation Number**

* The oxidation number indicates the degree of oxidation of an atom in a chemical compound.
* Oxidation numbers can be assigned to any atom in a compound, and it helps identify whether an atom has been oxidised or reduced.

**Rules To Assigning Oxidation Numbers**

1. Uncombined elements have an oxidation number of 0.

   1. Example: the oxidation number of $O_2$, $Mg$, etc. is simply 0.

2. Group 1 elements have an oxidation number of +1.

3. Group 2 elements have an oxidation number of +2.

4. Group 3 elements have an oxidation number of +3.

5. Halogens generally have an oxidation number of -1.

6. Oxygen has an oxidation number of -2 except in $H_{2}O_{2}$, where its oxidation number is -1 and with fluorine, it is +2.

7. The sum of all the oxidation numbers in a compound without a charge is 0.

8. In the presence of a charge, the oxidation numbers add up to the specific charge.

9. Hydrogen has an oxidation number of -1 with metals and +1 with non-metals.

* $2NO_{2} + H_{2}O ​\rightarrow HNO_{3} + HNO_{2}$
  * This is an example of an equation where oxidation and reduction of the **same** species are taking place simultaneously.
  * N in NO$_2$ has an oxidation number of +4.
    * Oxygen is $-2 \times 2=-4$, and the sum of oxidation numbers is 0 because the molecule does not have a charge.
    * The whole number coefficients do not affect the oxidation numbers.
  * N in $HNO_{3}$ has an oxidation number of +5.
    * An equation can be formed: $+1 + x + (-2 \times 3) = 0$, so $x is +5$
  * N in HNO$_2$ has an oxidation number of +3.
    * The equation that can be framed is: $+1 + x + (-2 \times 2) = 0$, so $x is +3$
  * Clearly, it is noticed that N (+4) has undergone both oxidation (+5) and reduction (+3)
  * When the same species undergoes both oxidation and reduction simultaneously, the reaction is called a **disproportionation** reaction.
* Some elements can exist with more than one oxidation number, so oxidation numbers are very useful in this regard.
  * Phosphorous chloride, for example, can exist as phosphorous (III) chloride or phosphorous (V) chloride because phosphorous has two oxidation states.
  * Below shows compounds with different oxidation states of chlorine and their systematic names:
  * $NO_{3}^{-} \rightarrow \text{ Nitrate (V)}$ 
  * $NO_{2}^{-} \rightarrow \text{ Nitrate (III)}$
  * $H_{2}SO_{3} \rightarrow \text{ Sulfuric (IV) acid}$
  * $H_{2}SO_{4} \rightarrow \text{ Sulfuric (VI) acid}$
  * $SO_{3}^{2-} \rightarrow \text{ Sulfate (IV)}$
  * $SO_{4}^{2-} \rightarrow \text{ Sulfate (VI)}$
