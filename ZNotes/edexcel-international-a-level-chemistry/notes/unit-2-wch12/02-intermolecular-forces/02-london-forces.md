## London Forces

* Arise from instantaneous fluctuations in electron density in a molecule.
* They are universal; they are found in all molecules, regardless of polarity.
* Consider the Oxygen molecule:
  * Even though both atoms are identical, fluctuations can cause the electron density in one atom to increase, giving it a short-lived negative ($\delta -$) charge.
  * Simultaneously, the neighbouring atom experiences a decrease in electron density, which gives it a partial positive ($\delta +$) charge.
* The strength of London forces increases with the size and mass of molecules.
  * $N_{2}$, being bigger and heavier than $O_{2}$ will have stronger London forces because it has more points of contact.
* As the number of electrons increases in the molecules, the strength of London forces also increases.
  * The boiling temperatures of noble gases follow the trend: He<Ne<Ar<Kr<Xe<Rn. This is because of the increasing number of electrons, which results in stronger London forces.
* London forces are also the reason why $Cl$ is gas, $Br$ is liquid, and $I$ is solid at room temperature.
  * $Cl$ is smaller and has a small surface area, leading to weaker London forces, so the atoms are away from each other, and it has lower melting and boiling points.
  * On the other hand, $I$ has a larger surface area and stronger London forces; atoms are closer to each other, so the melting and boiling points are higher.
