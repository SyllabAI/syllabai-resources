## Carbon-13 NMR Spectroscopy

- Although C-12 does not produce NMR signals as it has an even no. of
  protons, approximately 1.1% of an organic compound would have C-13
  which produces a signal

- C-13 NMR produces a spectrum with different chemical shifts for
  non-equivalent carbon atoms in a molecule

- Signals produced are discrete vertical lines on the spectra (no
  splitting)

- The heights of the lines are **not** usually proportional to no. of
  <sup>13</sup>C atoms present

- The solvent used for analysis is CDCl<sub>3</sub> that produces a
  small signal at 80ppm which can be ignored

- Use chemical shift values as in proton spectroscopy to identify
  protons present & hence work out the molecule

## **Predicting the Number of Peaks (Carbon Environments)**

The number of peaks in a C-13 NMR spectrum tells you exactly how many *different* carbon environments exist in the molecule.

* **No Symmetry:** If a molecule has no lines of symmetry, every single carbon atom is in a different environment.

  * *Example:* Butan-2-ol has 4 carbon atoms and no symmetry, so it will produce **4 peaks**.

* **With Symmetry:** If a molecule is symmetrical, equivalent carbon atoms will absorb at the exact same frequency and overlap into a single peak.

  * *Example:* Butane ($CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3$) has 4 carbon atoms, but it is symmetrical down the middle. Therefore, it only has 2 unique environments and will produce **2 peaks**.

  * *Example:* Benzene ($C_6H_6$) has 6 carbon atoms, but they are all perfectly equivalent due to the delocalised ring. It produces **1 peak**.

**NOTE: The Reference Standard:** Tetramethylsilane (TMS) is added to the sample to provide a reference signal at **0 ppm**. It is chosen because all 4 of its carbons are in the exact same environment (yielding one strong, sharp peak) and it is chemically inert and volatile (easy to remove).
