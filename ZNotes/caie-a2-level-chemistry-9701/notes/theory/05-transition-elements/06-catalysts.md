## Catalysts

* Transition elements can act as catalysts because they possess:

  * multiple stable oxidation states

  * vacant d-orbitals that are energetically accessible

* This allows them to form intermediate complexes

**Homogenous Catalysts-**

* Transition elements can transition between different oxidation states by losing or gaining electrons in the d shell

* Instead of reactants colliding directly and taking the pathway with high activation energy, the transition metal ion reacts with one reactant, changes its oxidation state, and then reacts with the second reactant to return to its original state.

* This creates a multi-step pathway where each individual step has a **lower activation energy** than the un-catalyzed single step 

* The reaction between $I^-$ and $S_2O_8^{2-}$ can be catalyzed by $Fe^{2+}/Fe^{3+}$.

  1. $S_2O_8^{2-} + 2Fe^{2+} \rightarrow 2SO_4^{2-} + 2Fe^{3+}$ ($Fe^{2+}$ gets oxidised to $Fe^{3+}$ )

  2. $2I^- + 2Fe^{3+} \rightarrow I_2 + 2Fe^{2+}$ ($Fe^{3+}$ gets reduced to $Fe^{2+}$ so it can again catalyse the reaction)

**Heterogenous Catalyst-**

* Heterogenous catalysts provide a surface for the reaction to take place. This relies on energetically accessible vacant d-orbitals

* Adsorption- reactant molecules use their lone pair electrons or $\pi$ electrons form weak bonds with the vacant d orbitals on the surface of the transition element

* Weakens bond between reactant molecules making them more reactive

* Because the d orbitals are energetically accessible, the transition state is stabilized.

* Desorption- once the product forms, the bonds to the surface break and the product molecule leaves
