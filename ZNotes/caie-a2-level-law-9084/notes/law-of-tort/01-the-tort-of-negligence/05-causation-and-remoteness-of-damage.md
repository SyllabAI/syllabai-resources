## Causation and Remoteness of Damage

### **Factual and Legal Causation**

* **Factual Causation**: Factual causation, also known as the "but for" test, involves determining whether the harm suffered by the claimant would not have occurred "but for" the defendant's breach of duty. Factual causation is established if the harm would not have happened but for the defendant's actions.
* **Legal Causation**: Once factual causation is established, legal causation considers whether it is fair and just to hold the defendant legally responsible for the harm. This involves assessing foreseeability and the proximity of the defendant's breach to the resulting harm.

### **Multiple Causes and Intervening Acts**

* **Multiple Causes**: When multiple factors contribute to the harm, the defendant's breach must be a substantial cause. If the defendant's breach materially contributes to the harm, they can be held liable.
* **Intervening Acts**: An intervening act may break the chain of causation. If the act is foreseeable, the defendant may still be held liable. However, if the act is unforeseeable and highly extraordinary, it may be considered a novus actus interveniens, breaking the causal link.

### **The Test for Remoteness of Damage**

* **Direct Consequences**: The test for remoteness of damage assesses whether the damage suffered was a direct consequence of the defendant's breach.
* **Reasonable Foreseeability**: For damage to be recoverable, it must be reasonably foreseeable at the time of the breach. The defendant may be held liable if the type of harm is foreseeable.
* **Wagon Mound Test**: The Wagon Mound (No. 1) (1961) case introduced the foreseeability test. If the harm is foreseeable, it may still be recoverable even if the extent or manner of occurrence is not.

### **Summary**

* Factual and Legal Causation:
  * Factual Causation: Determined by the "but for" test.
  * Legal Causation: Considers fairness and foreseeability in holding the defendant legally responsible.
* Multiple Causes and Intervening Acts:
  * Multiple Causes: The defendant's breach must be a substantial cause.
  * Intervening Acts: Foreseeable acts may not break the chain of causation; unforeseeable acts might.
* The Test for Remoteness of Damage:
  * Assesses whether the damage is a direct consequence of the breach.
  * Reasonable Foreseeability: Damage must be reasonably foreseeable during the breach.
  * Wagon Mound Test: Foreseeability of harm is crucial, even if the extent or manner is unforeseeable.
