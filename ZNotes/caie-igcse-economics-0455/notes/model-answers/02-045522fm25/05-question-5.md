## Question 5

**5(a)** Exports lead to money coming **into** the economy, and imports lead to money going **out** of the economy.

**5(b)** Commercial banks aim to maximise their profit, as most of them are in the private sector. If, for example, incomes have risen, commercial banks may be more confident that loans will be repaid and may increase their lending as more revenue will be earned when interest is paid back.

**5(c)** Economic growth may increase as an aging population has more work experience, and therefore can be more productive. However, older workers may be less healthy, the government has to spend more on healthcare and be absent from work frequently to rest. Economic growth may reduce as the aged workers may have skills that are outdated and may be less mobile. A larger proportion of the population are dependents, so more people may leave the labour force to take care of them, reducing economic growth.  

**5(d)**

Supply-side policies include spending on education & training, privatisation, lowering tax rates, and imposing regulations.

* Supply-side policy measures (which aim to increase the quantity and quality of factors of production) might reduce a government’s budget deficit as spending on education and training may increase productivity and output. This will increase economic growth and employment. Privatised firms may earn higher profits, and the government can collect tax revenue. Additionally, privatisation reduce government spending to support state-owned firms. These measures can result in the reduction of a government budget deficit in the long run.

* However, education and training may be expensive and will increase government spending and may not be effective. Lowering tax rates may reduce the government to lose out on tax revenue. Imposing regulations may be expensive to enforce. Selling profitable government-owned firms to the private sector (privatisation) will reduce government revenue. These measures may result in an increase in the government budget deficit in the short run.
