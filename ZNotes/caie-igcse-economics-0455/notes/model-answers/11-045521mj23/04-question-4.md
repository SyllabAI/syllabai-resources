**4(d)** A country’s foreign exchange rate is the price of its currency in terms of another. For example 1 USD = ₹80.

* A rise in a country’s foreign exchange rate would decrease the price of imports because the price of its currency in the terms of another currency increases. For example, if the exchange rate increases from 1 USD = ₹80 to 1 USD = ₹90, people in the US (paying in USD) would be able to pay less. If a good is ₹720, previously they would have to pay 720/80 = 9 USD but now they would only have to pay 720/90 = 8 USD. This means that the price of imports decreases and they become more affordable. The country can now buy more imports with their revenue from exports. Imported raw materials would be cheaper, so the cost of production of firms decreases and they can keep prices low while still making a profit. Cheaper imports keep inflation low as domestic firms feel pressured to keep prices low, as if domestic firms charge high prices, then customers will buy imports instead because they are relatively cheaper.

* However, if a country’s foreign exchange rate increase the price of exports increases. For example, if the exchange rate increases from 1 USD = ₹80 to 1 USD = ₹90, people in India (paying in ₹) would have to pay more for exports from the US (USD). If they bought a good costing 5 USD, they would previously have to pay ₹80 $\times$ 5 = ₹400, but now for the same good they would have to pay ₹90 $\times$ 5 = ₹450, which is ₹50 more expensive. Demand for exports will decrease as the price of exports increases. So other countries buy less exports from the country, and revenue from exports decreases. With less demand for the country’s products, economic growth may fall. Additionally, a deficit on the current account balance could be created, with import expenditure exceeding export revenue.
