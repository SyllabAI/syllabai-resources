## Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

### **What is Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)?**

* Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) measures **how much the quantity supplied of a good changes** in response to a change in its **price**.

* PES is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

* This tells us **how quickly and easily producers can adjust their output** when prices go up or down.

### **Formula for PES -**

**Change in Quanity Supplied/Change in Price**

Just like with demand, PES is **a ratio**, but it's from the **supplier's perspective**.

### **Why Does PES Matter?**

* **For businesses:** Helps them decide how quickly they can scale up if prices rise.

* **For governments:** Useful when planning taxes or subsidies, they’ll want to know how producers will react.

* **For markets:** Tells us how flexible supply chains are during sudden changes (e.g. COVID lockdowns or war in oil-producing countries).

### **Determinants of PES**

**1) Time Period**

* **Short run**: Supply tends to be more **inelastic** – producers can’t instantly increase output.

* **Long run**: Supply is more **elastic** – firms can invest in capital, hire workers, expand production.

*Example:* You can’t grow wheat overnight, but over years you can buy more land and equipment.

**2) Availability of Factors of Production**

* If it's **easy to get more labour, land or machinery**, supply becomes more **elastic**.

* If resources are **specialized or limited**, supply is **inelastic**.

*Example:* Handmade luxury watches have inelastic supply – not everyone can make them.

**3)** **Spare Capacity**

* If a firm has **unused resources**, it can increase output quickly = **elastic supply**.

* If a firm is already producing at full capacity = **inelastic supply**.

*Example:* A bakery with unused ovens can easily bake more bread.

**4) Stock Levels (Inventories)**

* Firms with **large stocks of finished goods** can supply more quickly = **more elastic**.

* If goods are perishable or stock levels are low = **inelastic**.

*Example:* A clothes retailer with a warehouse of shirts can respond to price rises quickly.

**5)** **Ease and Cost of Switching Production**

* If it's **easy and cheap to switch** from one product to another, supply is **elastic**.

* If switching requires **expensive equipment or retraining**, supply is **inelastic**.

*Example:* A factory that can switch between making phones and tablets has elastic supply.
