## Population

### **What is Population in Economics?**

In economics, **population** refers to the **total number of people living in a country**. It plays a huge role in shaping:

* The **labour force** (people who can work),

* The level of **demand** in the economy,

* The potential for **economic growth**,

* **Government spending** on public services, and

* Overall **living standards**.

### **Key Population Terms (Explained Clearly)**

* **Birth rate** refers to the number of live births per 1,000 people per year in a country. A high birth rate usually leads to population growth.

* **Death rate** is the number of deaths per 1,000 people per year. A falling death rate can lead to population increase if it drops below the birth rate.

* **Natural increase** happens when the birth rate is higher than the death rate, causing the population to grow without considering migration.

* **Natural decrease** is the opposite — it’s when the death rate is higher than the birth rate, causing population to fall.

* **Net migration** is the difference between the number of people entering (immigration) and leaving (emigration) a country. A positive net migration means more people are entering than leaving.

* **Population growth rate** is the percentage change in a country’s population over a period of time, typically a year.

* **Dependency ratio** is the proportion of a country’s population that is not in the labour force — mainly children (under 15) and the elderly (usually over 65) — compared to those who are working and earning.

### **Causes of Population Growth**

* Population growth is often caused by a combination of high birth rates, falling death rates, and migration into the country.

* High birth rates may be due to cultural beliefs, lack of access to contraception, or economic reasons (e.g., families needing children to work).

* Falling death rates are usually linked to improved healthcare, sanitation, and food supply.

* Migration can also boost population growth if more people move into the country than leave.

### **Problems of Rapid Population Growth**

* Rapid population growth can put enormous pressure on a country’s infrastructure and resources, especially in developing countries.

* Cities can become overcrowded, leading to slums and poor living conditions.

* Governments may struggle to provide enough schools, hospitals, and clean water.

* Unemployment may rise if there are more job seekers than available positions. It can also harm the environment through deforestation, pollution, and overuse of natural resources.

* In many cases, GDP may grow but GDP per capita (average income per person) stays low, meaning people don’t actually become better off.

### **Benefits of Population Growth (If Managed Well)**

* When managed properly, population growth can have economic benefits.

* A larger population means a bigger **labour force**, which can boost production and economic output.

* More people also mean a **larger consumer market**, encouraging businesses to expand.

* A **younger population** can be an advantage if they are well educated and healthy — they can drive future innovation and productivity.

* Countries like India are trying to harness this potential by investing in education and skill development.

### **Problems of an Ageing Population**

* In developed countries, the problem is often not rapid growth, but **population ageing**.

* As people live longer and birth rates fall, the proportion of elderly rises. This puts pressure on public services like pensions and healthcare.

* Fewer working-age people are available to support a growing number of dependents, which can lead to labour shortages and slower economic growth.

### P**opulation and the Labour Force**

* The size and structure of a population directly affect the supply of workers.

* A **growing working-age population** can boost productivity, but only if there are enough jobs.

* Too few jobs lead to high unemployment and poverty. On the other hand, **labour shortages** can happen when the population is ageing or shrinking.

* So population growth must be balanced with investment in **education, training, jobs, and infrastructure**.

### **Population Policies (How Governments Respond)**

* Governments use **population policies** to manage these challenges.

* Some countries use **pro-natalist policies** to encourage people to have more children, like offering cash benefits, free childcare, or extended parental leave (e.g., France and Sweden).

* Others use **anti-natalist policies** to reduce birth rates, like China’s former one-child policy.

* Governments also use **migration policies** to either attract skilled workers or reduce strain in overcrowded areas.
