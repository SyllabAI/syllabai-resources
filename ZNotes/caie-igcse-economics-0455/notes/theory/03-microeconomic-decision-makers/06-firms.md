## Firms

### Classification of Firms -

By size, firms are measured by 3 factors:

* The number of employees

* the output

* the capital employed

By sector, firms have 3 sectors in total. The first is the primary sector, which is the extraction of natural resources. The second is the secondary sector, which is responsible for the manufacturing of goods. And the last is the tertiary sector, responsible for providing services. Sectors shift over time (economics can move from primary to tertiary).

In terms of ownership, the private sector’s main motive is profit, whereas the public sector’s main motive is social welfare.

**The legal structure is as follows -**

1) **Sole trader** - One owner, unlimited liability.

2) **Partnership** - 2-20 owners, shared responsibility.

3) **Private Limited** - Limited liability, shares not sold publicly.

4) **Public Limited** - Shares are sold on the stock exchange, operations are large scaled.

### Small Firms -

Small firms exist so for many reasons. The main reasons are listed and explained below in detail -

1) Low barriers to entry - Industries with low barriers to entry have low startup costs. Due to this, firms are naturally drawn towards entering them. Since entering these industries is easy, they have low barriers to entry.

2) Provide specialised goods and services - Small firms frequently focus on personalized customer needs which large firms find too small to address. Due to this, they are able to customize and edit goods according to consumer needs, which makes them specialise in specific goods.

**Advantages** -

* Personalised customer service, which helps build loyalty.

* Flexible, which makes them quick to respond to market changes.

* Low communication costs due to being small, which helps make decisions faster.

**Disadvantages** -

* Limited access to finance, which restricts growth.

* Less bargaining power due to not having a major reputation in markets.

* High risk of failure due to fewer consumers and lesser revenue.

### **Causes & Forms of Growth -**

**Causes of growth of small firms -**

1) Increase profits (main long-term objective)

2) Gain market power - Large firms can influence prices and create barriers to entry for competitors.

3) Achieve economies of scale - As firms grow, they can reduce long-run average costs through bulk buying and specialisation.

4) Improvement in survival chances. This helps them sustain in the market longer and have a better shot at growing.

**Forms of growth of small firms -**

Internal Growth - Internal/Organic growth occurs when a firm expands its own operations, revenue, and capacity using internal resources rather than mergers or acquisitions.

External Growth - External Growth/Inorganic Growth/Integration is a rapid strategy where a firm expands through mergers, acquisitions, or partnerships rather than internal operations.

### Types of integration -

1) **Horizontal** - Firms at same stage. The aim is to reduce competition and improve market share.

2) **Vertical** - Has 2 types under itself: Backward & Forward. Backward vertical integration is when a company merges with suppliers in the backward stage of the supply chain. An example could be a shoe display company merging with a shoe manufacturing company. Forward vertical integration, on the other hand, is when a company merges with suppliers in the forward stage of the supply chain. Like a shoe display company merging with a shoe delivering company.

3) **Conglomerate** - A conglomerate integration is 2 companies merging from completely different industries. An example could be a food delivery company merging with a pen manufacturing company.

### Mergers -

Mergers are two or more firms combining to form one..

**Reasons for mergers -**

1) Reduce competition – By merging, firms eliminate competitors, which can increase market power.
2) Increase market share – A larger firm can dominate a bigger portion of the market.
3) Achieve economies of scale – Larger firms can lower average costs due to bulk buying and specialisation.
4) Access new markets – Firms can expand geographically or into new industries.

**Advantages of mergers -**

* Lower costs due to economies of scale

* Higher profits due to increased market power

* Greater efficiency and resource use

**Disadvantages of mergers -**

* Diseconomies of scale (firm becomes too large to manage efficiently)

* Conflict between management styles

* Job losses due to duplication of roles

### Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

**Economies of Scale -**

Economies of scale are the cost advantages a firm experiences when production increases, causing the average cost per unit to fall.

Types of economies of scale -

(a) Internal economies of scale - arise within a firm as it expands its own size and output. -

1) Technical economies

Use of better and more efficient machinery
Large-scale production reduces cost per unit

2) Managerial economies

Hiring specialised managers improves efficiency

3) Financial economies

Large firms get loans at lower interest rates

4) Marketing economies

Bulk advertising reduces cost per unit

5) Purchasing economies

Buying raw materials in bulk at discounts

(b) External economies of scale - arise when the entire industry grows, benefiting all firms in that industry.
Achieved when the entire industry grows

Examples:

* Better transport and infrastructure.

* A larger pool of skilled workers.

* More specialised suppliers.

* Knowledge spillovers between firms.

**Diseconomies of Scale -**

Diseconomies of scale occur when average cost increases as output increases.

**Causes of diseconomies -**

* Poor communication in large firms

* Slow decision-making due to bureaucracy

* Lack of control over workers

* Worker dissatisfaction and low motivation

### Firms and Production

**Demand for Factors of Production**

Demand for factors of production is a derived demand, meaning it depends on demand for the final good.

* **Factors affecting demand -**

* Demand for product: Higher demand → higher demand for labour/capital

* Productivity of factor: More productive workers → more demand

* Price of factor: Higher wages → lower demand for labour

* Price of substitutes: If machines are cheaper → firms may replace labour

### Labour-Intensive and Capital-Intensive Production

**Labour-Intensive Production -**
Uses more labour than capital

* Used when:

  * Labour is cheap and abundant

  * Advantages:

  * Creates employment

  * Lower initial cost

**Disadvantages**:

* Lower productivity

* Quality may vary

* Capital-Intensive Production -

* Uses more machinery than labour

* Used when:

  * Labour is expensive

  * Technology is available

**Advantages**:

* High productivity

* Consistent quality

* Lower long-term cost

**Disadvantages**:

* High setup cost

* Can lead to unemployment

### Production and Productivity

**Production** -
Total output produced by a firm

**Productivity -**
Output per unit of input (usually per worker)

**Ways to increase productivity -**

* Training and education

* Better technology

* Division of labour

* Improved working conditions

* Motivation (bonuses, incentives)

**Importance of productivity -**

* Lower costs of production

* Higher profits

* More competitive firms

* Economic growth

* Possibility of higher wages
