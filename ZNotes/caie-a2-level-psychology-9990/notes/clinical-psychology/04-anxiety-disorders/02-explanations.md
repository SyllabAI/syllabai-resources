## Explanations

### **Behavioural (Classical Conditioning, Watson, 1920)**

* Phobia may result from classical conditioning, where when a neutral stimulus is paired with a frightening stimulus, future association with the neutral stimulus will produce a fearful conditioned response.
* Watson and Rayner wanted to check whether fear could be learned via classical conditioning and whether it could be transferable to other objects and individuals.
* They used the principles of classical conditioning to create a phobia in a young and healthy 11-month-old infant, ‘Little Albert’.
* Before conditioning, he was shown a range of stimuli: white rat, rabbit, dog, monkey, masks, cotton wool, etc, to which he reacted neutrally.

### Cognitive Explanation (Beck, 1976 / Cognitive Theory of Anxiety)

* Anxiety disorders result from maladaptive thinking patterns, not from external conditioning alone.

* Individuals develop negative and irrational beliefs about threats, danger, and their ability to cope.

* These thought patterns form a cognitive triad of anxiety:

  * 1. Negative view of the world (the world is dangerous)

    2. Negative view of the self (I cannot cope)

    3. Negative view of the future (something bad will happen)

  * People with anxiety often show:

    1. Catastrophizing: expecting the worst possible outcome

    2. Hypervigilance: constant scanning for danger

    3. Overgeneralisation: one bad event means all situations are dangerous

    4. Misinterpretation of bodily sensations (e.g., fast heartbeat = heart attack)

  * These distorted thoughts create a cycle of anxiety:
    Negative thoughts → increased anxiety → avoidance or safety behaviours → more negative thoughts

  * This model explains why patients with anxiety feel overwhelmed in situations that aren’t objectively dangerous.
