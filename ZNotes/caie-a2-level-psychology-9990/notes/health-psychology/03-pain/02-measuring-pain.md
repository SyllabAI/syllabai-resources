## Measuring Pain

### **Self-Report Measures (Clinical interview)**

* This method is the preferred approach to pain measurement
* There are multiple tools used to assess pain reliably, but there is no accepted criterion standard
* Pain intensity, pain quality, and pain location must be considered when discussing a patient’s experience of pain
* **Pain location** is measured by asking patients to identify the
  area of the body where they are experiencing pain
* For those who have difficulties verbalizing the location of pain, such as children, an outline of the body can be used on which the patient marks the location of the pain
* An example of this can be seen in the Pediatric Pain Questionnaire
* **Pain Intensity** can be measured through several different means
  such as psychometric measures, observations or measuring the impact
  of pain (counting the number of painkillers the person is taking)
* **Pain Quality** is measured by self-report measures, which consider the level of unpleasantness of two measures (deep and surface pain) and several descriptions
* The second part of the McGill Pain Questionnaire uses this method of
  measurement

### **Psychometric Measures and Visual Rating Scales (McGill Pain Questionnaire)**

* **Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)**: Usually appears as a 100mm line
  with descriptors at either end
* Scores can be measured by recording the distance from the starting point to the mark
* This allows scores to be compared over time

* As pain is subjective and internal experience measurement is usually
  carried using patient self-reports such as the **McGill Pain
  Questionnaire (Melzack, 1975)**
  * **The McGill Pain Questionnaire** is the first proper
    self-report pain-measuring instrument
  * Patients are asked to tick the word in each of the 20 sub-classes that best describe their pain
  * Based on this, a **Pain Rating Index (PRI)** is calculated
  * Patients are also asked to indicate the location of pain on a
    body chart

### **Behavioural and Observational Methods (UAB Pain Behavior Scale)**

* Observations of the patient’s behaviour when he experiences pain
* Behaviorists claim that people can behave in certain ways when they
  are in pain

<!-- end list -->

* Sample behaviours can include:
  * Complaints
  * Gestures
  * Postures
* As a result, they developed pain behavioural scales, such as the UAB Pain Behavior Scale.
  * Tracks the severity of chronic pain over time
  * Ten types of questions note certain behaviours, and each item is rated on a three-point scale (0, 0.5, 1)
  * The higher the score, the more marked the pain-associated behaviour and the greater the level of impairment.
  * Patients can get a score of between 0 and 10
