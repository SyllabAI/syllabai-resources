# Pain

## Types and Theories of Pain

### **Acute and Chronic Organic Pain**

* **Acute pain** begins suddenly and is usually sharp in quality
* This can be a warning of disease or a threat to the body

<!-- end list -->

* **Acute pain can be caused by many events, including:**
  * Surgery
  * Broken bones
  * Dental work
  * Burns or cuts
  * Labour or childbirth

<!-- end list -->

* They can either be temporary and not severe or severe and can last
  for weeks
* **Chronic pain** persists regardless of the healing of the injury
* Pain signals remain active for a long time
* Physical effects include tense muscles, limited mobility, a lack of energy, and changes in appetite
* Emotional effects include anxiety, anger, fear of re-injury, and depression
* These can interfere with a person’s life

<!-- end list -->

* Common chronic complaints include:
  * Cancer pain
  * Headaches
  * Low back pain
  * Arthritis pain
  * Psychogenic pain (pain not due to past injury)
  * Neurogenic pain (pain due to nerve damage)

<!-- end list -->

* Chronic pain can be caused by trauma, injury or infection.

### **Specificity Theory (Descartes)**

* The body has a sensory system for perceiving pain
* It contains its own special receptors for detecting pain stimuli and
  its own area of the brain
* When a noxious event stimulates pain, a signal travels to the centre of the brain, where the pain is perceived

### **Gate Control Theory (Melzack, 1965)**

* Suggests that pain is a combination of sensory experience and
  psychological gates that can increase or decrease the perception of
  pain
* Argues that the nerve impulses that produce pain pass through a series of gates on their way to the brain
* These gates are influenced by the messages descending from the brain
  and other information

### **Psychogenic Pain (Phantom Limb Pain)**

* Caused, increased or prolonged by mental, emotional or behavioural factor
* Caused by psychological pain
* Headaches, back pain and stomach pain are the most common types of psychogenic pain
* **Phantom limb pain** refers to ongoing painful sensations that come
  from a removed limb
* After amputation, the amputee may continue to have awareness that the removed limb is still part of them and they experience sensations linked to it
* These sensations are also present when children are born without one of their limbs, suggesting that phantom limb pain is ‘hard-wired’

<!-- end list -->

* **Melzack (1992)** reviewed evidence on phantom limbs and noted several remarkable features:
  * Phantom limbs have a vivid sensory quality and precise location
    in space
  * Phantoms have a wide range of sensations, such as warmth and itchiness
  * A phantom arm would hang down when the person sits or stands but
    moves in coordination with other limbs
  * Sometimes, a limb gets stuck in an unusual position
  * Patients perceive phantoms as an integral part of their body
  * Phantom is also experienced by some people with spinal injuries
  * Wearing artificial arms or legs enhances the phantom

<!-- end list -->

* It occurs in 90% of amputees, with itching as the most common pain
