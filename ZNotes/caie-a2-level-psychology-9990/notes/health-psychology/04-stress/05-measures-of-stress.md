## Measures of Stress

### **Physiological Measures (Wang et al., 2005; Evans and Wener, 2007)**

* Functional Magnetic Resonance imaging (fMRI) is a neuroimaging
  procedure using MRI technology to measure brain activity by using
  MRI technology to identify changes in blood flow
* In the simplest fMRI study, a participant alternates between
  completing a specific task and control or rest state to measure
  baseline activity
* fMRI data is then analysed to identify brain areas in which the the signal changed between activity and rest state

**Wang et al. (2005)**

* used an fMRI scanner and a technique called arterial spin-labelling perfusion MRI

* **Arterial spin-labelling perfusion MRI**: arterial blood is labelled and, after a delay, moves the imaging plane during which time there is decay of the label. Snapshot images are acquired in labelled and control conditions and subtracted

* In Wang et al.’s research, 32 participants were split into a stress condition (n = 25) and control condition (n = 7)
* Participants were asked to perform a mental task, responding
  verbally while in the fMRI scanner
* Throughout, participants were prompted for faster performance and
  were required to restart if an error occurred
* The high-stress condition was preceded by the low-stress condition where participants counted aloud backwards from 1000 (to control activation of verbal and auditory centres)
* Self-reports of stress and anxiety levels (1-9 scale) and saliva
  samples were collected straight after each task
* Regression analyses were carried out to search for the specific
  brain regions associated with individual participants’ experience of
  stress
* The results provided neuroimaging evidence that psychological stress induces negative emotion, and the ventral right prefrontal cortex plays a key role in stress response
* They also suggested that psychological scans can be used as a
  measure of stress
* Cortisol is released in response to fear or stress by the adrenal
  glands as part of the ‘fight or flight’ mechanism
* Measuring salivary cortisol is often used as a biomarker in
  psychological stress and is a technique preferred by researchers as
  a non-invasive method of measuring stress
* In addition, saliva collection does not require the collaboration
  with skilled personnel, allowing trouble-free sample collection
* However, one issue with measuring salivary cortisol is that stress salivary cortisol measures can indirectly assess mechanisms

**Evans and Wener (2007)**

* conducted research looking at how frequently personal space can be intruded upon and how this can be one of the key underlying factors that can underlie stress when travelling

* A total of 139 adult commuters (54% male) who are commuting from New Jersey to Manhattan, New York City, were recruited

* Each participant was provided with a free monthly rail pass for their participation

* Participants had been on the same commuting route for an average of
  82 months, with a 12-month minimum

* Their average duration of the commute to work was 83 minutes

* Two measures of crowding were taken for each journey:

  * **Car density** – is measured by dividing the total number of passengers within the train car by the total amount of seats
  * **Seat density** – a more proximal measure of crowding to the participant’s location and was calculated by dividing the number of people sitting on the same row with 5 (number of seats in the row)

* Salivary cortisol was collected through a chewable swab

* They found that the density of the train car was inconsequential for levels of stress, whereas the seating density near the passenger affected self-reported stress and cortisol levels in the participant’s saliva

### **Psychological Measures (Self-reports) (Holmes and Rahe, 1967; Friedman and Rosenman, 1974)**

**Holmes and Rahe (1967**)

* investigated the causes and sources of
  stress and focused on life events as stressors

* The scale consisted of 43 life events each had a stress score

* Respondents would add up the scores that they had experienced
  within the last 12 to 24 months to obtain their score

**Rahe et al. (1970)**

* Tested the SRSS’ reliability
* **2500 US Military sailors** were asked to rank stressful events
  in their lives with the scale
* The sailors were tracked for six months, noting their visits to
  the dispensary to identify a correlation between their visits to
  the doctor and the stressful events that they reported
* Rahe et al. proved the reliability of the scale with the exact same positive correlation between reported stress and illness
* It is also possible to categorise individuals under Type A or Type B
  personalities (Friedman and Rosenman, 1974)
* Classifying these behaviors is usually done through interviews or questionnaires
* Example questions include:
  * ‘’Has your partner or friend ever told you that you eat too
    fast?”
    * Type As are likely to say “Yes, often”.
    * Type Bs are likely to say “Yes, once or twice” or “No”
* The idea that people can be categorised is desirable to doctors as it allows them to predict their patients’ behaviour based on the results of a personality test
* Such a simplistic approach can be criticised for trying to explain human personality in such basic terms
* A major issue with Type A and B theory is determining which factors
  are influencing stress and CHD
* Some research focused on hostility, arguing that Type A personalities are characterised by an underlying hostility

### **Evaluation:**

* **Self-reports**
  * Can be prone to **social desirability** as patients can
    sometimes lie about their responses
* **Physiological measures**: considered as **objective** measures
  to stress.
  * This can be achieved by looking at brain activity, or levels
    of hormones produced
  * However, it is **time-consuming and expensive**

### **Issues and Debates:**

* **Psychometric Tests**: allows practitioners to better
  understand the person’s feelings and perceptions of anxiety
* **Application to everyday life**: measures of stress are useful
  in both medical and home settings. If we can identify potential
  triggers of stress, we can collaborate with practitioners early
  enough to prevent any future instances of stress/illness
