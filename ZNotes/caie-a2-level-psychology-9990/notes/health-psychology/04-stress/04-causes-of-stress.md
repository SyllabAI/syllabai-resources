## Causes of Stress

### **Work (Chandola et al., 2008)**

* Investigated which biological and behavioural factors link work stress to coronary heart disease (CHD)
* **10,308 civil servants** from **Whitehall, London,** were recruited in this study
* Data was gathered through questionnaires and clinical assessments
* Participants were classified as being under work stress if the report job strain felt job control was low and felt socially isolated at work

<!-- end list -->

* Various follow-up measures were taken, including:
  * Fatal and non-fatal CHD episodes
  * Biological risk factors (e.g. morning rise in cortisol, blood
    pressure, waist circumference, etc.)
  * Behavioural risk factors (e.g. alcohol, smoking, activity, diet)

<!-- end list -->

* Results indicate that cumulative work stress is a high-risk factor for developing CHD, especially in the younger, working-aged population
* 32% of the effect of work stress on CHD can be explained by the effect of work stress on health behaviours such as low physical activity and poor diet
* Chronic work stress was associated with CHD, and this association is stronger among participants under 50.

### **Life Events (Holmes and Rahe, 1967)**

* Investigated what makes a life event stressful
* Used case histories and interviews to study a large number of people who suffered extreme stress
* Developed a social readjustment scale from these case material
* Using 394 participants, researchers were able to rank 43 events on
  their scale
* The events ranged from most stressful (death of spouse, n = 100) to
  least stressful (minor violations in law, n = 11)

### **Personality (Friedman and Rosenman, 1974)**

* Believes that stress comes from the individual’s personality
* Type A and B models are used to explain this theory
* These models were developed to explain why some people are prone to
  CHD
* People who seemed to be particularly susceptible to CHD tended to
  have certain similar personalities
* As a result, the Type A and Type B personalities are developed


|**TYPE OF INDIVIDUAL**|**CHARACTERISTICS**|
|:---|:---|
|**TYPE A**|Competitive|
||Works fast|
||Strong desire to succeed|
||Likes control|
||Prone to stress|
|**TYPE B**|Non-competitive|
||Works slowly|
||Less desire to succeed|
||Does not enjoy control|
||Less prone to stress|

* In a **longitudinal study**, 3000 healthy men between age 39 and 59 were assessed to determine their personality type and then followed up for the next nine years
* The men were then split into two groups depending on whether they
  were assessed as Type A or B
* 70% of the 257 men who died were from the Type A group
* Concluded that people who are of Type A are more susceptible to stress because of their behavioural traits
* Type A people are also more likely to suffer from stress-related
  illnesses

### **Evaluation**

* **Work (Chandola et al., 2008):** use of longitudinal research
  allows researchers to see how different sources of stress impact
  individuals
* Chandola et al. give an insight into how a large population of over 10,000 people respond to life events, as well as the impact it had on their risk of developing CHD

### **Issues and Debates**

* **Application to everyday life**
* It is unlikely that an individual explanation of stress will be able to take into account for everyone’s experience of stress following a trigger event
* Thus, it is important that we consider how **reductionist**
  explanations of stress are when trying to explain the sources.
* **Cultural Differences**: different cultural expectations and
  beliefs may have a significant impact on what they perceive as a
  trigger to stress
* **Nature vs Nurture**: suggests that both cause stress biological and environmental factors
* **Reductionism**: sources of stress may vary between
  individuals. For example, Selye’s research claims that stress is
  a result of a depletion in physiological resources
