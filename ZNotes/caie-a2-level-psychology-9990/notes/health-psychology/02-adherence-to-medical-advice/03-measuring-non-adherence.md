## Measuring Non-Adherence

### **Self-Reports (Riekart & Droter, 1999)**

* Self-report measures involve asking the patient or the doctor if the
  patient if he is following their treatment programme
* The validity of self-reports is limited because of the challenges
  associated with gaining information without response bias

### **Riekart and Droter (1999)**

* Investigated the implications of studies using self-report
  measures to investigate adherence to medical treatment for
  adolescents suffering from diabetes
* It was felt that participants who refused to be involved in the
  research might have less adherence than those who took part of
  the study
* The participants were adolescents between the ages of 11 to 18 years old who were living with at least one parent
* Participants were excluded if they were diagnosed with diabetes within the last year, had an additional chronic illness or had evidence of a learning disability
* An interview was conducted after their appointment at the clinic
  while the parents answered a demographic questionnaire
* Both adolescents and parents were given a further questionnaire
  to be completed at home with a self-addressed return envelope
* If the questionnaire was not returned within ten days of the
  interview, the family was called once to remind them
* 80 out of 94 families consented to participate in the study,
  took part in the adherence interview and completed the
  demographic questionnaire
* 52 of these families had at least one family member return to
  the follow-up self-report questionnaires
* Medical records were reviewed for each participant to gain
  information about the number of blood sugar tests taken per day
  as a measure of adherence
* The families were categorised into three groups, participants
  (52), non-returners (28) and non-consenters (14)
* The results showed significant differences in adherence levels
  between study participants and those who did not return their
  self-reports
* The families who returned their questionnaires had adolescents
  with higher adherence interview scores and blood sugar tests
  more than those who did not return their questionnaires

### **Evaluation:**

* Self-reports are a good way to identify adherence to medications
* It is most applicable to both drug-based and physical therapy

### **Objective: Pill Counting (Chung & Naya, 2000)**

* Tested compliance with treatment featuring an oral asthma medication
  using the **MEMS Track Cap**
* 57 patients (32 male) formed the sample
* The sample had a history of asthma
* A 12-week followed an initial screening of two to three weeks treatment period
* Participants were instructed to take the asthma medication twice a
  day
* At the start of treatment, they were given 56 tablets with a one-week supply to spare
* Tablets were dispensed in screw-top bottles fitted with **TrackCap
  medication event monitoring system (MEMS)** device

<!-- end list -->

* The MEMS is composed of two parts:
  * A standard plastic container
  * A lid containing a computer chip that registers the time of
    opening and closure of the container

<!-- end list -->

* Patients were then scheduled to return to the clinic every three
  weeks for four more visits
* During these visits, comparisons of tablet counts and TrackCap
  monitoring
* Each patient was told, *“Take one tablet in the morning and one tablet in the evening, approximately 12 hours apart. Do not take the tablets at mealtimes.”*
* Median adherence was 89%, according to TrackCap events
* On days when patients took two tablets, the mean time between doses was 12 hours and 34 minutes, with tablets taken eight to 16 hours apart on 86% of treatment days
* On tablet counts, median adherence was 92%

### **Biochemical Testing (Roth & Caron, 1978)**

* Considering the relative merits of a variety of methods that can be used to investigate adherence
* Suggests that self-reports from patients are correct when they say
  that they are taking little or no medicine
* Self-reports are overly estimated when patients say that they are
  taking their medicine regularly
* Doctors usually overestimate patient’s intake of medicine
* Reliability in keeping appointments is not a good measure of
  adherence
* States that pill counts provide an indication of self-intake, but it cannot be presumed that the pills were taken
* Medication monitors indicate when, as well as whether, the medication was removed from the container
* Blood and urine testing usually provides a reasonably good indication of the amount of medicine taken
* Clinical responses or side effects can be indications that medicine
  is taken
* Biochemical tests that measure drugs can be used to monitor drugs, and metabolites can be used to monitor plasma concentrations of a number of drugs used in the cardiovascular system
* Biochemical testing can be used to monitor adherence to medical
  treatment and empirical evidence has shown that adherence for
  prescriptions are higher with frequent urine monitoring
* These measures are reliable and objective

### **Repeat Prescriptions (Sherman et al., 2000)**

* Investigated how obtaining a prescription refill history could identify poor adherence to asthma medications
* 116 children with asthma were interviewed by their parents or caregivers on a visit to the clinic
* One of the questions asked them was if measures of estimated adherence on a checklist and a question on where they obtained the medications
* Adherence was operationalised by calculating the number of dosages
  refilled divided by the number of doses prescribed over up to a
  period of 365 days
* The patient’s doctor was asked to make a judgement about their
  adherence by estimating if patients were taking the required dosage
  of their inhaler based on case history and clinical judgement
* Information provided by pharmacies was 92% accurate
* Mean adherence was 72%, 61% and 38% for the three different inhalers
  prescribed to patients in the study
* Doctors were able to identify 21 of 49 patients who refilled less
  than 50% of their dosages
* Three of 11 patients used their inhalers excessively

### **Issues and Debates:**

* **Applications to everyday life:** Understanding the
  effectiveness of such measures of non-adherence can allow us to
  identify the most reliable methods for investigating adherence
* **Validity:** Self-reports can be questioned as patients can
  sometimes be prone to lying about their adherence levels
* Physical measures of adherence are more **objective**, as they use biological characteristics (e.g. levels of the drug in the blood) as a form of measurement
* Riekart and Droter **use children as participants**. This means that their data may be more prone to **demand characteristics** by answering desirably.
  * It is important to question the **ethical concerns** within
    the study, most notably, protection and informed consent

### **Evaluation:**

* Self-reports
  * Self-reports are a **quick** way to identify adherence
    levels.
  * These are applicable to both drug and treatment programmes
  * However, self-reports can be prone to **social
    desirability**
* Pill Counting
  * A reliable method for measuring the number of pills taken per interval
  * However, it does not track what the user does to the drug, whether they consume it or dispose of it
* Biochemical
  * An objective method of measuring adherence
  * However, it can be very costly and time-consuming
  * It may also involve invasive procedures if blood, saliva or urine is needed
* Repeat Prescriptions
  * Using this measure only informs the researcher that the
    pills are collected from the pharmacy
