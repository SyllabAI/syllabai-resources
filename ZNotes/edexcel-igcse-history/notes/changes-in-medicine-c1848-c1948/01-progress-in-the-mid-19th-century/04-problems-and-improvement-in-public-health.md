During the Industrial Revolution, rural to urban migration

increased, leading to overcrowded, low-quality housing in dark, narrow streets. 

Families lived in single rooms with over 50 people

per house, sharing water from a communal pump supplied by polluted rivers for a few hours daily.

Although a vaccine existed for smallpox, few people were

vaccinated. Other deadly diseases like typhoid fever, typhus, and influenza had no prevention methods in Britain. Survivors of thes diseases often succumbed to pneumonia or bronchitis.

The most common and deadly disease in the past was Cholera. It caused severe symptoms like diarrhea, vomiting, and muscle spasms, often leading to death within 24 hours. Approximately 20,000 people died during the epidemic of 1831-1832.

The lack of understanding of diseases was attributed to MPs not feeling the need to get involved. The belief was that miasma spread Cholera, affecting those in unhygienic conditions. Dr. Robert Baker's 1832 report noted muddy streets with limited sewers, contributed to the epidemic.

Edwin Chadwick advocated for improving living conditions of the poor through his report "The Sanitary Conditions of the Labouring Population" in 1842. He proposed using taxes to enhance housing, working conditions, and sanitation by emphasizing clean water,

waste removal, and sewage systems.

The 1848 Public Health Act involved establishing a General Board of Health, enabling towns to appoint medical officers, and appointing three commissioners for the board of health.

The 1848 Public Health Act had limited impact due to its

temporary nature and the short life of the General Board of Health. Although it enabled local authorities to enhance hygiene, however it did not mandate them to take action.

During the 1848-1849 epidemic, over 50,000 people died. Attitudes changed during the second epidemic in 1854 due to high fatality rates.

In 1849, Dr. John Snow linked Cholera to polluted water by investigating a pump on Broad Street. Confirming his theory, he removed the pump handle, reducing Cholera deaths.

Snow's research urged local authorities to improve water safety, but little action was taken to enhance public health until 1858.
