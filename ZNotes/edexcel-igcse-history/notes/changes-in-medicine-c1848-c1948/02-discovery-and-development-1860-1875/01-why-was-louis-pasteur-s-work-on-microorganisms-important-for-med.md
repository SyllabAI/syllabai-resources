In 1854, Louis Pasteur disproved the theory of Spontaneous Generation by showing that microorganisms appeared in a Petri dish left open but could be prevented by heating and sealing the

substance. In 1861 his findings led to the 'Germ Theory' stating that microorganisms do not germinate.

In 1878, Pasteur published "Germ Theory and its Applications to Medicine," facing skepticism from scientists unsure of its medical benefits.

Pasteur's work over 20 years influenced surgery by explaining post-surgery infections, leading to Joseph Lister's antiseptic techniques.

The germ theory explained the connection between hygiene and health, leading to advancements in treatments and vaccines.

In 1846, Dr. Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated at Vienna General Hospital that washing hands with a chlorinated solution before surgery reduced infection-related deaths.
