## The Turning Effect of Forces

### Moments

A force can cause an object to **rotate about a fixed point called a pivot**. The **moment of a force** describes its turning effect.

The size of the moment depends on:

* the **force applied**

* the **perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force**

A larger force or a greater perpendicular distance produces a **greater turning effect**.

$$\text{moment}=\text{force}\times\text{perpendicular distance from pivot}$$

In symbols:

$$M=F\times d$$

**M** = moment in Nm

**F** = force in N

**d** = perpendicular distance from the pivot in m

**Important:** Always use the **perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force** when calculating a moment.

![](https://znotes-static.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/cie/igcse/physics-0625/66807a710ca3711ec7f1dd8122b5d52bad912b4289506c25.png =500x)

**Example**

A force of **40 N** acts perpendicular to a spanner at a distance of **0.25 m** from the pivot. Calculate the moment.

$$M=F\times d$$

$$M=40\times0.25$$

$$M=10\text{ Nm}$$

So the moment is **10 Nm**.

### Centre of Gravity

The **centre of gravity** is the point through which the **entire weight of an object can be considered to act**.

* The weight of an object acts **vertically downwards through its centre of gravity**.

* For a **uniform, symmetrical object**, the centre of gravity is at its geometric centre.

* The position of the centre of gravity can affect an object's **stability**; a lower centre of gravity generally makes an object more stable.

### Principle of Moments

* For an object to be **balanced and not rotate**, the total clockwise moment about the pivot must equal the total anticlockwise moment.

* $$\text{sum of clockwise moments}=\text{sum of anticlockwise moments}$$

* If the clockwise and anticlockwise moments are **not equal**, there is a resultant turning effect and the object rotates in the direction of the greater moment.

**Example**

A child weighing **200 N** sits **1.5 m** from the pivot of a seesaw. A second child sits **1.0 m** from the pivot on the opposite side. Calculate the weight of the second child needed to balance the seesaw.

**1. Apply the principle of moments:**

$$200\times1.5=F\times1.0$$

**2. Rearrange and calculate:**

$$F=\frac{200\times1.5}{1.0}$$

$$F=300\text{ N}$$

Therefore, the second child must have a **weight of 300 N** for the seesaw to balance.

### Support Forces on a Beam

* A **light beam** supported at both ends experiences **upward support forces** at each end. When a heavy object is placed on the beam, its **weight acts downwards**.

Because the beam is balanced:

\\\[ R\_A\+R\_B=W \\\]

$$R_A+R_B=W$$

\\(R\_A\\) = upward force at support A

\\(R\_B\\) = upward force at support B

\\(W\\) = weight of the object

As the object moves along the beam:

* Moving the object **closer to support A** increases \\(R\_A\\) and decreases \\(R\_B\\).

* Moving it **closer to support B** increases \\(R\_B\\) and decreases \\(R\_A\\).

* If the object is exactly in the **middle**, the two support forces are equal.
