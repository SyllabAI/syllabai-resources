## **Momentum**

* **Momentum** describes the quantity of motion an object has. It depends on both the object's **mass** and **velocity**.

* An object with a greater mass or greater velocity has greater momentum.

$$p=mv$$

where:

**p** = momentum in kg m/s

**m** = mass in kg

**v** = velocity in m/s

Momentum is a **vector quantity**, so its **direction is important**.

**Rearranging the Equation**

To calculate mass:

**$$m=\frac{p}{v}$$**

To calculate velocity:

$$v=\frac{p}{m}$$

**Example**

A car of mass **1200 kg** is travelling at **15 m/s**. Calculate its momentum.

**1. Use the equation:**

$$p=mv$$

**2. Substitute:**

$$p=1200\times15$$

$$p=18,000\text{ kg m/s}$$

So the car has a momentum of **18 000 kg m/s**.

### Momentum and Safety Features

During a collision, a passenger's momentum changes rapidly as they are brought to rest.

Safety features such as **seat belts, airbags and crumple zones** increase the **time taken for this change in momentum**.

* For the **same change in momentum**, increasing the collision time **reduces the force** acting on the passenger.

* **Seat belts** stretch slightly, increasing the time taken for the passenger to stop.

* **Airbags** increase stopping time and spread the force over a larger area.

* **Crumple zones** deform during a collision, increasing the time over which the vehicle comes to rest and reducing the force. The specification also specifically suggests investigating how crumple zones reduce forces in collisions.

**Important exam idea:**

> **longer collision time → smaller force → reduced risk of injury**

### Conservation of Momentum

* The **principle of conservation of momentum** states that when objects interact, the **total momentum                                                                                           before the interaction equals the total momentum after**, provided there is **no external resultant force** acting on the system.

$$\text{total momentum before}=\text{total momentum after}$$

For two objects:

$$m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2$$

where **u** represents velocity **before** the collision and **v** represents velocity **after** the collision.

Because momentum is a **vector**, direction matters. Choose one direction as positive; velocity in the opposite direction is taken as **negative**.

**Example**

A **1200 kg** car travelling at **18 m/s** collides with an **800 kg** car travelling in the same direction at **6 m/s**. The cars stick together after the collision. Calculate their velocity immediately after the collision.

**1. Calculate the total momentum before the collision:**

$$p=(1200\times18)+(800\times6)$$

$$p=26,400\text{ kg m/s}$$

**2. After the collision, the cars move together, so their total mass is:**

$$m=1200+800=2000\text{ kg}$$

**3. Use conservation of momentum:**

$$26,400=2000v$$

$$v=\frac{26,400}{2000}=13.2\text{ m/s}$$

**Force and Change in Momentum**

A force acting on an object causes its **momentum to change**. The force depends on:

* how much the momentum changes

* how quickly that change happens

$$F=\frac{\text{change in momentum}}{\text{time taken}}$$

Since momentum is \\(p=mv\\)

$$F=\frac{mv-mu}{t}$$

where:

**F** = force in N

**m** = mass in kg

**u** = initial velocity in m/s

**v** = final velocity in m/s

**t** = time taken in s

**Example**

A **0.15 kg** ball travels towards a wall at **20 m/s**. It rebounds in the opposite direction at **12 m/s**. The collision lasts **0.04 s**. Calculate the force acting on the ball.

Take the original direction of the ball as positive:

$$u=20\text{ m/s},\qquad v=-12\text{ m/s}$$

Because the ball rebounds in the **opposite direction**, its final velocity is negative.

**1. Use the equation:**

$$F=\frac{mv-mu}{t}$$

**2. Substitute:**

$$F=\frac{(0.15\times-12)-(0.15\times20)}{0.04}$$

$$F=\frac{-4.8}{0.04}=-120\text{ N}$$

The force has a **magnitude of 120 N** and acts in the direction **opposite to the ball's initial motion**.

**Newton’s Third Law**

When two objects interact, they exert forces on each other that are:

* **equal in magnitude**

* **opposite in direction**

* **acting on different objects**

In simple terms:

**If object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.**

**Example:** When a person pushes backwards on the ground while walking, the ground pushes forwards on the person with an **equal and opposite force**.

**Important:** Newton’s third-law forces **do not cancel each other out**, because they act on **different objects**.
