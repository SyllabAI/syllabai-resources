## Electric Charge

### Electrical Conductors and Insulators

Materials can be classified as **electrical conductors** or **electrical insulators**, depending on how easily electric charge can flow through them.

**1. Electrical Conductors**

* **Conductors** are materials that allow electric current to flow through them easily.

* Metals are good conductors because they contain **free (delocalised) electrons** that can move through the material.

* Common examples include:

  * **Copper:** Used in electrical wires.

  * **Aluminium:** Used in overhead power cables.

  * **Steel:** Conducts electricity but is less conductive than copper.

**2. Electrical Insulators**

* **Insulators** are materials that do not allow electric current to flow through them easily.

* Their electrons are tightly bound and cannot move freely through the material.

* Common examples include:

  * **Plastic:** Used to cover electrical wires.

  * **Rubber:** Used in protective insulation.

  * **Glass:** Used as an electrical insulator.

  * **Dry wood:** A poor conductor of electricity.

**Example — Electrical Wires**

* The **copper core** allows electric current to flow.

* The **plastic covering** prevents users from touching the live conductor, reducing the risk of electric shock.

**Remember:** Conductors allow current to flow, while insulators resist its flow.

### Producing Positive and Negative Charges

* **Electrostatic charge** is a build-up of electric charge on an object.

* An object is **electrically neutral** when it has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

* When you rub two insulating materials together, **electrons can transfer** from one material to the other.

* Only **electrons move between the materials**. Protons remain in the atoms' nuclei.

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/cf8c0a701a334a76a6d571a9.webp =700x)

**1. Gaining Electrons**

* Electrons have a **negative charge**.

* When an object gains electrons, it has more electrons than protons.

* The object becomes **negatively charged**.

**2. Losing Electrons**

* When an object loses electrons, it has fewer electrons than protons.

* The object becomes **positively charged**.

**Example — Rubbing a Balloon on Hair**

* When a balloon is rubbed against dry hair, electrons can move from the hair to the balloon.

* The balloon **gains electrons** and becomes negatively charged.

* The hair **loses electrons** and becomes positively charged.

* The opposite charges cause the balloon and hair to attract each other.

**Remember:**

* **Gain electrons → Negative charge**

* **Lose electrons → Positive charge**

* Electric charge is **conserved**. It is transferred between objects, not created or destroyed.

### Attraction and Repulsion of Electric Charges

* There are **two types of electric charge**: positive (\+) and negative (−).

* **Like charges repel:** Two positive charges or two negative charges push away from each other.

* **Unlike charges attract:** A positive charge and a negative charge pull towards each other.

* These forces are called **electrostatic forces** and can act without the objects touching.

**Examples**

* Two negatively charged balloons repel each other.

* A positively charged rod and a negatively charged rod attract each other.

**Quick Memory Tip:** Like = Repel \| Unlike = Attract

### Explaining Electrostatic Phenomena

**Electrostatic phenomena** happen when electric charges build up, move or become separated, causing attraction, repulsion or electric discharge.

**1. Charging by Friction**

* When two insulating materials are rubbed together, **electrons transfer** from one material to the other.

* The material that gains electrons becomes **negatively charged**.

* The material that loses electrons becomes **positively charged**.

* Protons do not move between the materials.

**2. Attraction of Neutral Objects**

* A charged object can attract an **electrically neutral object**.

* For example, a negatively charged balloon can stick to a neutral wall.

* The balloon causes the electrons in the wall to shift slightly away from it.

* This leaves the surface nearest the balloon slightly positive.

* The opposite charges attract, causing the balloon to stick to the wall.

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/c4e46e1c183f2f9b1c56df25.webp =700x)

**3. Static Electricity in Hair**

* When hair loses electrons to a balloon, the hair becomes positively charged.

* The strands of hair have the **same charge**, so they repel each other and spread apart.

**Remember:** Electrostatic effects can be explained by the transfer or movement of electrons and the forces between electric charges.

### Dangers of Electrostatic Charges

* **Static electricity** can be dangerous when a large amount of charge builds up on an object.

* If the charge suddenly moves between objects, it can produce an **electric spark**.

* Near flammable substances, a spark can cause a **fire or explosion**.

**Example — Fuelling Aircraft and Tankers**

* Aircraft can become charged due to **friction with the air** during flight.

* Static charge can also build up when fuel moves through pipes.

* If this charge suddenly discharges, it may produce a spark that ignites **flammable fuel vapour**.

* To prevent this:

  * Aircraft and fuel tankers are **earthed** before refuelling.

  * Earthing provides a safe path for excess charge to flow into the ground.

  * Connecting metal parts together (**bonding**) prevents a large potential difference from forming between them.

**Remember:** Static charge → Spark → Risk of fire or explosion.

### Uses of Electrostatic Charges

**1. Photocopiers**

* A rotating **light-sensitive drum** is given an electrostatic charge.

* Light reflected from the white areas of the original document removes the charge from those parts of the drum.

* The areas corresponding to the dark parts of the document remain charged.

* **Toner particles** are attracted to these charged areas, forming the image.

* The toner is transferred onto paper and fixed in place using heat.

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/653b275cb52ad699f7e41931.webp =866x)

**2. Inkjet Printers**

* In some inkjet printers, tiny **ink droplets are given an electric charge**.

* The droplets pass between electrically charged plates.

* **Electrostatic forces** deflect the droplets towards the correct positions on the paper.

* This allows the printer to form words and images accurately.

**3. Electrostatic Paint Spraying**

* Paint droplets are given the same electric charge, causing them to **repel and spread apart**.

* The object being painted is given the opposite charge.

* The paint droplets are attracted to the object's surface.

* This gives a more even coating and reduces paint waste.
