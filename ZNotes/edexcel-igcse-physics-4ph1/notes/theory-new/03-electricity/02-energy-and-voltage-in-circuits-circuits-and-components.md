## Energy and Voltage in Circuits — Circuits and Components

### Series and Parallel Circuits

Electrical components can be connected in **series** or **parallel**, depending on how the circuit needs to work.

**Series Circuits**

* Components are connected in a **single loop**, so current has only one path to follow.

* The **current is the same** through every component.

* The supply voltage is **shared between the components**.

* If one component breaks, the whole circuit stops working.

* Adding more identical lamps in series makes them dimmer because the total resistance increases and the current decreases.

**Parallel Circuits**

* Components are connected in **separate branches**, so current has more than one path to follow.

* The **current splits** between the branches.

* Each branch receives the **same voltage as the supply**.

* If one component breaks, the others can continue working.

* You can switch components on and off independently.

### Choosing Between Series and Parallel Circuits

**Domestic Lighting — Parallel Circuits**

Household lights are connected in parallel because:

* Each light receives the full supply voltage and works at its normal brightness.

* You can switch lights on and off separately.

* If one bulb stops working, the others stay on.

* Adding another light does not reduce the brightness of the others under normal conditions.

**Simple Torch — Series Circuit**

* A simple torch can use a series circuit containing a battery, switch and lamp.

* Only one path for current is needed, so the circuit is easy to connect.

* When the switch is opened, the circuit breaks and the lamp turns off.

**Quick Memory Tip**

* **Series = Single path**

* **Parallel = Paths split**

### Current in a Series Circuit

* In a **series circuit**, current has only one path to follow. This means the **current is the same through every component**.

* The current depends on:

  * The **applied voltage**.

  * The **number of components**.

  * The **nature (resistance) of the components**.

**Electrical Circuit Symbols**

![](https://images.znotes.org/allimages/f994afdb528261855c330aba.webp =800x)

**1. Effect of Applied Voltage**

* Increasing the supply voltage increases the current, as long as the resistance stays constant.

* Decreasing the supply voltage decreases the current.

**2. Effect of the Number of Components**

* Adding more resistive components in series increases the **total resistance**.

* If the supply voltage stays the same, a higher resistance causes a smaller current.

* Removing a resistive component reduces the total resistance and increases the current, provided the circuit remains complete.

**3. Effect of Different Components**

* Different components have different electrical resistances.

* Replacing a resistor with one of **higher resistance** decreases the current at a fixed voltage.

* A **variable resistor** can be used to change the resistance and control the current.

**Example**

* Two identical lamps connected in series carry the same current.

* If another identical lamp is added in series:

  * The total resistance increases.

  * The current decreases.

  * The lamps become dimmer.

**Quick Memory Tip**

At a constant resistance: Higher voltage → Higher current.

At a constant voltage: Higher resistance → Lower current.

### Current–Voltage Characteristics

A **current–voltage (I–V) graph** shows how the current through a component changes when the voltage across it changes.

* **X-axis:** Voltage (V)

* **Y-axis:** Current (I)

* Different components have different I–V graphs because their resistance behaves differently.

**1. Metal Wires**

* As the voltage increases, the current increases.

* At a **constant temperature**, current is directly proportional to voltage.

* This means that doubling the voltage doubles the current.

* The resistance stays constant.

* **I–V graph:** A straight line passing through the origin.

**2. Fixed Resistors**

* As the voltage increases, the current increases in the same proportion, provided the temperature stays constant.

* A fixed resistor obeys **Ohm's Law** when its temperature is constant.

* Its resistance remains constant.

* **I–V graph:** A straight line passing through the origin.

**3. Metal Filament Lamps**

* Increasing the voltage causes more current to flow through the filament.

* The filament becomes hotter as more current flows.

* As its temperature increases, the **resistance increases**.

* Therefore, the current increases more slowly as the voltage increases.

* **I–V graph:** A curve that becomes less steep at higher voltages in either direction.

**4. Diodes**

* A **diode** allows current to flow easily in one direction but blocks it in the opposite direction.

* **Forward direction:** Very little current flows until a certain voltage is reached. After this, the current increases rapidly.

* **Reverse direction:** Almost no current flows because the diode has very high resistance.

* **I–V graph:** Shows a sharp increase in current in the forward direction, with almost no current in reverse.

**Quick Memory Tip**

* **Metal wire / Fixed resistor:** Straight line.

* **Filament lamp:** Curved line.

* **Diode:** One-way current flow.

### Effect of Resistance on Current

* **Resistance** is the opposition to the flow of electric current in a circuit.

* When the **voltage stays constant**:

  * Increasing resistance decreases the current.

  * Decreasing resistance increases the current.

* A **variable resistor** can be used to change the resistance and control the current in a circuit.

**Example — Brightness of a Lamp**

When a variable resistor is connected in series with a lamp:

* **Higher resistance** → Less current → Dimmer lamp.

* **Lower resistance** → More current → Brighter lamp.

**Remember:** At a constant voltage, current decreases as resistance increases.

### Light-Dependent Resistors and Thermistors

**1. Light-Dependent Resistors (LDRs)**

* A **light-dependent resistor (LDR)** is a resistor whose resistance changes with the amount of light falling on it.

* When **light intensity increases**, resistance decreases.

* When **light intensity decreases**, resistance increases.

* In darkness, an LDR has **high resistance**.

* LDRs are used in **automatic streetlights**, which switch on when it becomes dark.

**2. Thermistors**

* A **thermistor** is a resistor whose resistance changes with temperature.

* For a typical **negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor**:

  * When **temperature increases**, resistance decreases.

  * When **temperature decreases**, resistance increases.

* Thermistors are used in **temperature sensors and thermostats** to detect changes in temperature.

**Quick Memory Tip**

* **LDR:** More light → Less resistance.

* **NTC Thermistor:** More heat → Less resistance.

### Lamps and LEDs as Current Indicators

* **Lamps** and **light-emitting diodes (LEDs)** can be used to show whether electric current is flowing through a circuit.

**1. Filament Lamps**

* A filament lamp lights up when current flows through it.

* The current heats the filament until it becomes hot enough to glow.

* A filament lamp works with current flowing in either direction.

**2. Light-Emitting Diodes (LEDs)**

* An **LED** is a diode that gives out light when current flows through it in the forward direction.

* LEDs allow current to flow in **one direction only**.

* If an LED is connected the wrong way around, it will not light up.

* LEDs are commonly used as **indicator lights** in electronic devices to show that they are switched on.

* LEDs usually need a series resistor to prevent excessive current.

**Remember:** Both lamps and LEDs can indicate that current is flowing, but an LED must be connected in the correct direction.
