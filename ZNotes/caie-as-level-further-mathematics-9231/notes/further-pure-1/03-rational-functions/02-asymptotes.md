## Asymptotes

An asymptote is the line a curve approaches but **never meets**, as it **approaches infinity**.

### **Horizontal Asymptote**

A **horizontal** asymptote is an asymptote in the **horizontal direction**. These lines are represented by the equation $y = a$ (where $a$ is a constant).

* If the degree of the numerator **=** degree of the denominator
  * The ratio of the highest degree of $x$ coefficients is the horizontal asymptote.
  * Another way to find a horizontal asymptote is to take the limit till infinity, $\lim_{x \ \rightarrow \ \infty} f(x)$.
* If the degree of the numerator **<** the degree of the denominator
  * The line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote.
* If the degree of the numerator **>** the degree of the denominator
  * The function has no horizontal asymptote.

### **Vertical Asymptote**

A **vertical** asymptote is an asymptote in the **vertical direction**. These lines are represented by the equation **$x = a$** (where $a$ is a constant).

* These can be found by finding the values of $x$ where the denominator is equal to 0
  * If $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote, then $lim_{x \ \rightarrow \ a} \ f(x) = \infty$ (Note that this can be positive or negative infinity)

### **Oblique Asymptote**

An **oblique**, or slant, asymptote is an asymptote in a **linear direction**. These lines are represented by the equation $y = mx + c$ (where $m$ represents the gradient and $c$ represents the $y$-intercept).

* Only occurs when the degree of the numerator **>** the degree of the denominator
* These can be found using long division and can be written as

  $$
  
  \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \text{Quotient} + \frac{\text{Remainder}}{g(x)}
  
  $$

  Where, as $x$ approaches infinity, we get the oblique asymptote as

  $$
  
  y = \text{Quotient}
  
  $$
* Note: The quotient has to be in the form of $y = mx + c$ to be considered an oblique asymptote. Otherwise, if it is $y = a$ (Where a is a constant), this line is a horizontal asymptote.
