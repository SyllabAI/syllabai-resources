## Basic methods: Payback, Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

* **The primary quantitative methods used in investment appraisal are:**
  * payback period
  * accounting (or average) rate of return (ARR).

### The meaning, calculation, and interpretation of the payback method

* If a project costs **2,000,000 dollars** and pays back **500,000 dollars** annually, the payback period is four years, which can then be compared with alternative investments. It is common to refer to "year 0" as when the investment is made, with a negative cash flow shown in brackets (shown in table below)
* The table below shows forecasted annual **net cash flows** and **cumulative cash flows**, which represent the **running total**. The cumulative cash flow becomes positive in year 3, indicating the initial capital cost is paid back during that year. Assuming cash flows are evenly distributed, payback occurs at the end of the fourth month of year 3.
* We know this because, at the end of year 2, **50,000 dollars** is still needed to repay the initial investment. With an expected **150,000 dollars** in year 3, **50,000 dollars** represents one-third of this, equating to four months. Use this formula to find the exact month:
  * $\frac{\text{additional net cash inflow needed}}{\text{annual cash flow in year 3}}$ x 12 months
  * $\frac{\text{50000}}{\text{150000}}$ x 12 = 4 months


|**Year**|**Annual net cash flow ($)**|**Cumulative cash flows ($)**|
|:---|:---|:---|
|0|(500000)|(500000)|
|1|300000|(200000)|
|2|150000|(50000)|
|3|150000|100000|
|4|100000 (including residual value)|200000|

#### **Why is the payback of a project important?**

* **Managers can rank projects by comparing their payback periods and may set a cut-off period, rejecting any project exceeding it (e.g., five years). Reasons for setting a cut-off period include:**
  * If the investment is financed through loan, a longer payback period increases interest cost
  * Even with internal financing, capital has an opportunity cost, so quicker payback means quicker capital for other projects
  * A longer payback period increases uncertainty of the investment, as changes in the external environment over time may impact profitability.
  * Some managers that are risk-averse prefer quicker paybacks to reduce uncertainty
  * Future cash flows have lower real value due to inflation, so the faster the payback, the higher its real value is

#### **Evaluation of the payback method**

* The payback method is often used as a quick check on a project's viability or for comparing projects but is rarely used alone without other appraisal methods.


|Advantages of payback method|Disadvantages of payback method|
|:---|:---|
|Quick and easy to calculate|Does not measure a project’s overall profitability and ignores cash flows after the payback period, so a quick return might not lead to further cash inflows|
|Results are easy to understand|Focusing on short-term returns may lead businesses to reject highly profitable investments that take longer to repay|
|Emphasising quick cash returns allows focus on more accurate short-term profitability forecasts|Does not account for the timing of cash flows within the payback period, a concept further clarified by discounting in later appraisal methods|
|Results help identify or eliminate projects with substantially delayed returns||
|Especially useful for businesses prioritising liquidity over total returns||

### The meaning, calculation, and interpretation of the Accounting rate of return (ARR)

* The **accounting rate of return (ARR)**, or **average rate of return**, helps compare investments.
* For example, if Project A averages an 8% return per year and Project B 12%, choosing between them becomes easier.
* **ARR (%) is measured by this formula:**
  * ARR = $\frac{\text{average annual profit}}{\text{average investment}}$ x 100
* **where, average investment =**
  * $\frac{\text{initial capital cost + residual capital value}}{\text{2}}$
* The table below shows expected net cash flows from investing $8 million in fuel-efficient vehicles. Years 1–3 reflect annual cost savings, and year 4 includes the vehicles' residual value.


|**Year**|**Net Cash Flow (NCF)**|
|:---|:---|
|0|($8 million)|
|1|$3 million|
|2|$3 million|
|3|$3 million|
|4|$3 million (including 2M residual capital value)|

* The table below shows the five stages of calculating ARR


|**Stage**|**Working**|
|:---|:---|
|**1** Add positive net cash flows|= $12M|
|**2** Subtract investment cost|= $12M - 8M = 4M (total profit or total net cash flow)|
|**3** Divide by lifespan|= $4M/4 = 1M (average annual profit)|
|**4** Calculate average investment|= $8M + 2M÷2 = 5M|
|**5** Divide the result in stage 3 by average investment x 100 to calculate ARR%|= $1M ÷ 5M x 100 = 20%|

#### **Why is the ARR of a project important?**

* **This result indicates that the business can expect an average annual return of 20% on the investment, which can be compared to:**
  * ARR on other projects
  * The minimum return the business requires, known as the criterion rate (e.g., if the business requires at least 18%, this project meets the criterion)
  * The loan interest rate, as taking a loan is not worthwhile if ARR is lower than the interest rate

#### **Evaluation of average Rate of Return**

* ARR is a popular measure for appraising projects, however is best considered together with payback results
* The two results allow consideration of both profit and cash flow timing.


|**Advantages of ARR**|**Disadvantages of ARR**|
|:---|:---|
|It includes all cash flows, unlike payback method|It ignores cash flow timing, so two projects with similar ARR may differ in payback speed|
|It emphasises profitability|It includes all cash inflows, including later ones that may be less accurate|
|The result is clear and easy to compare with other projects|It disregards the time value of money since cash flows are not discounted (inflation)|
|It can be quickly evaluated against the business's criterion rate||
