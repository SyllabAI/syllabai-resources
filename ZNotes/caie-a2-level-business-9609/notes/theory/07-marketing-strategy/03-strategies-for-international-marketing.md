## Strategies for international marketing

* Business analysts debate how much international marketing strategies should differ from national ones, but all agree that increased international trade impacts marketing.

### **Globalisation and international cooperation: implications for marketing**

* Globalisation, accelerated by multinational companies and economic collaboration, has led to free-trade agreements that reduce or eliminate trade barriers.

* **Key features include:**

  * Increased international trade due to reduced trade barriers

  * Growth of multinational businesses with greater capital freedom

  * Freer movement of workers between countries

* **World trade has grown in recent years, driven by:**

  * The World Trade Organization (WTO) and free-trade agreements

  * The rise of regional free-trade areas like ASEAN and the EU

* Since 2001, Asian economies, led by China and India, have contributed 40% of global trade expansion. China’s WTO membership played a key role, with exports making up 20% of its GDP by 2020.

* Trade growth averaged 7% per year from 1997–2007 but slowed after the 2008 financial crisis, reaching 2.5% in 2013 and 3.9% in 2018. Trade protectionism may limit future growth.

* Recent import and export increases have come mainly from less economically developed countries.

### **Importance of international marketing for a business**

* International marketing offers businesses growth opportunities, especially with the rise of developing countries (BRICS).

* **Key reasons include:**

  * **Saturated home markets**: Expanding to countries with fewer competitors can boost sales.

  * **Profit opportunities**: Low operating costs in some countries allow higher profit margins in wealthier nations.

  * **Spreading risks**: International markets reduce reliance on domestic economic and legal constraints.

  * **Poor trading conditions at home**: For example, luxury sales in Greece fell during the 2019 crisis, while they grew in China.

  * **Legal differences**: Stricter regulations in some countries can push businesses to target markets with fewer restrictions, like tobacco manufacturers in emerging economies.

* While profitable, the ethics of such strategies may be questioned.

### **International markets: identification, selection and entry**

* Entering international markets is a major strategic decision that requires thorough research.

* **The decision-making process includes four stages:**

  * **Identify potential markets**: Research factors like GDP growth, standard of living, languages, culture, and geography.

  * **Screen markets**: Assess political stability, currency stability, market size, competition, transportation costs, and distribution channels.

  * **Select a market**: Shortlist the best markets, visit each, meet with potential partners, and analyze competitors' success rates.

  * **Enter the market**: Decide on the best marketing strategies to succeed in the new market.

### **International marketing strategies**

* Globalisation reduces national and regional differences in tastes, culture, fashion, and wants.

* Analysts like Theodore Levitt argue the world is increasingly demanding standardised goods, enabling companies to leverage technology for economies of scale by selling the same product globally.

* However, others, such as Susan Douglas and Yoram Wind, note persistent consumer differences across markets.

* While standardisation offers one approach, it can fail.

* An alternative is localisation, where businesses adapt their marketing mix to suit local needs and maintain market differences.

### **Pan-global marketing strategies**

* Truly global corporations minimise costs by treating the world or large regions as a **single market**.

* A **pan-global marketing strategy** uses standardised products, branding, and promotions worldwide.

* Smaller-scale versions include **pan-European or pan-Asian strategies** focused on specific regions.

* Using a uniform marketing strategy across countries offers businesses both advantages and disadvantages.

* Concerns about cultural imperialism and anti-globalisation movements highlight the value of adapting products to meet specific cultural, religious, and consumer needs.

* Multinationals like **McDonald’s, Coca-Cola, and Starbucks** face backlash for one-size-fits-all strategies and are increasingly tailoring their approaches to diverse communities, despite higher costs.

* This shift toward global localization aligns marketing strategies with local preferences.

* Pan-global marketing remains vital for two product groups: upmarket brands like Rolex and Versace, prized for exclusivity and global appeal, and mass-market brands like Levi’s, Apple, and Nike, which benefit from global campaigns and economies of scale.

### **Global localisation strategies**

* This approach to international marketing is often described as "**Thinking global - acting local."**
* **Yum!**, the world’s largest fast-food organisation with brands like **KFC and Pizza Hut**, successfully uses the *"Think global, act local"* approach.
* It offers franchisees the benefits of being part of a giant corporation while customising its marketing mix for different markets.
* **For example:**
  * In China, **Yum!** offers unique products like **KFC Dragon Twisters and crayfish burgers** to suit local tastes.

  * Prices vary by country to reflect income differences.

  * Ads feature local people, and Yum! tests local restaurant concepts, such as Chinese-style quick-service restaurants.
* However, global localisation has its limitations.

### **Choosing the strategy to develop a global market**

* The choice of strategy depends on the differences between the national and target global markets.

* Greater differences call for a localised strategy rather than a pan-global one.

### **Key differences between international markets include:**

* **Economic and social differences**

  * Average living standards vary widely, influencing international marketing decisions.

  * In 2018, Qatar had the highest GDP per capita at **130,475 dollars**, while the Central African Republic had the lowest at **712 dollars**.

  * Cost of living, tax rates, interest rates, and population age structures are key factors.

  * Social differences, like the role of women and marriage's importance, also affect product choices and marketing strategies in foreign markets.

* **Legal differences**

  * **Legal differences worldwide significantly impact international marketing strategies:**

    * Some products, like guns, are legal in the USA but banned elsewhere.

    * Advertising restrictions vary, such as Sweden's ban on ads targeting children under 12.

    * Product safety and labeling standards are stricter in regions like the EU compared to some African countries.

* **Cultural differences**

  * Cultural differences strongly influence consumer behavior and can undermine marketing strategies if overlooked.

  * Language translation errors can lead to unintended meanings in foreign markets.

  * Using male and female models together in ads may be unacceptable in countries with strong religious traditions.

  * Colors hold different meanings, such as white for mourning in the Far East and black in the West.

* **Differences in business practices**

  * Accounting standards and rules differ globally.

  * Setting up a local company for direct marketing varies in ease and cost by country.

  * In the UK, it takes a few days, while in Sierra Leone, it can take over a year due to formalities.

### **International franchising**

* International franchising involves using foreign franchisees to run a firm’s operations abroad.

* This can include a single franchisee managing all outlets in a country, as **McDonald’s** does in Argentina, or appointing individual franchisees for each location.

  The benefits and limitations of franchising, include the added advantage of foreign franchisees’ local market knowledge.

### **Joint ventures**

* An example of a joint venture is **McDonald’s** 50-50 joint venture with **Indian chains Hardcastle Restaurants** and **Connaught Plaza Restaurants.**

### **Licensing**

* Licensing allows a foreign business to produce branded or patented products under controlled quality terms.

* It saves on export time, transport costs, and keeps products fresher, while avoiding capital cost of setting up operations abroad.

* **However, there are limitations:**

  * Potential quality lapses.

  * Unethical production methods by the licensee, damaging the brand.

  * Licensee business failure, reducing production, sales, and income.

### **Direct investment in foreign subsidiaries**

* Merging with or taking over local companies can cause issues due to differences in business culture, structure, and technology.

* Wholly owned subsidiaries are an alternative, often more successful than mergers or takeovers.

* Subsidiaries can include factories *(e.g., Toyota in the EU and South America)* or retail operations *(e.g., Tesco in Thailand and China)*.

* Subsidiaries may be decentralised with local management or centralised with control from the home office.
