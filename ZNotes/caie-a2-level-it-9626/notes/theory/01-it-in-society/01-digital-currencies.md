## Digital Currencies

### Types, Characteristics and Uses of Digital Currencies

* Digital Currency: A type of currency that is only available in digital form, unlike cash or coins. It is an internet-based exchange medium that allows for instantaneous transactions to be made without requiring face-to-face interactions.

* Virtual Currency: All virtual currencies are a type of digital currency, but not all digital currencies are virtual. The main difference is control: digital currencies can be centralised or decentralised, while virtual currencies are typically decentralised and controlled by a private entity.

* Cryptocurrency: Uses cryptography to ensure security when making transactions digitally. Susceptible to fluctuation in value of assets, leading to it being a high-risk activity. Transactions are irreversible and are not accepted by all countries.

* Central Bank Digital Base Money: A form of currency that is set up and monitored by the government and regulated using a set of laws. The value of the currency is *backed.* Unique serial numbers are used to minimise counterfeiting.

  * ‘Backed currency’ refers to money that comes with a guarantee of maintaining its value in the form of physical reserves such as gold or silver.

* Stored-value Cards: Refer to cards that hold a fixed amount of money, which can be used for making purchases at POS (Point-Of-Sale) Terminals and then either discarded or topped up with a certain amount of money.

  * Uses a magnetic chip to store information.

  * May not carry the name of the owner.

  * Used primarily for low-value transactions.

  * Not commonly accepted for online payments.

  * Security issues due to lack of authentication required when using the card.

#### Centralised Systems

* A region's financial operations are regulated and overseen by a centralised banking system. It has authority over the state's foreign exchange, interest rates, reserves, and total money supply.

* Credit Cards: Although they can be issued by different banks, credit card systems are bound by centralised banking systems. They are issued by banks to cardholders. They may have a variety of authentication methods, such as signatures or PINs. This differs from the use of cryptocurrency because the bank loans the money instead of it being debited by the user.

* Debit Cards: Similar technology to credit cards that enables payments to be made without requiring the sufficient funds in physical form, although unlike credit cards, the money is not being borrowed from the bank; instead, the funds need to be topped up in the card by the user, and transactions can’t be made unless sufficient funds are input by the user.

* Electronic Point-Of-Sale: Customers can use credit or debit cards to pay in stores that use EPOS. These are standalone computer programs that do every task an employee might perform to close a deal. Devices like barcode scanners, keypads, touchscreens, and weighing scales that take in information about the goods being sold to the client are used. The amount due is determined and shown to the consumer at the moment of checkout. The customer pays using a contactless card reader or a chip-and-PIN reader.

#### Decentralised Systems

* These currency systems are those that are not controlled by a single organisation.

* Bitcoin: It is a digital currency created through mining. It allows peer-to-peer transactions without a central bank or authority, using cryptography to verify transactions. These transactions are recorded on a public, *distributed ledger* called the blockchain. Bitcoins can be stored in wallets, exchanged for goods or services, or converted into other currencies.

* Litecoin: It is a digital currency based on Bitcoin, functioning in a similar decentralised way. It processes transactions about four times faster and uses a more complex verification method, requiring greater processing power for *mining*.

* Peer-to-peer electronic monetary system: It is a decentralised digital payment system in which there is no central authority, such as a bank, and transactions take place directly amongst users (nodes). Every node has the same public ledger (*blockchain*), which uses cryptography to safely record and validate transactions.

* Mobile electronic wallet: Smartphone software that safely saves credit card and loyalty card information. By keeping the phone close to a payment device, it enables contactless payments using Near Field Communication (NFC). In the event that the phone is lost or stolen, the wallet can be remotely erased or disabled, and data is encrypted and secured with two-step authentication (such as a PIN or fingerprint).

### Distributed Ledger

* A shared digital database spread across multiple computers or locations rather than being stored in one central place. It records and replicates transactions across all participating sites, ensuring transparency, security, and consistency without needing a central authority.

### Blockchain

* A kind of distributed ledger that uses encryption to store transaction records. Each block is secure and difficult to alter since it includes transaction data, a timestamp, and information about the previous block. A copy of the blockchain is owned by each user on the network, and all copies are synced to guarantee accuracy.

* Uses: Asymmetric encryption is used by blockchains in cryptocurrencies to safeguard data. Every user has a private key to access and manage their bitcoin and a public key to receive money. Security and ownership are guaranteed, as only the owner is aware of their private key. All related documents, transactions, and money, however, become permanently inaccessible in case the private key is lost.

### Impact and Risks of Digital Currencies

* Individuals:

  * Allow fast, secure, global money transfers with lower fees than banks.

  * Benefit people in poorer or unstable economies by offering access to digital payments without bank accounts.

  * Transactions cross international borders and are almost instantaneous.

  * Values are very volatile and unregulated, making them a high-risk investment.

  * Transactions are irreversible, so mistakes or fraud can lead to permanent loss.

  * Losing private keys makes funds permanently inaccessible.

  * Malware, hardware, or software failures can destroy or lose wallet data.

  * Transaction data cannot be deleted, creating privacy concerns.

  * Some people prefer physical cash to keep their transactions private.

* Businesses:

  * Make transactions faster, cheaper, and more efficient for businesses, especially across international borders.

  * Smart contracts allow automatic, secure, and trackable enforcement of agreements without third parties.

  * Reduced risk of fraud during money transfers between organisations or countries.

  * More valuable transactions can be automated through digital escrow systems, ensuring payment only goes through after conditions are met.

  * Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) make payments quicker and more reliable, with encoded conditions that prevent unauthorised changes.

  * Unregulated cryptocurrency markets have led to scams and unrealistic investment offers, causing financial losses for businesses and investors.

  * CBDCs could cause bank runs if businesses move large amounts of money quickly, creating instability in traditional banking systems.

  * Overreliance on digital systems increases vulnerability to hacking, cyberattacks, or technical failures.

  * The introduction of CBDCs may weaken commercial banks if businesses prefer holding funds directly with central banks.

* Governments:

  * Digital currencies can threaten a government’s control over its economy because they can be decentralised and unregulated.

  * Growth in digital currency use can reduce monetary stability and weaken the role of central banks.

  * Unregulated expansion of digital money supply can lead to inflation, rapid price rises, and devaluation of national currency.

  * Increased fluctuation in cryptocurrency prices can cause financial instability and loss of investor confidence.

  * Governments risk losing control over money creation, interest rates, and exchange rate policies.

  * Anonymous transactions increase the risk of money laundering, illegal payments, and tax evasion.

  * Loss of customer protection and reduced taxation income may occur if large-scale transactions happen outside regulated systems.

  * Some governments restrict or ban cryptocurrencies to prevent economic instability and currency outflow.

  * Introducing central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) allows governments to maintain regulation and control while offering faster and cheaper transactions.

  * CBDCs can improve tax collection, reduce financial crime, and provide accessible banking services to more citizens.

  * However, CBDCs also raise privacy concerns since all transactions are recorded and traceable, giving governments more financial oversight over individuals.

* Global Economy:

  * Digital currencies make global money transfers faster and cheaper than traditional bank methods.

  * Increased movement of money promotes greater international trade and global commerce.

  * People in developing economies can use digital credits on smartphones without needing physical cash or bank accounts.

  * Safer than carrying physical money, allowing people to carry funds securely.

  * Easier use in e-commerce enables global e-business growth and wider market access.

  * E-businesses in underdeveloped countries can easily trade internationally.

  * Enables use of smart contracts and programmable money, automating international financial transactions.

  * Can be linked to global stock markets, allowing rapid transfers based on share price or sales movements.

  * Currency exists only digitally, making transactions harder to track or regulate.

  * Value can fluctuate unpredictably, affecting global markets and trade stability.

  * Can be exploited for criminal or illegal activities due to anonymity and limited oversight, e.g., money laundering.

  * Loss of access (e.g., file corruption or lost keys) can result in permanent financial loss.
