## Analysis

* The poem is made up of only a single stanza. This represents of both death and life are final, and how they only happen once. Once life ends, it is over forever, much like the single stanza of this poem.
* The poem is entirely an extended metaphor. It is a representation of death, and the legacy which one leaves behind in death.
* The speaker begins by directly addressing the fly, which is an example of apostrophe, which is when a speaker directly addresses or attempts to converse with someone, or something, which does not exist or is not present there.
* The speaker tell the fly that ‘*some hand, that never meant to do thee hurt, has crushed thee here between these pages pent*’. The trivial way in which the speaker says ‘*some hand*’ is indicative of the speaker’s belief that the method of death here was irrelevant, and this is furthered by the speaker saying that it ‘*never meant to do thee hurt*’.
* The word ‘*crush*’ had a negative and brutal connotation, and its usage is representative of the harshness of the fly’s death. 
* The brutal wording here is accompanied with a tone of pity, as the fly seems to have suffered even in its death; this can be seen as related to religion, where martyrs are often held in high regard and respected.
* The usage of the word ‘*pent*’ shows how the fly is trapped in the pages, and how it cannot leave.
* Here, the ‘*pages*’ are an extended metaphor for history, as throughout humanity’s existence, writing has been a consistent form of recording the past and its events.
* The speaker says that the fly has left a ‘*fair monument*’ in the forms of its ‘*wings*’ which ‘*gleam out and tell me what thou wert*’. This continues on with the extended metaphor of the pages being history.
* The ‘*monument*’ and ‘*wings*’ here are a metaphor for one’s legacy, and this is strengthened by the fact that the wings tell the speaker what the fly was. This shows how the legacy one leaves behind is representative of who one is as a person.
* The first four lines showcase a tone of regret and mourning from the speaker. This is similar to how humans are often mourned and missed at first, regardless of what their legacy and history was. It is used while the speaker observes and contemplates upon the fly’s life, and recognizes what the fly has left behind, even appreciating its corpse.
* The speaker expresses their wish to be like the fly. They say that they wish that ‘*the memories*’ which ‘*survive*’ them are ‘*half as lovely as these wings of thine*’. This once again furthers the theme of death and legacy that is prevalent in this poem, and reiterates the metaphor of the wings being compared to the fly’s legacy.
* The speaker is attempting to say that our true legacy is the memory of us which others hold in their minds.
* The fly’s wings, something which would typically be overlooked due to their minuscule nature, are being played up and described by beautiful in the poem. This shows both the speaker’s attention to detail, and how one’s legacy is often different from what one would expect it to be.
* The speaker continues by calling the wings ‘*pure relics of a blameless life*’. The words ‘*pure*’ and ‘*blameless*’ both create an image that the fly lived a perfect and pure life, without having ever done anything wrong.
* The speaker here is saying that the way for one to leave behind a legacy as beautiful and prominent as the fly’s is to live such a ‘*pure*’ and ‘*blameless*’ life.
* The word ‘*relics*’ shows how the speaker finds these wings to be much like artifacts. The word ‘*relics*’ is often used in a historical/archeological context, and its use reiterates the theme of legacy and history.
* The speaker, in lines 5-7, shows a sense of admiration for the fly’s life once again, this time in more detail. It focuses on its ‘*blameless*’ life, and the tone used here shows one of awe, similar to how humans speak good of the dead at first as a form of respect.
* The speaker simply states, ‘*thou art gone*’, and the plain way in which this is stated shows how death is final.
* The speaker states that ‘*our death is ever near*’, and the extremely simple way in which they state this shows the inevitability of death.
* They state that ‘*the book will close upon us, it may be*’, which is a euphemistic and metaphoric reference to death, and to the ‘*pages*’ of history.
* The lines 8-11 depict a tone of a sort of impending doom from the speaker, where he reflects on the inevitability of death. 
* There is some religious allegory used in line 10 as well; the word ‘book’ could refer to those of religions \[ex. the Quran, Bible, Torah, etc.\] as religions often give their followers an idea of what death is like, and often, how it is unavoidable and a part of fate. The tone used here is contemplative, yet negative as the speaker begins to address the plight of mankind.
* The speaker states that unlike the fly, the ‘*closing book may stop our vital beach, yet leave no lustre on our page of death*’. This once again uses a euphemism to describe death, but follows it up with directly mentioning it in the very same sentence.
* The last three lines show a resigned tone from the speaker: they criticize mankind for their rejection of leading a good life, comparing it to how they wouldn’t leave any admirable legacy behind, like the fly did.
* The poem ends with the conclusion that not everyone is like the fly, and not everyone lives the same ‘*blameless*’ and ‘*pure*’ life. It concludes that for many people, when they die, they will not leave much of a legacy behind.
