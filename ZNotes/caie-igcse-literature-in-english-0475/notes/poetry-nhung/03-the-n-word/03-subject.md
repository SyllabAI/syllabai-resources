## Subject

* ### **Personification:**

  * “You sly devil.” The speaker directly addresses the N-word as though it were a person with its own personality. The adjective “sly” suggests that the word is deceptive, able to enter conversations without immediately revealing its harmful associations. Meanwhile, “devil” carries connotations of evil and temptation, presenting the word as morally dangerous and capable of influencing people's behaviour.

  * The personification suggests that racist language possesses a power beyond its literal meaning. Like a deceptive character, the word can disguise its harmful history beneath humour, familiarity or casual conversation, exposing how racism can become normalised in everyday life.

* ### **Semantic field of speech and language:**

  * “script”, “conversation”, “signifyin(g)”, “sign.” These references establish language as the central concern of the stanza. However, the word is presented as more than a means of communication: its meaning changes depending on the context in which it is used.$\rightarrow$ suggests that social relationships and historical associations shape language. Chingonyi allows readers to question whether a word can ever be separated from the prejudice and power structures that gave it meaning.

* ### **Contrast between politeness and racism:**

  * “polite conversation”. “polite” suggests socially acceptable behaviour, respect and restraint. However, the word appears alongside the idea of something being “smuggled” into conversation, creating a contrast between society's expectations of politeness and the continued presence of racist language $\rightarrow$ suggesting that racism does not always appear through openly aggressive behaviour. It can also survive beneath ordinary social interactions, revealing the difference between appearing respectful and genuinely challenging prejudice.
