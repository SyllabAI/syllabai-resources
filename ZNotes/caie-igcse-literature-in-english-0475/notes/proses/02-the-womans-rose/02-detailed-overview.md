## Detailed Overview

* The prose starts with the writer explaining a small brown box that she owns. Inside the box is a dry rose, which she has held on to since age fifteen. However, the acacia flowers she once had was discarded. This rose is not merely a rose but a powerful reminder of an experience from her early years and of a relationship that defined women's friendship to her, the rose also offers a sense of hope that someone will have her back in this judgmental and superficial society.

* The narrator recalls being sent to stay in a distant up-country town when she was fifteen. There, she encountered another girl, roughly the same age, and she was drawn to her straight away. The narrator is descriptive about her, noting her beauty, her grace, and how men looked at her. This immediately brings tension between the two girls—a natural, almost silent rivalry that emerged when they interacted with the opposite gender. This also highlights the themes of admiration and appearance, and how the men in town value only appearance and youth.

* Although the two girls never admitted  to love each other, the narrator guarantees us that they never hated each other too. This contradiction accounts for the development of the themes  of female relationships, admiration, jealousy, rivalry, and curiosity.

* The townsmen, upon sighting both girls, saw them in comparison, which amplified the competition. Yet, beneath the competition, the narrator senses a deeper bond between her and the girl. They saw something in one another: an implicit understanding of what it meant to be young women in a society where their worth generally came to be based on looks and male approval.

* The rose becomes the master symbol of the story. The Rose was a wordless mark of comprehension between the two girls. Unlike men's fleeting attention, this token meant something more significant, something beyond the ability to be expressed. This further highlights why the writer threw away her acacia flowers from her box, as it didn’t mean as much to her as the rose did.

* Further, the act of the girl giving the writer the rose to raise her, even after the writer was the reason the men had rejected her, symbolizes the dominant motifs of sisterhood and highlights the themes of hope even in patriarchal society and expectation, and evokes the themes of feminism.

* After she departed from the town, the girl was not seen by the narrator ever again. But the rose was always a reminder of their special connection. She stored it in the small box, preserved well years later, with the intensity of that fleeting but not to be forgotten experience.

* The author ends on a deeply reflective tone, where the narrator identifies with the extent to which the experience had affected her. The rose, dehydrated and wilted as it stands, remains a metaphor for how sisterhood always prevails and highlights the contradiction of women's relations: competition and togetherness, jealousy and awe, aloofness and intimacy.
