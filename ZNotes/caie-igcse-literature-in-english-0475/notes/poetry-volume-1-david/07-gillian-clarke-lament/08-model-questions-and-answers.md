## Model Questions and Answers

### Question 1: How does Clarke present the effects of war in *Lament*?

**Model answer**

Clarke presents war as a destructive force that affects both humanity and the natural world. Through the repeated phrase “For the”, she lists different victims, including turtles, birds, marine animals, soldiers and civilians. This accumulation emphasises the widespread consequences of conflict and creates a solemn, memorial-like rhythm.

The metaphor “her eggs laid in their nest of sickness” suggests that pollution has contaminated even the places where new life should develop. The word “sickness” conveys the unnatural condition of the environment and implies that war threatens future generations of wildlife. Similarly, “the ocean’s lap with its mortal stain” presents the sea as a nurturing figure that has been fatally damaged.

Clarke also draws attention to human suffering through the reference to “Ahmed at the closed border”. This places the experience of a civilian alongside the suffering of animals, showing that the consequences of war extend beyond the battlefield. Ultimately, Clarke portrays war as an environmental and humanitarian catastrophe rather than simply a military conflict.

### Question 2: How does Clarke use language and imagery to convey suffering in the poem?

**Model answer**

Clarke uses emotive language, metaphor and vivid imagery to communicate the suffering caused by war. The phrase “the cormorant in his funeral silk” compares the bird to a figure dressed for a funeral. The association with death establishes the poem's elegiac tone and presents wildlife as deserving of mourning.

The image of “the whale truck dumb by the missile’s thunder” contrasts the silence of the whale with the overwhelming noise of military weapons. This contrast suggests that the natural world is powerless against the destructive force of warfare. The reference to “the missile’s thunder” also evokes the violence and scale of modern weaponry.

Furthermore, “the long migrations and the slow dying” contrasts the endurance of migrating animals with their gradual destruction. The phrase suggests prolonged suffering and the loss of natural processes. Through these images, Clarke encourages readers to recognise the devastating consequences of war for vulnerable living things.

### Question 3: How does Clarke use structure to develop the poem's meaning?

**Model answer**

Clarke uses repetition and accumulation to emphasise the widespread consequences of war. The repeated opening “For the” introduces successive victims and gives the poem the rhythm of a lament or prayer. This repetition creates a sense that each victim deserves recognition and mourning.

The poem's lists move between wildlife, human beings and damaged landscapes. The reference to “Ahmed at the closed border” introduces a human dimension to the poem, connecting the suffering of civilians with the destruction of nature. This movement suggests that environmental and humanitarian consequences cannot be separated.

Towards the end, the imagery becomes increasingly bleak, culminating in “For vengeance, and the ashes of language”. The reference to vengeance suggests that hatred perpetuates suffering, while “the ashes of language” implies that meaningful communication has been destroyed. The ending therefore expands the poem's criticism beyond physical destruction to include the moral and emotional consequences of conflict.
