## **Question 1**

### How does Shakespeare present the beloved's beauty?

Shakespeare presents the beloved's beauty as **superior to the beauty of nature**. The speaker begins by asking **"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"**, immediately establishing summer as a symbol of beauty. However, he quickly concludes that the beloved is **"more lovely and more temperate."** The adjective **"more"** establishes superiority, suggesting that even the attractive qualities of summer cannot match the beloved.

Shakespeare then emphasises the imperfections of summer. **"Rough winds"** can damage the **"darling buds of May"**, while summer itself has **"all too short a date."** This makes natural beauty appear fragile and temporary.

In contrast, the speaker claims that **"thy eternal summer shall not fade."** The metaphorical **"eternal summer"** suggests that the beloved's beauty will remain permanently through poetry.

Overall, Shakespeare presents the beloved as possessing a beauty that **surpasses nature and can ultimately escape the effects of time**.

## **Question 2**

### How does Shakespeare explore the theme of time?

Shakespeare presents time as a force that causes beauty to disappear. Summer is described as having **"all too short a date"**, using the metaphor of a **"lease"** to suggest that its beauty has an inevitable expiry point.

The poet expands this idea by stating that **"every fair from fair sometime declines."** The repetition of **"fair"** emphasises that all beautiful things eventually experience change and decay. This makes the effects of time seem universal.

However, the volta **"But thy eternal summer shall not fade"** introduces a contrast. The adjective **"eternal"** directly opposes the earlier description of summer as temporary. Shakespeare claims that the beloved will escape time through **"eternal lines"**, referring to the poem itself.

Therefore, Shakespeare presents time as powerful over **physical beauty**, but ultimately powerless against the beauty preserved through poetry.
