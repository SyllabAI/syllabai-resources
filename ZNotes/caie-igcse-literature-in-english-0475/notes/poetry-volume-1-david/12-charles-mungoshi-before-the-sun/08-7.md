## Model Questions & Answers

### Q1: How does Mungoshi make *Before the Sun* such an enjoyable and memorable poem?

### Model Answer

Mungoshi makes *Before the Sun* enjoyable and memorable through his vivid descriptions, youthful narrator, and celebration of ordinary experiences. Although the poem focuses on a simple morning spent roasting maize, the poet transforms this everyday event into something meaningful and uplifting.

One way Mungoshi engages the reader is through his presentation of the speaker's youthful enthusiasm. The repeated reference to being fourteen years old emphasises the energy and confidence associated with adolescence. The speaker declares that "big logs are what you want," suggesting that young people actively seek challenges rather than avoid them. This creates an optimistic tone and allows readers to admire the speaker's determination.

The poet also uses rich sensory imagery to bring the scene to life. Descriptions of the "intense blue morning," the smell of the wood, and the sparks flying from the axe appeal to the senses and make the setting feel vivid and realistic. Readers can easily imagine themselves alongside the speaker, experiencing the freshness of the morning.

Another memorable feature of the poem is the personification of the sun. The speaker imagines the sun as "some latecomer to a feast" and later says that it "winks like a grown-up." These playful images reveal the speaker's imagination and create a friendly relationship between humanity and nature. The sun becomes more than a natural object; it becomes a companion in the speaker's morning ritual.

Finally, beneath its cheerful surface, the poem contains deeper reflections on time and life. The image of the maize cobs becoming "little skeletons" introduces a subtle reminder that all experiences eventually come to an end. This contrast between youthful joy and the passage of time adds depth to the poem.

Overall, Mungoshi makes the poem memorable through vivid imagery, humour, personification, and thoughtful themes about youth, nature, and growing up.

### Q2: Explore how Mungoshi presents the relationship between humans and nature in *Before the Sun*.

In *Before the Sun*, Mungoshi presents nature as a source of joy, companionship, and spiritual meaning. Throughout the poem, the speaker demonstrates a close connection with the natural world, suggesting that humans and nature exist in harmony rather than conflict.

From the opening lines, the speaker is surrounded by natural beauty. The descriptions of the "intense blue morning" and "dewy grass" create a peaceful atmosphere and immediately establish nature as an important part of the poem. Rather than observing nature from a distance, the speaker is fully immersed within it.

The activities he performs are also closely linked to the environment. Chopping wood, making a fire, and roasting maize all depend on natural resources. However, these actions are not presented as exploitation of nature. Instead, they demonstrate a respectful and balanced relationship between humans and their surroundings.

Mungoshi further develops this connection through personification. The sun is described as "some latecomer to a feast" and later "winks like a grown-up." By giving the sun human qualities, the poet transforms it into a companion. The speaker even pretends to share his maize with it, creating a sense of friendship between himself and the natural world.

The poem also contains spiritual elements. The smoke is described as "a sacrificial prayer," suggesting that nature can serve as a bridge between the physical and spiritual worlds. Ordinary actions therefore acquire deeper significance.

Through imagery, personification, and symbolism, Mungoshi presents nature as something beautiful, comforting, and deeply connected to human life. The poem ultimately suggests that happiness comes from appreciating and living in harmony with the natural world.
