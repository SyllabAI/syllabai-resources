## Model Essay 1

## **How does Katherine Philips present marriage in *A Married State*?**

Katherine Philips presents marriage as a difficult and demanding institution that places heavy emotional, physical, and domestic burdens on women. Rather than portraying marriage as a source of happiness and fulfilment, she challenges the traditional belief that it is the ideal life for every woman. Through vivid imagery, direct advice, and strong contrasts, Philips encourages women to think carefully before entering marriage.

The poem opens with the striking statement, **"A married state affords but little ease."** This immediately establishes the speaker's critical attitude towards marriage. The phrase **"little ease"** suggests that married life is filled with hardship instead of comfort. Philips strengthens this idea by claiming that **"The best of husbands are so hard to please."** The hyperbole implies that even kind husbands place great expectations on their wives, highlighting the unequal responsibilities women face in marriage.

Philips also suggests that many wives hide their unhappiness. She writes that women **"dissemble their misfortunes well,"** meaning they conceal their suffering despite their **"careful faces."** This creates a contrast between appearance and reality, showing that marriage may seem joyful to outsiders while many women privately experience emotional strain. By exposing this hidden reality, Philips questions society's idealised image of marriage.

In contrast, unmarried life is presented as peaceful and fulfilling. The speaker declares that **"A virgin state is crowned with much content"** and is **"always happy as it's innocent."** The metaphor of being **"crowned"** suggests that remaining unmarried is a reward rather than a failure. Philips reinforces this positive image through the repetition of **"No,"** listing the problems single women avoid, including **"No blustering husbands," "No pangs of childbirth,"** and **"No children's cries."** This repeated structure emphasises the freedom and peace that come with remaining unmarried.

Towards the end of the poem, Philips directly advises her audience by saying, **"Thus, Madam, be advised by me."** The use of direct address and imperative verbs such as **"Turn, turn"** makes the poem sound like wise guidance from someone with personal experience. Rather than simply expressing her own opinions, the speaker actively encourages other women to avoid the difficulties of marriage.

Overall, Philips presents marriage as an institution that brings stress, responsibility, and suffering, while single life offers peace and independence. By challenging the expectations placed upon women, the poem encourages readers to value happiness and personal freedom over social pressure.

## Model Essay 2

## **How does Katherine Philips contrast marriage and single life in *A Married State*?**

Throughout *A Married State*, Katherine Philips creates a sharp contrast between the hardships of marriage and the happiness of remaining unmarried. By presenting marriage as restrictive and stressful while portraying virginity as peaceful and contented, Philips persuades readers to question society's expectations of women.

From the beginning of the poem, marriage is associated with discomfort. The opening line, **"A married state affords but little ease,"** immediately rejects the conventional belief that marriage guarantees happiness. Philips goes further by stating that **"The best of husbands are so hard to please,"** suggesting that wives constantly face pressure to satisfy their husbands. This creates the impression that marriage demands continuous effort from women without offering equal rewards.

The speaker then contrasts this with the advantages of remaining unmarried. She describes **"A virgin state"** as being **"crowned with much content,"** presenting single life as both rewarding and peaceful. The word **"crowned"** symbolises honour and fulfilment, suggesting that happiness does not depend on marriage.

Philips develops this contrast through repetition. The repeated use of **"No"** introduces a list of burdens that unmarried women escape: **"No blustering husbands," "No pangs of childbirth,"** and **"No children's cries."** This repetitive structure reinforces the many responsibilities associated with marriage while making single life appear calm and free from anxiety.

The poem also explores the difference between public appearance and private reality. Married women **"dissemble their misfortunes well,"** meaning they hide their suffering despite showing signs of worry through their **"careful faces."** Philips implies that society often celebrates marriage without recognising the sacrifices women make behind closed doors.

In the final lines, the speaker directly advises another woman to reject romantic impulses by urging her to **"Turn, turn apostate to love's levity."** The repetition of **"Turn"** creates urgency and shows the speaker's determination to protect women from entering unhappy marriages simply because society expects them to.

Through strong contrasts, persuasive language, and vivid imagery, Philips argues that single life offers greater peace and happiness than marriage. The poem challenges traditional attitudes towards women and encourages readers to judge marriage by its realities rather than its reputation.
