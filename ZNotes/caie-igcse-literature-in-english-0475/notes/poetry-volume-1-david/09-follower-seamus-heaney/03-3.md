## Themes

**Admiration & Idealisation**

Heaney presents the father as a figure of **near perfection,** seen through the eyes of a child who idolises him. The detailed description of his movements, precise, controlled, and effortless, elevates him beyond an ordinary farmer into something almost heroic.

However, this admiration is not entirely objective. It is shaped by **childhood perception,** which tends to exaggerate and idealise. This father becomes a symbol of **strength, authority, and competence,** representing everything the speaker aspires to be.

This theme highlights how children construct **idealised versions of their parents,** often placing them on a pedestal.

**The Struggle for Identity**

The speaker’s desire to “follow“ his father reflects a deeper attempt to **inherit identity and purpose.** In a rural context, this would traditionally mean continuing the family’s work and lifestyle.

However, the speaker’s clumsiness and inability to match his father’s skill suggest a **failure to replicate that identity.** This creates a sense of tension between:

* Who he wants to be (his father)

* Who he actually is (inexperienced and different)

Heaney explores the idea that identity is **not simply inherited, but must be developed individually, often through failure and self-realisation.**

**Disillusionment & Changing Perspective**

As the speaker grows older, his view of his father becomes less romanticised. The admiration of childhood gives way to a more realistic, and slightly frustrated perspective.

The father, once seen as powerful and flawless, is now portrayed as **fallible and dependent.** The verb “stumbling“ contrasts sharply with the earlier image of precision and control.

This reflects a broader theme of **disillusionment,** where growing up involves recognising that **those we once admired are human and imperfect.**

**Distance, Guilt & Emotional Complexity**

Although the poem presents a physical closeness (following, walking behind), there is an increasing sense of **emotional distance.**

The speaker’s frustration at the end suggests a possible feeling of **guilt or discomfort.** He is no longer the admiring child, yet he is not fully at peace with this new dynamic.

This suggests that changing family roles can bring:

* Emotional tension

* Unspoken guilt

* A sense of loss for the past

The poem does not resolve these feelings, making the ending more **realistic and thought-provoking.**
