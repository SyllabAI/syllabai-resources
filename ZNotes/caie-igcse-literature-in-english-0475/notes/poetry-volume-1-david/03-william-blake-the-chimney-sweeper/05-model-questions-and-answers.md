## **Model Questions and Answers**

**1 :** How does Blake present the suffering of children in The Chimney Sweeper?

**Model Answer:**

Blake presents the suffering of children as both physically harsh and emotionally suppressed. From the opening line, *“my father sold me,”* the verb “sold” immediately reduces the child to an object of trade, suggesting that poverty has destroyed parental protection. This highlights the cruelty of a society where children are treated as economic commodities. Furthermore, the speaker’s inability to pronounce “sweep” correctly *“’weep! ’weep!”*  emphasises his extreme youth, while also echoing the word “weep,” subtly reinforcing the child’s hidden grief.

Blake also uses imagery of entrapment to show the severity of their suffering. The metaphor *“coffins of black”* suggests that the chimneys are like graves, presenting their work as a form of living death. The darkness contrasts sharply with the bright, hopeful imagery in Tom’s dream, emphasising how bleak their reality truly is.

However, what makes the suffering more disturbing is the children’s acceptance of it. The final line, *“So if all do their duty, they need not fear harm,”* is deeply ironic. The children obey, yet they are clearly harmed. Blake uses this irony to criticise a society that teaches vulnerable children to accept exploitation rather than resist it.

**2:** How does Blake use religious imagery in the poem?

**Model Answer:**

Blake uses religious imagery to explore both comfort and control. In Tom Dacre’s dream, the children are freed from *“coffins of black”* by an Angel with a *“bright key.”* The brightness symbolises hope and divine salvation, offering a powerful contrast to the dark soot of their daily lives. The children running freely on a *“green plain”* and shining in the sun suggests purity restored, presenting heaven as a place of peace and freedom.

However, Blake subtly critiques this religious comfort. The Angel promises that Tom will have joy *“if he’d be a good boy,”* making salvation conditional upon obedience. This implies that religion encourages passive acceptance of suffering rather than challenging injustice. The dream offers spiritual escape but no practical change  when Tom awakens, he returns immediately to work in the dark.

Through this contrast, Blake suggests that religion can provide emotional comfort while simultaneously reinforcing social control, allowing exploitation to continue unquestioned.

**3:** Explore the importance of innocence in the poem.

**Model Answer:**

Innocence is central to Blake’s message, as it shapes both the tone and the tragedy of the poem. The speaker narrates his experience in a calm, matter-of-fact way, despite describing abandonment and forced labour. His mispronunciation of “sweep” as *“’weep!”* highlights his youth and vulnerability, making his exploitation more shocking.

Tom Dacre represents innocence even more clearly. His hair is described as *“curled like a lamb’s back,”* linking him to the biblical symbol of purity and sacrifice. The shaving of his head symbolises the stripping away of childhood and identity. Yet instead of rebellion, the children comfort each other and accept their situation.

Blake suggests that innocence is not protective; instead, it makes children easier to manipulate. Their belief that obedience will prevent harm shows how innocence can be shaped by powerful institutions. Through this, Blake exposes how society exploits the very purity it claims to value.

## **Conclusion**

Ultimately, in The Chimney Sweeper, Blake uses a child’s naïve voice, powerful symbolism, and structural contrasts to criticise a society that exploits children while disguising cruelty as moral duty. Through the tension between comforting religious imagery and harsh reality, he reveals how innocence can be manipulated to maintain injustice rather than challenge it.
