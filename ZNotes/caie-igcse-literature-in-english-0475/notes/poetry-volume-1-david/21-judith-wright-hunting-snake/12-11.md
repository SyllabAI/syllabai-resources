## **Question 1**

### How does Wright present the snake in *Hunting Snake*?

Wright presents the snake as both **powerful and beautiful**. When it first appears, the speakers immediately react with fear, as they **"froze half-through a pace."** The verb **"froze"** suggests that the snake has the power to interrupt human movement and make the speakers feel vulnerable.

However, Wright also presents the snake as visually beautiful. The image **"sun glazed his curves of diamond scale"** uses the metaphor **"diamond"** to make the snake's scales appear precious and brilliant. The natural sunlight further enhances its appearance.

Wright combines these opposing qualities at the end of the description, calling the snake **"Cold, dark and splendid."** While **"cold"** and **"dark"** create a threatening impression, **"splendid"** expresses admiration.

Overall, Wright presents the snake as a creature that inspires **both fear and wonder**, demonstrating the complexity and power of nature.

## **Question 2**

### How does Wright explore the relationship between beauty and danger?

Wright explores beauty and danger by presenting the snake as both a predator and a visually impressive creature. The snake has **"fierce intent"**, suggesting that it is actively hunting another animal. This establishes it as a potentially threatening presence.

At the same time, Wright describes its appearance through beautiful imagery, particularly **"diamond scale."** The metaphor makes the snake appear precious, encouraging the reader to admire it despite its dangerous nature.

The strongest combination of these ideas appears in **"Cold, dark and splendid."** The first two adjectives suggest danger and mystery, while **"splendid"** expresses admiration. The contrast demonstrates that the snake cannot be understood simply as frightening.

Therefore, Wright suggests that **nature's beauty and danger are not opposites but can exist together**.

## **Question 3**

### How does Wright present the power of nature?

Wright presents nature as powerful enough to completely interrupt human activity. The speakers are initially walking peacefully, but when the snake appears, they **"froze half-through a pace."** This sudden change shows that the humans immediately recognise the snake's power.

The snake itself appears confident and purposeful as it **"quested through the parting grass."** The verb **"quested"** suggests determination, while the snake's movement is not affected by the humans watching it.

The humans do not interfere with the snake. Instead, they stand still and allow it to continue on its own path. Eventually, it disappears into **"the grass that hid his prey."**

The poem therefore presents nature as an **independent force that humans cannot control**, reminding us that human beings are only observers within the natural world.
