3. b. ‘Death and the King’s Horseman’, by Wole Soyinka, is a play revolving around the night a king’s horseman is supposed to commit a ritualistic suicide. The horseman is Elesin Oba, a lively and energetic man that portrays multiple themes through his enjoyment of life.

   \
   To begin with, Elesin’s strong sense of duty is a part of how Soyinka powerfully conveys his enjoyment of life. As he is the king’s horseman, after the monarch’s death he must also die, in order to follow the king into the afterlife. One would expect him to attempt to evade this fate, as he appears to love living in this world; but the opposite is seen in the play. As the Praise-Singer banters with him in the market, Elesin assures him that he will not escape death. By welcoming it instead of fearing it, the stark contrast made contributes to how Soyinka portrays Elesin’s enjoyment of life. Moreover, even when all hope of completing the ritual seems to be gone, Elesin still attempts to complete at least a part of his duty by talking to the supposed ‘courier’ brought to him by some women. Enjoying life is often associated with modernism, and rejecting traditional customs within a culture. However, by maintaining Elesin’s character as that of a traditionalist, Soyinka highlights his enjoyment of life even further as he does not reject the Yoruba tribe’s culture, but rather embraces and practices it. This strong attachment to his duty helps to emphasize the horseman’s love for living amongst the people in this world, rather than dwelling upon what the afterlife would be like.

   \
   In addition to this, Soyinka also uses Elesin’s relationships and interactions with other characters in the play, in order to further highlight his enjoyment of life. A large part of this comes from his love for women. He constantly surrounds himself with them, especially within the market, and seems to be notorious for doing so. He jokes with them, and it is implied that he is familiar with the women and visits them regularly. To add on to this, it is clear that the women respect and value him, so much so that the ‘mother of the market’ Iyaloja even allows him to marry her own son’s fiancée. This relationship helps to show how Elesin adores living, especially amongst those that are youthful; this is further emphasized by how he marries a young woman mere hours before his death. His love for living is clear, as he still makes the most out of his living, though this is about to end within less than a day. Furthermore, his interactions with the Praise-Singer also contribute to the portrayal of his love of life. The Praise-Singer appears to be extremely attached to Elesin, getting highly emotional and breaking down, when simply thinking of what the afterlife would be like for the horseman. Even while he breaks down, Elesin is still dancing. This shows his enjoyment of life, as he appears to be constantly optimistic and focuses on the present, rather than the future.

   \
   Finally, Elesin’s love for living is also shown through the character’s intense
   emotions, throughout various scenes in the play. For instance, he is seen having
   moments of intense joy and pleasure through multiple scenes, such as when he
   first enters the market with drummers and praise singers, and also when he
   sees his future bride for the first time. Soyinka’s vivid writing in such scenes
   also contributes to showcasing his enjoyment for life: he is accompanied by
   drummers, which are commonly associated with celebration. Moreover, praise
   singers also add to the positive atmosphere surrounding Elesin, as well as the
   lively women around him in the market. To further add on to this, his desire to
   live a fulfilling life is also a part of his love for living, as he constantly tries to
   make sure that he has lived a memorable life, where he’d forever be
   remembered by those around him. Elesin’s commitment to his role as the
   king’s horseman, as mentioned earlier, is a large component of how Soyinka
   conveys his enjoyment of life to the reader, as it shows his constant optimism
   throughout multiple conflicts within the play. The vivid writing in such scenes
   also adds onto this, as Elesin constantly speaks of his fate positively, seeing it as
   a duty, rather than a tragedy.

   \
   In conclusion, Soyinka makes use of multiple forms of writing to convey Elesin’s
   enjoyment of life, in particular his interactions with other characters, his sense
   of duty, and his intense emotions within multiple scenes of the play.
