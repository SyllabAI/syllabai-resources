## Designing products to have minimum environmental impact

To achieve minimum environmental impact, designers must evaluate four critical lifecycle stages:

* ### Raw Material Extraction

  * Environmental Impact - The initial phase often causes severe ecological disruption, including deforestation, habitat destruction, open-cast mining scars, soil erosion, and heavy water and chemical pollution. Extracting virgin ores and refining fossil-fuel feedstocks deplete finite natural reserves. Sustainable design seeks to mitigate this by substituting virgin materials with recycled, bio-based, or abundant renewable alternatives.

* ### Energy Consumption

  * Environmental Impact - Energy is consumed during both the manufacturing phase (machining, thermal processing, assembly) and the use phase (electricity consumed by appliances over years of operation).

  * Design Mitigation - Designers must specify energy-efficient internal components (e.g., brushless motors, LED illumination), incorporate automatic standby or sleep modes, and lower the embodied energy of the production methods selected.

* ### Ease of Repair and Maintenance

  * Environmental Impact - Products that fail prematurely or cannot be serviced generate massive volumes of electronic and municipal solid waste.

  * Design Mitigation - Integrating modular architecture, accessible fasteners, and readily available replacement parts ensures products can be easily maintained, extending their functional lifespan.

* ### Disposal at the End of Life

  * Environmental Impact - Improper disposal leads to overflowing landfills, toxic chemical leachate from electronic waste (e-waste), and greenhouse gas emissions from incineration.

  * Design Mitigation - Broader disposal considerations include designing for complete biodegradability, ensuring compatibility with municipal recycling streams, avoiding toxic composite bonding, and establishing closed-loop collection pathways so materials do not end up as permanent waste.
