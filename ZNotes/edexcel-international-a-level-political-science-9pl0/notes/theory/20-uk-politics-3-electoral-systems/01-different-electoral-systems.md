# UK Politics - 3 Electoral Systems

## Different Electoral Systems

### **First-past-the-post (FPTP)**

* The UK General Election uses an electoral system known as first-past-the-post (FPTP)
* The UK is divided into 650 constituencies
* In each constituency, each voter votes for 1 candidate
* Each candidate usually represents a political party, and very few are independent (currently, there are 17 independent MPs in Parliament, out of 650)
* It uses a majoritarian system: the candidate with the most votes wins a seat in Parliament and becomes an MP
* The political party with the most seats wins the general election

### **Safe Seats and Marginal Seats**

* A safe seat is a constituency where election results are unlikely to change from one election to another
  * E.g. Liverpool Walton has always elected a Labour MP since 1964
* A marginal seat is a constituency where election results are likely to change from one election to another
  * E.g. Bury North- out of the 10 elections from 1983-2019, 6 were won by Conservative, 4 were won by Labour

### **Majority and Hung Parliament**

* A majority government is when a political party wins over half of the 650 seats in Parliament
* A hung parliament is when no single parties win over half of the seats in Parliament
  * In 2010- the Conservative Party did not win a majority and formed a coalition government with the Liberal Democrats
  * In 2017- the Conservative Party did not win a majority and signed a confidence-and-supply agreement with the Democratic Unionist Party (DUP)
* In the most recent election (2019), the Conservative Party won with a majority of 80 seats

**Single transferable vote (STV)**

|

Northern Ireland Assembly Scottish local councils

|

Voters place candidates in order of preference Each candidate must achieve a certain quota of votes to win When a candidate has already received enough votes to win, the extra votes will be moved to each voter’s second favourite candidate If no candidates reach the quota, the least popular candidate will be removed- voters who voted for them would have their votes moved to their second favourite candidate This process repeats until all vacancies are filled

|

**Proportional**- the number of seats won by each party is more likely to match the % of votes The quota ensures that each candidate must win a certain number of votes in order to be elected

|

Less likely to lead to majority results and strong governments The process is more complicated, and results can take longer to count

||
||

**Supplementary vote (SV)**

|

Directed elected mayors, Police and Crime Commissioners

|

Each voter votes for two candidates (first and second preference) A candidate must receive over 50% of the votes to win If no candidate wins over 50% of the vote, all candidates apart from the top 2 are eliminated- the ‘second preference vote’ for these two are counted, and the candidate with the most votes wins

|

It ensures that a candidate must win over 50% of the vote to be elected

|

It is more complicated than FPTP but allows fewer preferences than STV It still discriminates against smaller parties, as only 2 candidates can cut

**Additional member system (AMS)**

|

Scottish Parliament Welsh Parliament Greater London Assembly

|

Each voter two votes- the first ballot paper votes for a candidate, and the second ballot paper votes for a party. The first ballot paper is counted first- the same way as FPTP. Then, the second ballot paper is counted, and additional members for each party are added to make the % of votes match the number of seats. E.g. if a party wins 10% of the vote, which equates to 10 seats, but only 3 candidates have been elected from the first stage, 7 additional members will be added and elected from the second stage

|

Proportional It gives voters a wider choice

|

Ends up with two types of elected members, which can be confusing
