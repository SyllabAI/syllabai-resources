## **Understanding the Objectives and Texts**

You can earn **up to 10 marks** on this task for how well you analyze and respond to both the implicit and explicit ideas and viewpoints provided in the two texts. Alongside mentioning points from both passages, you must also evaluate the points. Evaluation means to judge how strong or weak the points are and to add any extra details (not present in the texts) to strengthen the points. For example, if you are supporting Text A, you could strengthen your point by explaining its wider impact or adding a personal anecdotes (but do not do this in articles). If you disagree with Text B, then explain why its points are less convincing by giving evidence or reasoning against them. In the end, your essay should make clear which text you find more convincing, based on the arguments you’ve made in your body paragraphs.

### **Your response to the above question will be based upon the following texts:**

### **TEXT A:** The post below is from an online education forum in which a Headteacher argues for study exchange visits to different countries by teenagers.

Many people here think that when teenagers go abroad on study exchange trips, they don’t learn very much and that these programmes are basically short holidays paid for by parents and already underfunded schools. Some argue that older students should spend all their time in their own classrooms so they stand a better chance in university entrance and the tough job market later on.

At my school, though, we’ve recently launched an ambitious exchange scheme. In the second term of their course, students can spend a few weeks living and studying in another country, while staying up to date with some of their work from home via online platforms. The scheme has become extremely popular. Students can apply to spend four weeks at a partner school abroad, but they must prove they are mature and responsible enough to represent both their school and their country, and to genuinely gain from the experience themselves.

When we select destinations carefully, our students come back from these exchanges having been enriched by contact with new cultures and different teaching styles and philosophies. One of our top 14-year-old students recently returned from four weeks at a school in a country with a struggling economy, where the facilities were very basic. She realised that it is the teacher – not the latest technology – that truly makes learning effective. Other students have come back inspired to work in the countries they visited, which has boosted their ambition and determination to succeed.

Education is about much more than cramming students’ minds with facts. The chances that exchange visits offer – to build character, resilience and a spirit of adventure – simply cannot be reproduced in an ordinary classroom. We’ve seen students return from overseas study more driven and excited about learning, and more accepting and open-minded about the wider world. To me, that is what real education looks like.

### TEXT B: This post was written by another Headteacher in response to the Headteacher in Text A.

People often say that travel broadens the mind, and perhaps that’s true. However, I don’t think a school should lose sight of its main purpose: giving students the knowledge and skills they need to build successful lives for themselves, their families, and their communities in the future.

A great deal of learning can take place in four weeks, and organising a long exchange visit demands huge amounts of time and effort. Teachers must thoroughly check host families, plan the trip, and prepare students, all of which is very demanding on staff. In my experience, it’s also quite unusual for a 14-year-old to cope well with being away from their home, school, and country for an entire month. The teenage years are already emotionally difficult for many young people, and such questionable “experiences” can make things worse, leaving the school to deal with the emotional fallout.

Having previously taught in schools abroad, I’m well aware that teaching methods and educational values differ greatly from one country to another. Students who spend four weeks in a foreign school may come back with shallow impressions about how other systems work, but their true education takes place in their own school. How can 14-year-olds fairly evaluate what they see elsewhere? A brief glimpse of a more relaxed, less demanding school might lower their ambitions. On the other hand, seeing a school with small classes, luxurious facilities and the latest technology could breed resentment. In some cases, that resentment might later push them away from the very communities where their education could have the greatest positive impact.

When it comes to welcoming overseas students here on exchanges, I would find it difficult to ask my teachers to adjust their lessons for someone whose exam results they are not accountable for. I also question how parents of our own students would feel about teachers devoting time to visitors who will only be here temporarily. We cannot reliably measure any concrete benefits that our students might gain from going on such exchanges, but we *can* measure potential drawbacks in the form of lower grades. At present, we already maintain strong partnerships with several schools abroad, where our students regularly correspond with their peers, improving their language abilities and widening their outlooks.
