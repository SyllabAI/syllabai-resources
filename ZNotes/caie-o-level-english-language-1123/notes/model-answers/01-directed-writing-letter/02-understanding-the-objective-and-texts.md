## Understanding the Objectives and Texts

You can earn **up to 10 marks** on this task for how well you analyze and respond to both the implicit and explicit ideas and viewpoints provided in the two texts. Alongside mentioning points from both passages, you must also evaluate the points. Evaluation means to judge how strong or weak the points are and to add any extra details (not present in the texts) to strengthen the points. For example, if you are supporting Text A, you could strengthen your point by explaining its wider impact or adding a personal anecdotes (but do not do this in articles). If you disagree with Text B, then explain why its points are less convincing by giving evidence or reasoning against them. In the end, your essay should make clear which text you find more convincing, based on the arguments you’ve made in your body paragraphs

### Text A: Understanding the value of space tourism (The piece below highlights some of the advantages that space tourism can offer)

At the moment, one of the most thrilling space hubs on the planet is actually an old Second World War hangar in California’s Mojave Desert. This wooden hangar is home to a company designing rocket planes that will take tourists into space. On the same runway, another billionaire is working on a new type of space launch system, and there are eleven more small space companies based around the Mojave Air and Space Port.

Some of these firms plan to charge enormous amounts of money so people can enjoy just five minutes without gravity; others will sell seats on small rocket ships carrying only two passengers. The prices mean that most people will never afford these trips, but there are still important reasons why space tourism matters.

For one thing, the technology being developed may eventually allow us to cut flight times drastically between major cities by briefly travelling through space. Space tourism is also likely to inspire young people to become engineers. As one 26-year-old engineer there explains, it’s a dream job: instead of sitting at a desk all day, he can go into the workshop, use tools, and fire rocket engines.

Another benefit is that the cost of going into space should fall. Traditional rockets could only be used once and were extremely expensive, but tourist space planes are being designed to fly to orbit many times. This could finally make access to space financially realistic for far more people.

Space travel will also give us a new perspective on our planet. Many people believe that one of the greatest outcomes of the Apollo missions was the view of Earth from space. Apollo 8 astronaut Bill Anders famously said, “We came all this way to explore the Moon, and the most important thing is that we discovered the Earth.” Those images showed us our world as a small blue sphere in the emptiness of space.

Every astronaut I’ve spoken to says that seeing Earth from space transformed how they think about the world. If more people could share that experience through space tourism, might it affect how we act on issues like pollution and climate change?

Text B: Why would I ever choose to be a space tourist? (The text below presents an argument against space tourism)

Imagine this for a moment: you’ve got a very wealthy elderly uncle who is obsessed with space. He offers you a huge amount of money on one condition: that you use it to do something he’s always dreamed of but is now too old and weak to attempt himself: buy a tourist ticket on one of the first commercial flights into space.

Sounds like the adventure of a lifetime, right? Personally, I’d have to politely suggest he gives that money to someone else.

Even without thinking about how much genuine good that money could do if spent on a real cause, it’s still difficult to defend the enormous financial and environmental cost of space tourism. There are millions of people who’ve never even left their own area, for whom ordinary travel on Earth is completely out of reach. Even in rich countries, going abroad is not the norm. In fact, 64% of people in the USA have never travelled outside their country. Before we turn space into the next trendy holiday destination, shouldn’t we be making it possible for more people to explore their own planet first?

Tackling poverty or the damage caused by war doesn’t seem to attract billionaires in the same way as rockets do. Those causes won’t deliver impressive profits in 10 or even 50 years’ time. I understand the excitement of high-tech engineering and rockets blasting off in spectacular displays. But while a tiny, privileged minority gains as space tourists or as workers in this industry, huge numbers of people are still left in hardship and darkness.

Astronauts often talk about how breathtaking Earth looks from space. Don Thomas, who has flown there four times, believes that the more people see that incredible view, the better it will be for all of us. Yet he also mentions how fragile the planet appears, and how unbelievably thin the protective layer of atmosphere is. I find it hard to see how a booming space tourism business is going to help protect that fragile world.

So I think my hypothetical space-loving uncle will need to find someone else willing to squander his fortune on such a trip.
