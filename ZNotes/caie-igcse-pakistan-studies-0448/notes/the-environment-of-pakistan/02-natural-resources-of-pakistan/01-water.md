# Natural Resources of Pakistan

## Water

### Water resources and the Indus system

* The Indus River provides the key water resources for the economy of Pakistan.

* The river supports many heavy industries and provides the main supply of potable water in Pakistan.

* The ultimate source of the Indus is in Tibet, flowing northwest through Ladakh and Baltistan into Gilgit, just south of the Karakoram Range.

* The Shyok River and Gilgit streams carry glacial waters into the main river.

* It gradually bends to the south, emerging from the hills between Peshawar and Rawalpindi.

* It flows swiftly across Hazara and is dammed at the Tarbela Reservoir, with the Kabul River joining it near Attock.

* The remainder of its route to the sea runs through the plains of Punjab and Sindh, where the river becomes slow-flowing and highly braided, joining the Panjnad River at Mithankot before ending in a large delta east of Thatta.

* The Indus basin covers an area of about $1$ million km and irrigates about $60$% of Pakistan's cultivatable land.

* The total journey of the Indus from its source at Lake Mansarovar in Tibet, China, to its mouth is about $2900$ km.

* The river has the highest mean monthly discharge in June and July.

### Irrigation infrastructure and tributaries

* Pakistan possesses a vast and unified irrigation system consisting of $3$ major reservoirs (Chashma, Mangla, and Tarbela), $19$ barrages (including Sukkur, Kotri, Guddu, Kalabagh, Jinnah, Chashma, Taunsa, Rasul, Marala, Khanki, Qadirabad, Trimmu, Panjnad, Balloki, Sidhnai, Sulemanki, Islam), $12$ link canals and $45$ irrigation canals.

* The Eastern tributaries of the Indus include the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej. The Beas joins the Sutlej before entering Pakistan, and all eastern tributaries join at Panjnad, flowing as a unified stream for $72$ km before joining the Indus at Mithankot.

* The Western tributaries include the Swat, Kabul, Kurram, Tochi, Gomal and Zhob rivers, which are smaller in length and width with less water than the eastern tributaries.

* The Indus Waters Treaty ($1960$) was brokered to settle water-sharing disputes, granting Pakistan full rights to the $3$ western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) and India rights to the $3$ eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej).

### Water management and regional rivers

* Water allocation conflicts involve internal political tensions between provinces (specifically Punjab and Sindh) over the disputed distribution of irrigation water.

* Solutions to water loss and pollution include lining open dirt canals with concrete blocks to prevent seepage and enforcing strict laws requiring factories to treat toxic wastewater before discharge.

* Rivers of Balochistan include the Hingol River (the longest, flowing year-round into the Arabian Sea), the Hub River (forming the provincial boundary between Sindh and Balochistan and feeding the Hub Dam for Karachi), the Zhob River (flowing northeast into the Gomal River) and the Dasht and Bolan rivers.

* Seasonal salt lakes called Hamuns, most notably Hamun-e-Mashkhel, form in western Balochistan as temporary rivers dry up in flat desert depression.

### How supplies are obtained maintained and controlled.

* **Obtained**: Surface water is captured via the Indus River and its tributaries, while groundwater is extracted through over $1.2$ million tube wells (both public and private), making Pakistan one of the largest groundwater users in the world.

* **Maintained**: Supply is maintained through the Indus Basin Replacement Works - a network of dams and barrages that store monsoon and glacial meltwater, releasing it gradually during dry winter months. The system operates on a "canal command area" basis, where barrages divert water into main canals, which branch into distributaries and watercourses leading to farm gates.

* **Controlled**: Control is exercised via the Provincial Irrigation Departments and the Water and Power Development Authority (WAPDA), which regulate flow rates using telemetry systems at barrages. However, control is weak, over $40$% of water is lost before reaching fields due to seepage, evaporation, and illegal theft (via unauthorized "kundas" or pipe diversions). The system relies on a warp rotation (warabandi) schedule, delivering water to farmers in fixed $7$- to $14$-day rotations, but this breaks down during low-flow periods.

### Water use by sector

* **Agricultural**: About $90$%; mostly for irrigation of wheat, rice and cotton. Used in tube wells for groundwater extraction and also used in flood irrigation, sprinkler, and drip systems.

* **Industrial:** About $5$%; mostly used in cooling of thermal plants, textile dyeing, bleaching. Other than that its used in manufacture of steel and cement and used in sugar mills alongside paper mills.

* **Domestic:** About $5$%; mostly used as drinking water. Also used in cooking, washing and sanitation alongside hand pumps and wells in villages.

  * So supply is maintained by the dams (store monsoon/glacial melt) and barrages (divert flow into canals).

    * Supply is controlled by the IRSA (Indus River System Authority), Provincial irrigation departments and WAPDA (control of dams and power).

### The Indus water treaty

Brokered to settle water-sharing disputes between India and Pakistan, granting Pakistan full rights to the $3$ western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) and India rights to the $3$ eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej).

### Consequences of the Indus Water Treaty

* Positive: The treaty averted a full-scale war over water and gave Pakistan exclusive, legally binding rights to the three western rivers, ensuring $80$% of the basin's flow remains under Pakistani control. It also forced Pakistan to build the Tarbela and Mangla dams as storage replacements for the lost eastern rivers.

* Negative (Severe): By surrendering the Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej, Pakistan lost about $33$% of its historical surface water that used to flow into Punjab's canals. This has made Pakistan hydrologically dependent on India's goodwill, as India has built upstream storage (e.g., Baglihar Dam on the Chenab) that can delay or reduce flows during critical planting seasons.

* Long-term evaluation: The treaty is "watertight but inflexible", it has no mechanism for climate change or population growth. Pakistan now faces a permanent water deficit of about $20$-$30$ MAF annually, which the treaty did not anticipate. The loss of eastern rivers has also accelerated groundwater overdraft in Punjab, where the water table drops by over $1$ meter per year in some districts.

### Water allocation conflicts and solutions

* Internal political tensions exist between provinces, specifically Punjab and Sindh, over the disputed distribution of irrigation water.

* Solutions to water loss and pollution include lining open dirt canals with concrete blocks to prevent seepage and enforcing strict laws requiring factories to treat toxic wastewater before discharge.

### Pollution

* Industrial effluents: Over $6,000$ factories (textiles, tanneries, sugar mills) in Punjab and Sindh discharge untreated toxic waste (including chromium, lead and arsenic) go directly into rivers and canals.

* Agricultural runoff: Excessive use of nitrate based fertilizers and pesticides leaches into water bodies, causing eutrophication (algal blooms that deplete oxygen).

* Municipal sewage: Only $8$% of urban wastewater is treated; the rest flows raw into the Indus, with Karachi alone dumping over $350$ million gallons of untreated sewage daily into the Arabian Sea via the Lyari and Malir rivers.

* Evaluation: Pollution has rendered over $60$% of Pakistan's drinking water sources unsafe, contributing to $40$% of all diseases (gastroenteritis, hepatitis, and cholera). Unlike physical water scarcity, pollution creates "economic scarcity" - water exists but is too expensive to treat. The cost of inaction is estimated at $1.3$% of GDP annually in health and productivity losses. Existing environmental laws (e.g., Pakistan Environmental Protection Act, $1997$) are weakly enforced due to limited EPA staff, political pressure on industry, and courts that rarely impose meaningful fines.

### Punjab vs Sindh conflict

* **The core issue**: The dispute is not just about quantity, it's about timing and location. Sindh argues that Punjab's upstream dams (particularly Chashma and Tarbela) store water during winter and release it during summer, but Punjab uses that release first for its own crops, leaving Sindh with reduced and late-arriving flows, often $10$-$15$ days after the optimal sowing window for Sindh's rice and cotton.

* **Legal friction**: Under the Water Accord of $1991$, Punjab is allocated $55$% of the Indus's water and Sindh $41$%, but Punjab consistently exceeds its share during low-flow years (by up to $15$%), while Sindh falls short by a similar margin. Sindh also accuses Punjab of "pillaging" the river by extracting groundwater near the riverbanks, which reduces baseflow into Sindh.

* **Political evaluation**: The conflict is institutionally frozen because the Indus River System Authority (IRSA) which is supposed to enforce the accord, has representatives from all provinces but operates on consensus, which Punjab can block. Since Punjab holds the largest voting bloc in the National Assembly, Sindh has no political leverage. This has led Sindh to threaten legal action in the Supreme Court and even raise the issue with the Council of Common Interests (CCI), but no binding resolution has been achieved. The conflict is likely to escalate as climate change reduces glacier melt, making water an even more explosive political issue than territorial disputes.

### Reasons for development of plantations in Indus Plain

* **Why plantations exist here:**

  * The Indus Plain has alluvial soil (replenished annually by silt deposits), flat terrain suitable for mechanized farming, and a reliable canal irrigation network, making it ideal for linear plantation along canals, roads and railway tracks.

  * Plantations are not natural forests; they are man-made, established under government schemes like the "Ten Billion Tree Tsunami" and provincial forest extension programs.

* **Key species planted**

  * **Shishum (Dalbergia sissoo) and Babul (Acacia nilotica)**: fast-growing, drought-resistant hardwoods used for furniture, fuelwood, and agricultural tools.

  * **Mulberry and Eucalyptus**: planted along canal banks for shade, windbreaks, and pulpwood

* **Purpose**

  * Reduce pressure on natural coniferous forests by supplying industrial timber locally.

  * Prevent soil erosion along canal banks and river embankments.

  * Provide fuelwood to rural communities, reducing illegal logging in northern forests.

  * Act as carbon sinks in agricultural landscapes.

* **Limitations**

  * Plantations are monocultures (low biodiversity), vulnerable to pests and diseases.

  * Eucalyptus is water-thirsty so it lowers the water table and depletes soil nutrients, making it controversial in arid areas.
