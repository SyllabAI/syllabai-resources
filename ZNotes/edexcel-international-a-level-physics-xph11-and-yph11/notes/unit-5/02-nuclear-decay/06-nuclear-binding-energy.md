## Nuclear Binding Energy

* When the actual mass of a nucleus is measured, it is always slightly less than the theoretical mass. The difference between these two masses is the mass defect or the mass deficit.
* The mass deficit comes about because a small amount of the mass of the nucleons is converted into the energy needed to hold the nucleus together.
* This is called binding energy. It is calculated using Einstein’s mass-energy relationship:

$$

\Delta E = mc^2

$$

$m$ represents the mass defect

* You will often see the mass defect accompanied by the symbol $u$. This is the atomic mass unit, which equals 1.66 x $10^{-27}$

### **Binding Energy Per Nucleon**

* Nuclear binding energy per nucleon, which is the energy that would be needed to remove one nucleon from a nucleus, can be calculated by dividing the nuclear binding energy by the number of nucleons.

* If a substance contains n number of nucleons, then its average binding energy per nucleon will be:

$$

E=\frac{\Delta mc^2}{n}

$$

* Drawing a graph of binding energy per nucleon against the mass number for the nuclei gives us a useful means of comparing how tightly different nuclides are bound together.

* The stability of a particular nucleus depends on its binding energy per nucleon.

* The most stable elements are those which have the greatest binding energy per nucleon. e.g.  iron is the most stable element because its binding energy per nucleon is greatest compared to all elements
