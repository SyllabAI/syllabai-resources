Litmus answers one question — acid or alkali. To measure the pH itself you
need an indicator whose colour slides through a range, and that is
[[universal-indicator]]: a mixture of indicators blended so their colours
merge into a gradual sweep across the whole [[ph-scale]]. Full-range
universal indicator changes through a ladder of colours from pH 1 to pH 14,
as a solution or as paper. You compare the colour against a chart and read
off the approximate pH. The word approximate matters — this is an estimate,
not a precise measurement.

The blending idea behind it generalises: any substance whose colour depends
on pH can serve as an [[indicator]]. Four matter for your exam, and you must
know their colours in acidic and in alkaline solutions without hesitation.

| Indicator | In acid | In alkali |
|---|---|---|
| [[litmus]] | red | blue |
| [[methyl-orange]] | red | yellow |
| [[phenolphthalein]] | colourless | pink |
| universal indicator | red | blue |

Neutral solutions complete the picture. Litmus is purple in a neutral
solution — not a third dye but an equal mixture of its red and blue forms,
which is exactly why it sits at the halfway point of its own colour story.
Universal indicator reads green at pH 7. While you are here, fix the five
formulae you will use all year: hydrochloric acid HCl, nitric acid HNO₃,
sulfuric acid H₂SO₄, ethanoic acid CH₃COOH and phosphoric acid H₃PO₄ — the
[[acid]]s whose replaceable hydrogens power the next section.

:::exam-hint
Colour-memory questions are free marks if you file the pairs, not the
list: litmus red/blue, methyl orange red/yellow, phenolphthalein
colourless/pink, universal indicator red/blue. Phenolphthalein is the one
that starts colourless — that makes it the indicator of choice for titration
endpoints, where a colour *appearing* is easier to see than one vanishing
— the titration section of this chapter shows you why. Universal indicator is the only one
that estimates a pH value; the other three only sort acidic from alkaline.
:::

:::misconception
"Universal indicator is the most accurate indicator, so always choose it."
Its gradual, many-coloured sweep is precisely its weakness: it gives an
approximate pH, it is not very accurate compared with a pH meter, and its
slow blend of colours makes it useless for detecting a sharp endpoint in a
titration. Accuracy belongs to the pH meter; sharpness belongs to the
single-colour-change indicators; universality belongs to universal
indicator. Each tool has its own job.
:::

:::quick-check
Question-bank join: spec 2.28 and 2.30 items. From memory, fill a four-row
table: each indicator against its acid colour and its alkali colour. Then
add the two neutral colours — litmus and universal indicator. Finally, state
which single indicator could tell you whether a solution is closer to pH 3
or pH 5, and why the other three cannot.
:::

*Extension.* At the next level of study, pH stops being a scale you memorise
and becomes a quantity you compute: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺(aq)], the negative
logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration. That equation explains a
curiosity you will meet at the end of this chapter — why one drop of alkali
near an endpoint can leap the reading from about pH 3 to about pH 11.
