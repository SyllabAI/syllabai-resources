When an [[acid]] meets a [[base]] or an [[alkali]], the reaction has a name:
[[neutralisation]]. The acid's hydrogen ions are consumed, and the products
tell the story — a salt and water. Two worked examples carry the whole idea,
one for each kind of reactant.

**Acid + base.** Copper(II) oxide is a metal oxide — a base. React it with
hot dilute sulfuric acid and the black powder dissolves to a blue solution:

CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

Copper(II) oxide is an ionic compound built from Cu²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, so the
mechanism is pure ion chemistry: the H⁺ ions from the acid combine with the
O²⁻ ions from the base to form water, and the copper and sulfate ions are
left holding the solution together as copper(II) sulfate.

**Acid + alkali.** Sodium hydroxide solution reacts with dilute hydrochloric
acid to give sodium chloride and water:

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Both sodium hydroxide and sodium chloride are ionic, so in solution every
one of them exists as separated ions — and HCl, being an acid, dissociates
into H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq). Rewrite the equation with every ion shown and
something jumps out: the Na⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) appear identically on both
sides. They are [[spectator-ion]]s — present, but unchanged. Strike them
out and what remains is the reaction itself, written as an
[[ionic-equation]]:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

This single line is the deepest idea in the chapter: every neutralisation
between an acid and an alkali has this same ionic equation, because every
one of them is the same event — the alkali's OH⁻ ions and the acid's H⁺
ions joining to make water. Change the acid, change the alkali, change the
salt; the reaction never changes.

:::exam-hint
Equation questions in this topic come in a reliable order: full formula
equation first, then identify the spectator ions, then the ionic equation.
State the spectators by name ("Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are unchanged on both sides")
before you cancel them — the cancellation is worth marks only once it is
justified. And "write the ionic equation for the reaction between any acid
and any alkali" is a gift question: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), every time.
:::

:::misconception
"Neutralisation needs an alkali, and alkalis are the only things that
neutralise acids." The copper(II) oxide example says otherwise: it is a
base, not an alkali — insoluble in water — yet it neutralises sulfuric acid
completely. What neutralisation requires is something that combines with
hydrogen ions: oxides, hydroxides and alkalis all qualify. Alkalis are a
sub-family of neutralisers, not the only one.
:::
