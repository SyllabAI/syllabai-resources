This section closes the chapter — and with it, the chemistry-only ribbon
that has run through the last stretch of Unit 2. Everything the chapter
taught now compresses into six abilities, one per objective, and each one is
a question shape you will meet on the page.

:::summary
The pH scale runs from about 0 to about 14 and sorts solutions into five
bands: strongly acidic (0–3), weakly acidic (4–6), neutral (7), weakly
alkaline (8–10) and strongly alkaline (11–14). Indicators read that
chemistry for you: litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein each give one
acid colour and one alkali colour, while universal indicator sweeps the
whole scale and estimates an approximate pH against a chart. Beneath the
colours, acids are sources of hydrogen ions and alkalis are sources of
hydroxide ions — sodium hydroxide dissociates straight to Na⁺ + OH⁻, ammonia
makes its OH⁻ by reacting with water, and soluble carbonates top up OH⁻
partially, which is why they are only weakly alkaline. Neutralisation is
those two ions meeting: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), the one ionic equation
behind every acid–alkali reaction, with the spectators struck out. And the
[[titration]] turns that chemistry into a measured volume: rough run, fast
then dropwise, one drop to the [[endpoint]], burette readings to two
decimal places.
:::

:::can-you
Keyed to the six objectives, in order: state the colours of litmus, methyl
orange and phenolphthalein in acidic and in alkaline solutions (2.28);
classify any given pH into its band and name an everyday example of each
band (2.29); describe universal indicator and say why its pH reading is
only approximate (2.30); define acid and alkali by their ions, with the
equations for NaOH and NH₃ in water (2.31); explain neutralisation with the
ionic equation and identify the spectator ions in a full equation (2.32);
and describe the full titration method — pipette, indicator, burette,
swirling, dropwise, two-decimal readings (2.33C, Paper 2C only).
:::

:::exam-hint
The chemistry-only flag matters for revision planning: the titration
objective is assessed in Paper 2C only, so Paper 1 never asks you to
describe the method — but the other five objectives of this chapter are
examined in both papers. Practice for all six objectives joins from the
platform question bank by spec ref, and the titration calculations
themselves wait in Chapter 6 (u1:c06).
:::

:::misconception
"'Chemistry only' means harder, so everyone should revise it first." It
means no such thing about difficulty — it means the Science Double Award
route never examines 2.33C, so Chemistry candidates meet titration
technique as their exclusive territory. The five shared objectives carry
the marks in both papers; the chemistry-only objective adds the technique
marks on top in Paper 2C.
:::

:::cross-curricular
Titration is one of chemistry's great exports. Pharmaceutical labs titrate
to verify the strength of every batch of medicine before it ships; the food
and drink industry checks acidity the same way — the citric acid of this
chapter's opening oranges is graded by titration in juice production;
environmental agencies run acid-rain monitoring by titrating rainfall
samples. The burette skills you are building are the same ones Quality
Control chemists clock in with — and the pH-band vocabulary of this chapter
is the language their reports are written in.
:::
