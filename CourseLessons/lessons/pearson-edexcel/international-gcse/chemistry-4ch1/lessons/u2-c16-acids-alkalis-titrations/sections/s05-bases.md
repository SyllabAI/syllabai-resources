A [[base]] is the mirror of an [[acid]]: bases neutralise acids by combining
with the hydrogen ions in them. At this level, a base means a metal oxide, a
metal hydroxide, or ammonia (NH₃). Copper(II) oxide and magnesium oxide are
bases you will react with acids this year; the black powder disappearing into
hot dilute sulfuric acid to give a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate is the
picture to keep.

Some bases go one step further: they dissolve in water to form solutions
containing [[hydroxide-ion]]s. These soluble bases are [[alkali]]s, and they
complete the ion story of this chapter — as acids are a source of hydrogen
ions, so alkalis are a source of hydroxide (OH⁻) ions in solution. The Group
1 hydroxides are the everyday examples: sodium hydroxide and potassium
hydroxide. In water, sodium hydroxide dissociates completely:

NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Ammonia is the alkali that behaves differently — it does not contain a
hydroxide to release. Instead, the dissolved ammonia reacts with the water to
make ammonium ions and hydroxide ions:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

You will meet this solution labelled "ammonium hydroxide" on cleaning-product
bottles; add universal indicator and it shows the blue-green of an alkali.
Every alkali in this family — hydroxides and ammonia solution alike — has a
pH greater than 7.

There are other routes to alkaline solutions too. Soluble metal carbonates
react with water to release hydroxide ions; the ionic equation at the next
level is CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) → OH⁻(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). The next section takes
one of those carbonate alkalis as its whole subject.

:::exam-hint
Keep the two definitions strictly paired: acid = source of H⁺(aq); alkali =
source of OH⁻(aq). The examiner's favourite follow-up is the relationship
between base and alkali — all alkalis are bases, but only bases that
dissolve in water producing hydroxide ions are alkalis. Copper(II) oxide is
the standard counter-example: a base, but insoluble, so not an alkali.
:::

:::misconception
"'Base' and 'alkali' are two words for the same thing." They overlap but are
not identical. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water to give hydroxide
ions; a base is anything that neutralises an acid by combining with its
hydrogen ions — including insoluble oxides such as copper(II) oxide and
magnesium oxide. Calling copper(II) oxide an alkali is a genuine chemistry
error, not a wording quibble.
:::
