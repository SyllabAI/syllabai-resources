Some acids you will never touch without full protective clothing and a spill
plan; others you eat by the slice. The citric acid in an orange and the acid
that demands gloves and goggles belong to the same chemical family — what
changes is how much hydrogen ion they release and how concentrated the
solution is. Alkalis have the same spread: the sodium hydroxide that clears
blocked drains and the mild carbonate solutions in household products all sit
on the same scale. This chapter gives you that scale, the tools to tell acids
from alkalis in seconds, and the classic technique chemists use to make them
react in exactly measured amounts: the [[titration]].

Three questions organise the chapter. First, **how do you tell an acid from an alkali?** With [[indicator]]s — the colour-switching dyes [[litmus]],
[[phenolphthalein]] and [[methyl-orange]], and the full-range
[[universal-indicator]] that estimates a pH value. Second, **how acidic or
alkaline is it, exactly?** With the pH scale, which you will learn to read
into five bands from strongly acidic to strongly alkaline. Third, **what is
really happening when they react?** Acids are sources of hydrogen ions,
alkalis are sources of hydroxide ions, and neutralisation is those two ions
meeting to make water — one ionic equation that covers every acid–alkali
reaction you will ever write at this level.

The chapter closes with a skill that belongs to chemistry alone in your
specification: carrying out an acid–alkali titration with burette precision.
Everything you learn here feeds forward — the reactions of acids continue in
Chapter 17 (u2:c17) with salt preparations, and the calculation side of
titrations is waiting in Chapter 6 (u1:c06).

:::exam-hint
Five of this chapter's six objectives are examined in both papers — Paper 1C
and Paper 2C. The sixth, describing how to carry out an acid–alkali
titration, is chemistry-only: it appears in Paper 2C only. The recurring
question shapes are: state the colours an indicator shows in acidic and
alkaline solutions; classify solutions into the five pH bands; write
equations for acid + base and acid + alkali, then the ionic equation; and
describe the titration method step by step. Watch the command words:
*state* wants a colour or a number, *describe* wants the method in order,
*explain* wants the ions.
:::

:::misconception
"An indicator tells you the pH of a solution." Not the three you must name
for the exam. Litmus, phenolphthalein and methyl orange each show just two
colours — they can only sort a solution into acidic or alkaline. Only
universal indicator, which blends several dyes, gives an approximate pH
value by the colour it turns. Choosing the right indicator for the job is
itself an exam skill this chapter teaches.
:::
