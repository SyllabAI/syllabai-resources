A [[neutralisation]] on paper is over in one line of equation; in the lab
you want to know exactly *how much* acid reacts with exactly how much alkali.
The [[titration]] is how you find out. One solution of known volume sits in
a conical flask with a few drops of indicator; the other runs in from a
[[burette]] until the indicator flips colour — and the burette reading tells
you the reacting volume to a fiftieth of a cubic centimetre. (What you do
with those volumes — the concentration calculations — is Chapter 6's
toolkit: u1:c06.)

The method has a rhythm, and the description of it is itself examinable —
this is the one objective in the chapter assessed in Paper 2C only:

1. **Rough run first.** Add alkali from the burette to the acid until the
   indicator changes colour, reading roughly. If the rough run needed about
   22.50 cm³, that number is your map for the accurate runs.
2. **Accurate run, fast then slow.** Repeat with a fresh mixture. Run in
   about 20 cm³ quite quickly, swirling the conical flask constantly to mix
   the solutions — then slow to a drip.
3. **Dropwise to the flip.** Add the alkali one drop at a time until a
   single drop changes the indicator from its acid colour to its alkali
   colour. You now know the neutralisation volume to within one drop — as
   precise as the technique allows.
4. **Choose the direction deliberately.** You could run acid into alkali
   instead, but with phenolphthalein the alkali-into-acid direction wins:
   watching colourless turn pink is easier on the eye than watching pink
   vanish.

Phenolphthalein is the classic choice because of exactly that sharp,
two-colour flip. [[methyl-orange]] works too. What must never be used is
[[universal-indicator]]: its colours change gradually through a whole range,
so there is no single unmistakable moment to call the endpoint.

Reading the burette has its own law. The scale runs the opposite way to
every measuring cylinder you have used: 0 sits at the top and 50 at the
bottom, so the numbers grow as the liquid level falls. Record every reading to two decimal places — the nearest
0.05 cm³. The second decimal place is a 0 when the meniscus sits on a scale
line and a 5 when it sits between two lines. Subtract initial from final and
you have the volume delivered.

:::key-point
The [[endpoint]] is the point at which the indicator changes colour — and it
does not necessarily coincide with the point where the solution is exactly
neutral. Near the endpoint the pH changes extremely fast: in some titrations
a single drop of alkali swings the pH from about 3 to about 11. That is why
knowing the neutralisation point to within one drop is the precision ceiling
of the technique.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Describe how to carry out an acid–alkali titration" — six beats, in order:
measure the fixed volume with a pipette into a conical flask; add a few
drops of indicator; fill the burette with the other solution and note the
initial reading (two decimal places); run it in fast-then-dropwise with
swirling; stop at the first permanent colour change; record the final
reading and subtract. Practise questions hand you results tables — final
reading minus initial reading gives the volume delivered, and the table
usually shows the two-decimal law already applied.
:::

:::misconception
"The endpoint of a titration is the moment the solution becomes neutral."
Close, and wrong. The endpoint is defined by the *indicator's* colour
change; the neutral point is defined by the chemistry. They are close enough
for excellent results precisely because the pH leaps so steeply near
neutralisation — but they are not the same event, and the one-drop pH swing
from about 3 to about 11 is the evidence.
:::
