Very few carbonates dissolve in water — but two of the exceptions are
chemicals you will handle constantly. Sodium carbonate and potassium
carbonate both dissolve to give [[alkali]] solutions with a pH greater than
7. Where does the alkalinity come from? Not from any hydroxide in their
formula — Na₂CO₃ and K₂CO₃ contain carbonate, not hydroxide. The alkalinity
is the hydrolysis you met in the previous section: the dissolved carbonate
ions react with water and release [[hydroxide-ion]]s.

What makes these solutions only [[weakly-alkaline]] is a matter of supply.
Only some of the carbonate ions react with water at any moment, so the
hydroxide concentration stays modest — the solution climbs above pH 7 into
the weakly alkaline band (8–10), but never approaches the strength of sodium
hydroxide, which dissociates completely. It is a neat piece of evidence for
the band system: two alkalis, both above pH 7, occupying different bands for
a reason you can now state.

:::exam-hint
"Explain why sodium carbonate solution is only weakly alkaline" has a
two-step answer shape: carbonate ions react with water to produce hydroxide
ions (that is the alkalinity), but only partially — so the OH⁻
concentration stays low and the pH sits in the weakly alkaline band rather
than the strongly alkaline one. Naming the band earns the classification
mark; the partial-reaction explanation earns the chemistry mark.
:::

:::misconception
"All carbonates are alkalis." Solubility decides, and most metal carbonates
are insoluble — copper(II) carbonate will never form an alkaline solution
because it will not dissolve. Only the soluble ones, sodium and potassium
carbonate among them, can release hydroxide ions into water and count as
alkalis. The word "soluble" is doing real work in every carbonate
definition.
:::

:::
