Every [[acid]] has one structural signature: its molecules contain hydrogen,
and when the acid reacts it is that hydrogen which takes part — the
[[replaceable-hydrogen]]. In the catalogue you met in the previous section,
the replaceable hydrogens are the ones that can be swapped for something
else. React hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide and the swap is visible
in the equation:

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

The H of the HCl has been replaced by an Na, and water is what the displaced
hydrogen becomes. Sulfuric acid carries two replaceable hydrogens and
phosphoric acid three; hydrochloric and nitric acid carry one each.

But not every hydrogen in a formula is replaceable. Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH,
holds four hydrogens — yet only the one attached to the oxygen ever reacts.
The three bolted to the carbon never budge. (Where that O-attached hydrogen
family leads is Chapter 27's story: u4:c27, carboxylic acids.)

What acids do in water is the definition that earns the marks. In aqueous
solution an [[acid]] dissociates — it breaks apart — releasing [[hydrogen-ion]]s:

HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

This is why measuring pH is really measuring ions: when you measure the pH
of a solution you are measuring the concentration of H⁺ ions in it. It is
also why the H in pH is a capital H — the symbol is literally the hydrogen
it measures. And it gives the definition the mark scheme wants: acids are
substances that act as a source of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in solution.

:::exam-hint
The one-line acid definition is a reliable mark: "a source of hydrogen ions
(H⁺) in solution". Keep the H⁺ in brackets and keep the word *source* —
"acids contain hydrogen" is true but scores nothing, because the marks are
for the ions the acid releases in water. If a question asks why pH carries
a capital H, the answer runs through this same idea: pH measures the
hydrogen ion concentration.
:::

:::misconception
"Every hydrogen in an acid's formula takes part in its reactions." Only the
replaceable hydrogens do. In ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, three of the four
hydrogens are attached to carbon and never react — only the hydrogen on the
oxygen is replaceable. Counting hydrogens in a formula and counting
reactive hydrogens are different skills, and confusing them loses marks in
both directions.
:::
