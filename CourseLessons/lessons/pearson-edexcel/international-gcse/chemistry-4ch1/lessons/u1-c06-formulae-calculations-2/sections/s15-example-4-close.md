The chapter's last worked example runs the stages backwards: instead of a
concentration, the unknown is a **volume** of solution. *Calculate the volume
of 0.100 mol/dm³ sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) solution needed to
neutralise 20.0 cm³ of 0.125 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid (HCl).*

NaHCO₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

**Stage 1.** Only the acid is fully known: moles of HCl = (20.0 ÷ 1000) ×
0.125 = **0.00250 mol** (the book: *we do not have enough information to
calculate the number of moles of anything else, so must start here*).

**Stage 2.** The equation is 1 : 1, so the same **0.00250 mol** of NaHCO₃ is
needed.

**Stage 3 — volume instead of mass or concentration.** Rearrange the
concentration equation: volume = moles ÷ concentration = 0.00250 ÷ 0.100 =
**0.0250 dm³** (the volume comes out in dm³ because the concentration is in
mol/dm³), then × 1000 for the answer the question wants: **25.0 cm³** of
sodium hydrogencarbonate solution.

The chapter closes on the book's end-of-chapter question bank (pp. 72–74):
sixteen questions spanning every skill this Lesson built — gas volumes at
rtp (including the potassium nitrate and chlorine preparation problems),
g/dm³ ↔ mol/dm³ conversions, mass-from-concentration problems, and a full
titration set. Those questions live in the student book, not here.

:::summary
In one page: **gases** — Avogadro's law says equal volumes (same temperature
and pressure) hold equal molecules, so 1 mol of *any* gas occupies 24 dm³
(24 000 cm³) at rtp; moles = volume ÷ molar volume (matching cm³ to 24 000
and dm³ to 24), and gas volumes react in the equation's big-number ratio.
**Solutions** — concentration is per dm³: g/dm³ ÷ Mᵣ = mol/dm³ (and × Mᵣ
back); moles = volume (÷1000) × concentration. **Everywhere** — the
three-stage method: moles of what you can, equation ratio, convert to what
is asked. Titration = the experiment that measures the reacting volumes.
:::

:::exam-hint
The Paper-2 pattern across this chapter: one gas-volume calculation
(molar volume at rtp, usually via an equation) and one solution
concentration calculation (usually a titration) are near-permanent fixtures.
Both are three-stage questions — full marks come from the *layout*, not
luck: convert first (÷1000 or ÷ molar volume), quote the ratio from the
equation, rearrange last, units on every line.
:::

:::misconception
"Example 4 needs a new method because the answer is a volume of *solution*."
It is the same rearrangement as the molar-volume triangle, pointed at the
solution triangle: moles ÷ concentration = volume in dm³. The only genuinely
new content in the whole chapter was two conversion constants — the molar
volume and the mol/dm³ definition — and both now sit inside the same
three-stage method you brought from part 1.
:::
