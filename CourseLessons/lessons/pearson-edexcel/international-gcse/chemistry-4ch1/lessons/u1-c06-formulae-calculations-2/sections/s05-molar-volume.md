Avogadro's law has one consequence that is the most important in the whole
chapter: since 1 mole of any gas contains the same number of molecules
as 1 mole of any other gas, it must occupy **the same volume**. At room
temperature and pressure ([[rtp]] — usually 20–25 °C and 1 atmosphere) that
volume is approximately **24 dm³, or 24 000 cm³**. This is the
**[[molar-volume]]**. It belongs to the conditions, not to the gas: 1 mole of
hydrogen, 1 mole of chlorine, 1 mole of carbon dioxide — 24 dm³ each at rtp.
(Extension: strictly the molar volume has units of dm³/mol — a unit you
are unlikely to meet at International GCSE.)

Two triangles convert between volume and moles the same way the Chapter-5
mass triangle does:

- **volume = number of moles × molar volume**
- **number of moles = volume of gas ÷ molar volume**

Worked straight through: the volume of 0.20 mol of CO₂ at rtp is
0.20 × 24 = **4.8 dm³** (using 24 because the answer is wanted in dm³). Going
the other way, 0.01 g of hydrogen (Aᵣ: H = 1) is 0.01 ÷ 2 = 0.005 mol, which
occupies 0.005 × 24 000 = **120 cm³** — using 24 000 because the question
asks for cm³. And 120 cm³ of carbon dioxide is 120 ÷ 24 000 = **0.005 mol**.
Same triangle every time; the only decision is which size of the molar
volume to substitute.

Now run the full three-stage method — *calculate the moles of
what you can, use the equation to deduce the moles of what you want, convert
to the quantity asked* — on a gas-volume version of the Chapter-5 reacting-mass
problem: **what volume of CO₂ forms when excess dilute hydrochloric acid is
added to 1.00 g of calcium carbonate?** (Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40; molar
volume = 24 dm³ at rtp.) The equation is
CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l). The acid is in
**[[excess]]** — more than enough to react with all the calcium carbonate —
so the CaCO₃ sets the yield. Mᵣ of CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100, so
1.00 g is 1.00 ÷ 100 = 0.0100 mol. The equation says 1 mol CaCO₃ produces
1 mol CO₂, so 0.0100 mol of CO₂ forms; converting with the molar volume in
dm³ gives 0.0100 × 24 = **0.24 dm³**. The closing warning is the
exam-hint below.

:::key-point
1 mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at rtp — the same for every
gas. The unit law: volume given in cm³ → use 24 000 cm³; volume given in
dm³ → use 24 dm³.
:::

:::exam-hint
"It is really important to include the units here because the examiner will
not know whether you mean cm³ or dm³ unless you write it down" — the
whole point for the 0.0100 × 24 = 0.24 dm³ answer. Quote the unit with
every intermediate and final volume, and match the constant to the unit
before you multiply, not after.
:::

:::misconception
"24 dm³ is a property of the gas — hydrogen occupies a different molar volume
from chlorine." At the same temperature and pressure, no: the molar volume is
fixed by Avogadro's law, not by the gas's identity. What *does* change with
conditions is the value itself — 24 dm³ is an **rtp** number; at STP (0 °C,
1 atm) the molar volume is different. Read the conditions in the question and
use the value the question pins (every 4CH1 question at rtp gives or implies
24 dm³ / 24 000 cm³).
:::
