The gas-volume half of the chapter opens with one of the most striking ideas
in the course: Figure 6.2 shows three identical flasks holding **different
gases** at the same temperature and pressure — and all three contain **equal
numbers of molecules**. Not similar numbers: equal. The identity of the gas
and the size of its molecules simply do not matter.

That single figure is the whole foundation for this half-chapter. If equal
volumes hold equal numbers of molecules, then volumes of gases tell you
directly about **numbers of molecules** — and since moles are just counted
molecules (the Avogadro constant of them per mole), volumes of gases tell you
about **moles** with no Mᵣ and no weighing step at all. The next section
names this statement properly (Avogadro's law) and starts using it on real
equations.

:::exam-hint
When a question gives you volumes of gases and a balanced equation, the
volumes are already "in moles" at the same ratio — do **not** convert them to
masses first. The book's methane example on the next section walks the full
reasoning: 100 cm³ of methane needs exactly 200 cm³ of oxygen and yields
exactly 100 cm³ of carbon dioxide, because the big numbers say 1 : 2 : 1.
:::

:::misconception
"Flaskfuls of chlorine must contain more molecules than flaskfuls of hydrogen
— chlorine molecules are bigger and heavier." Size and mass are properties of
each molecule, not of the space between molecules. In a gas the molecules
occupy a negligible fraction of the container; the volume is almost entirely
empty space, and at the same temperature and pressure every gas spaces its
molecules identically. Equal volumes → equal molecule counts, every time.
:::
