Everything so far has been calculation technique; the **[[titration]]** is
the experiment that feeds it. A titration finds out *how much of one solution
reacts with a certain volume of another solution of known concentration*.
The book's apparatus layout: a solution of alkali is measured into a conical
flask with a **[[pipette]]** (one accurate, fixed volume), and the acid is
run in from a **[[burette]]** (a variable, read-off volume), swirling the
flask constantly. Towards the end the acid goes in a drop at a time until
the indicator just changes colour — the **[[endpoint]]** (Figure 6.9 shows
the endpoint of a titration using methyl orange). Acid–alkali titration
technique itself is developed in Chapters 16 and 17; here it is the *numbers*
that matter.

The logic of the calculation: if you know the concentration of either the
acid or the alkali, the titration hands you the other piece of the pair — a
volume of solution of known concentration reacting exactly with a volume of
the unknown. That is precisely one volume × concentration term and one
unknown concentration: the standard three stages with the burette readings
as stage-1 data.

The book's two HINTs on titration practice are examiner-neutral and worth
pinning now, before the standard calculation uses them.

:::exam-hint
The book's two hints, verbatim in substance: (1) *the acid and alkali can be
added the other way round, with the alkali in the burette* — the
concentration calculation does not care which reagent sat where, only which
volumes reacted; (2) *remember to divide the volume by 1000 to convert to
dm³* — burette and pipette readings come in cm³, and every concentration in
this family is per dm³.
:::

:::misconception
"The endpoint tells you the acid and alkali are equal." The endpoint tells
you the *reaction is just complete* — the reacting amounts are in the
**equation's mole ratio**, which is 1 : 1 for NaOH + HCl but 2 : 1 for
2NaOH + H₂SO₄. Equal volumes of the two solutions are a coincidence of
concentrations, not a law; the mole ratio, never the volume equality, drives
stage 2.
:::
