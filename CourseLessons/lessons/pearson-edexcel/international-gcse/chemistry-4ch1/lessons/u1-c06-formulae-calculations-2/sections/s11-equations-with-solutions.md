Now the two halves of the chapter merge: solution arithmetic feeding the
three-stage method through a balanced equation. The context is domestic
chemistry — limescale (calcium carbonate) removed from kettles by a
dilute acid, such as the ethanoic acid in vinegar:

CaCO₃(s) + 2CH₃COOH(aq) → (CH₃COO)₂Ca(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

*What mass of calcium carbonate can be removed by 50 cm³ of a solution of
ethanoic acid that has a concentration of 2 mol/dm³? (Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16,
Ca = 40.)*

**Stage 1 — moles of what we can.** We know the volume and concentration of
the acid: convert the volume to dm³ by dividing by 1000, then
number of moles of ethanoic acid = (50 ÷ 1000) × 2 = **0.1 mol**.

**Stage 2 — equation ratio.** The equation has a **2** in front of the
CH₃COOH but no number (meaning 1) in front of the CaCO₃, so 2 mol of acid
react with 1 mol of CaCO₃ — the moles of CaCO₃ are **half** the moles of
acid: 0.1 ÷ 2 = **0.05 mol** of CaCO₃.

**Stage 3 — convert to the asked quantity.** Mᵣ of CaCO₃ = 100, so
mass = 0.05 × 100 = **5 g** of calcium carbonate removed.

This is the same skeleton as the hydrogen problem (s06) and the CaCO₃
problem (s05) — only the stage-1 conversion changes, from molar volume to
volume × concentration. "Again, we will use our three stages" is
deliberate: by now the method, not the chemistry, is the transferable skill.

:::exam-hint
The ratio extraction deserves its own written line: "2 mol CH₃COOH : 1 mol
CaCO₃, so moles CaCO₃ = moles acid ÷ 2". Mark schemes award the ratio line
even when the arithmetic fails, and the half-it habit (÷2 when your substance
has the bigger coefficient) is safer than cross-multiplying in your head.
State the equation's big numbers before using them.
:::

:::misconception
"The acid's coefficient 2 means the acid is twice as concentrated, so use
2 mol/dm³ differently." No — the 2 is already spent the moment the
concentration is multiplied in: 0.050 dm³ × 2 mol/dm³ is 0.1 mol **of acid**,
full stop. The coefficient re-enters only in stage 2 as the reacting ratio
between the substances. Never fold equation coefficients into the
concentration; keep stage 1 (how many moles of *this* solution) and stage 2
(how the *substances* relate) cleanly apart.
:::
