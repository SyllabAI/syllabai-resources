The book's Example 1 runs the × Mᵣ direction of the bridge. *What is the
concentration of a 0.050 mol/dm³ solution of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, in
g/dm³? (Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23.)*

The book reasons per dm³ of solution, which is the cleanest way to see the
units do the work:

1. 1 dm³ of solution contains **0.050 mol** of Na₂CO₃.
2. Mᵣ of Na₂CO₃ = (2 × 23) + 12 + (3 × 16) = **106**, so 1 mol weighs 106 g.
3. 0.050 mol weighs 0.050 × 106 = **5.3 g**.
4. 1 dm³ of solution contains 5.3 g — so the concentration is **5.3 g/dm³**.

Notice the structure: the question was per-dm³ in, per-dm³ out, and the only
arithmetic was the moles → grams conversion you have used since Chapter 5.
Every "convert a mol/dm³ concentration into g/dm³" question in the paper is
this four-line shape with different numbers.

:::exam-hint
Mark-scheme-friendly layout: write the Mᵣ line explicitly ((2 × 23) + 12 +
(3 × 16) = 106) — the Mᵣ itself usually carries a mark — then the multiply
line with units. The × Mᵣ direction check: mol/dm³ → g/dm³ must make the
number **bigger** (you are counting formula-unit masses); if your g/dm³
answer comes out smaller than your mol/dm³ number, you divided instead.
:::

:::misconception
"0.050 mol/dm³ is a small number, so 0.050 g/dm³ is the answer." The two
units are not interchangeable decimals — the Mᵣ bridges them and it is never
1. Here 0.050 mol/dm³ becomes 5.3 g/dm³, a hundredfold bigger, because each
formula unit has a mass of 106 g. Always × or ÷ by the Mᵣ; never equate the
two numbers.
:::
