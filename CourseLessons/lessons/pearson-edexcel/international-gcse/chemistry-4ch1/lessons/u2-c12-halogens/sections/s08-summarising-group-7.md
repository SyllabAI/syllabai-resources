Everything the chapter taught, compressed onto one card. The Group 7
elements:

- have **[[diatomic]]** molecules, X₂ (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂);
- go from **gas to liquid to solid** as you descend the group — melting
  points and boiling points increase;
- have **coloured, poisonous vapours** — the colours darken down the
  group;
- form compounds of formula **HX** with hydrogen (HF, HCl, HBr, HI),
  which dissolve in water to form **acids**;
- form **ionic [[salt]]s with metals** and **covalent compounds with
  non-metals**;
- form **X⁻ ions** (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻) in their ionic compounds;
- become **less reactive** towards the bottom of the group;
- **displace elements lower down** the group from their salts.

Each line is a lever for prediction, and prediction is the last skill
this chapter hands you. Take **astatine** — the element below iodine, so
rare and radioactive that no one has ever seen a sample of it. You have
never observed astatine, and you do not need to: every property above
extrapolates down one more rung. Astatine will be:

- a **darker colour than iodine** — very dark grey or black;
- a **solid** at room temperature, with a **higher melting point** than
  iodine's;
- **diatomic**, existing as At₂ molecules;
- reactive with hydrogen to form **HAt**, which dissolves in water to
  form an **acid**;
- a salt-former with metals — **NaAt** with sodium, **KAt** with
  potassium;
- built around the **astatide ion, At⁻**, in its ionic salts;
- **less reactive than iodine**;
- **displaced from solution by iodine**.

Notice what happened there: eight properties, zero experiments. The
trend in physical properties predicted the state, the colour, the
melting point; the reaction families predicted the formulas (HAt, NaAt,
KAt, At⁻); the displacement ladder predicted who beats whom in solution.
That is the real power of a chemical family — knowledge of a few members
plus the trend is knowledge of all of them, and this exact move (trend +
application) is what prediction questions on Paper 2 are built from.

:::summary
Group 7 in one breath: **diatomic, coloured, poisonous non-metals** that
go gas → liquid → solid and darker down the group; strong intermolecular
forces make melting and boiling points rise (no covalent bonds break);
they form HX acids with hydrogen, ionic salts with metals, covalent
molecules with non-metals, always carrying X⁻ in salts; reactivity
**falls** down the group because the incoming electron lands further from
the nucleus — so any halogen displaces the halogens below it from their
salt solutions, and astatine below iodine is blacker, more solid, less
reactive, and beatable by everything above it.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The halogens work far outside the lab. Chlorine's poisonous reactivity is
put to work in water treatment, where trace chlorine kills bacteria in
drinking water and swimming pools — dilution flips the hazard. Fluoride
compounds strengthen tooth enamel against decay. Silver bromide's
light-sensitivity underpinned photographic film for over a century. And
the family's readiness to form salts explains why iodide is added to
table salt in many countries to prevent thyroid disease.
:::

:::exam-hint
Prediction questions are template questions. The formula: **state the
trend, then apply it with a comparison word**. "Astatine will be less
reactive than iodine because reactivity decreases down Group 7" — trend
named, direction named, comparison explicit — is full marks. Answers
that predict correctly but never cite the trend ("it will be a black
solid") collect the property mark and drop the reasoning mark. And in
displacement predictions, remember who displaces whom: astatine is
displaced *by* iodine, never the reverse.
:::

:::misconception
"Astatine's properties are unknowable — no one has ever seen it." The
elements below iodine in the group were all *predicted* before they were
ever isolated, for exactly the reason this chapter works: group position
plus measured trends fix the properties. Astatine's state, colour,
melting point, molecular formula, salt formulas and reactivity all follow
from lines you can already recite. "Never seen a sample" is a statement
about rarity and radioactivity, not about knowledge.
:::
