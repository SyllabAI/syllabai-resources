Before any halogen reaction, the hazards — because this family is the
most dangerous set of elements you will meet on this course. **Fluorine**
is so dangerously reactive that you would never expect to find it in a
school lab at all; the reactions it drives are violent beyond anything a
teaching room is equipped to control. The school-taught members —
chlorine, bromine, iodine — are manageable, but only inside a
discipline: **all the halogens have extremely poisonous vapours**, so
every experiment with them is carried out in a **[[fume-cupboard]]**,
which pulls the toxic vapours away from the air you breathe.

Liquid **bromine** adds a second hazard: it is **very corrosive**, and
great care is taken to keep it off the skin. A drop of bromine on bare
skin burns chemically — that is what corrosive means — so bromine
handling is gloves, goggles and a fume cupboard, every time. Chlorine is
a gas with a choking, poisonous vapour; iodine is the gentlest of the
three but still stains and irritates, and its vapour is not something to
breathe by choice.

The safety picture is itself part of the chemistry. The same high
reactivity that makes halogens superb salt-formers makes their vapours
destructive to living tissue, and reactivity (like the boiling points)
tracks the group trend — fluorine at the top is the worst of the family,
which is precisely why it has no place in a school lab.

:::safety
Fluorine is far too dangerously reactive for school use — expect never to
see it in a teaching lab. Apart from the reactivity of the elements
(especially fluorine and chlorine), **all halogen vapours are extremely
poisonous**: halogen work belongs in a fume cupboard. **Liquid bromine is
very corrosive** — keep it off the skin, wear eye protection and gloves,
and treat any spill as a stop-work event.
:::

:::exam-hint
Hazard questions are free marks if you use the right register. The mark
scheme wants the formal hazard words — **toxic** (poisonous vapours),
**corrosive** (bromine attacks skin) — paired with the matching control
measure: experiments in a fume cupboard because the vapours are poisonous.
Writing "dangerous" or "not nice" earns nothing; writing "chlorine has a
toxic vapour, so the experiment is carried out in a fume cupboard" earns
both marks every time.
:::

:::misconception
"Chlorine can't be that dangerous — swimming pools use it." The chemical
in the pool is not the element; it is a vanishingly dilute solution of
chlorine compounds that kill bacteria at parts per million. The element
chlorine is a toxic green gas that attacked soldiers in the First World
War. Same family, utterly different scales — and the dilution is exactly
the point: hazards live with concentration, not with the name of the
element.
:::
