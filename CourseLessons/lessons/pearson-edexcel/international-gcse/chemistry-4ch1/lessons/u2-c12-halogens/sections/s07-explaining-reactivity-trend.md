Here is the Paper-2 crown of the chapter: *why* does reactivity fall down
Group 7? The answer runs through how a halogen reacts. In the reactions of
this chapter, a halogen reacts by **forming a 1− ion** — it takes
electrons away from something else (a metal atom, or a [[halide-ion]] in
solution). So reactivity measures how eagerly the atom can *gain* an
electron, and that eagerness depends on one geometric fact: the size of
the atom.

A chlorine atom is **smaller** than a bromine atom. Add an electron to
each and the chlorine's new electron enters a shell **closer to the
nucleus** — chlorine has fewer filled shells in the way. An electron in a
shell closer to the nucleus is **more strongly attracted** to it. So
chlorine pulls the incoming electron in more strongly, forms its 1− ion
more readily, and therefore has the stronger tendency to react: chlorine
out-reacts bromine, and will take an electron away from a bromide ion.
Down the group the atoms grow shell by shell, the incoming electron sits
further from the nucleus, the attraction weakens, electron-gaining gets
harder — and reactivity falls: Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

This is the mirror image of the Group 1 story from Chapter 11 (u2:c11).
There, reactivity *rose* down the group because losing an electron gets
*easier* as the outer electron sits further out. Here, reactivity *falls*
down the group because gaining one gets *harder*. Same physics —
outer-shell distance controls electrostatics — opposite directions,
because the families do opposite jobs with the electron.

:::exam-hint
The explanation question (spec 2.8C, Paper 2 only) pays out in a fixed
chain — write all four links: (1) halogens react by **gaining one
electron** to form a 1− ion; (2) down the group the atoms get **bigger**,
so the new electron enters a shell **further from the nucleus**; (3) the
attraction it feels is **weaker**; (4) so the atom gains the electron
**less readily** — reactivity decreases. Answers that skip link 1 (how
halogens react) or that write only "bigger atoms are less reactive" drop
half the marks: the mark scheme wants the mechanism, not the slogan.
:::

:::misconception
"Bromine should attract a new electron more strongly than chlorine — its
nucleus has 35 protons against chlorine's 17." The proton count is not
what the incoming electron feels. The inner, filled shells shield it:
chlorine's 17 protons minus 10 inner-shell electrons leave the newcomer
feeling a net pull of **7+**, and bromine's 35 protons minus 28 inner
electrons leave it feeling the **same 7+**. With equal pull, the contest
is decided by **distance** — and chlorine's incoming electron starts
closer, so it wins. (This shielding argument is extension territory: the
required-at-IG explanation is the size-and-distance chain above.)
:::
