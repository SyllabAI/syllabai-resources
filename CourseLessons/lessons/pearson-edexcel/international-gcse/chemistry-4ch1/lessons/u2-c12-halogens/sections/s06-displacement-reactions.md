The demos in the previous section generalise into a rule, and the rule
has three parts. **First, the rule itself:** a more reactive halogen
displaces a less reactive halogen from its salt solution — and the
reverse never happens. Test the impossible directions and nothing occurs:

- bromine + potassium chloride solution: **no reaction** — the less
  reactive bromine cannot displace the more reactive chlorine;
- iodine + potassium chloride solution: **no reaction**;
- iodine + potassium bromide solution: **no reaction**.

Every "no visible change" here is as informative as a colour change: the
displacement ladder Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ is confirmed by the reactions that
*fail* as much as by the ones that work.

**Second, what the metal ion is doing: nothing.** Remember that when an
ionic compound dissolves in water, it splits into its ions. So potassium
bromide solution is really a soup of K⁺(aq) and Br⁻(aq) ions, and when
chlorine reacts with it, the chlorine attacks the **bromide ion** — the
potassium ion never changes. It would make no difference if we used sodium
bromide or magnesium bromide instead: the reaction happens between the
chlorine molecule and the bromide ion, and the metal ion is a
**[[spectator-ion]]**. Write the chlorine + potassium iodide reaction
showing every charge and you can see it:

2K⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) + Cl₂(aq) → 2K⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

The K⁺ ions stand identical on both sides — untouched. Delete them and
you get the equation that matters, the **[[ionic-equation]]**:

Cl₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

Same surgery on the bromide reaction gives:

Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq)

and for bromine against iodide:

Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

**Third, these are redox reactions.** Remind yourself of the two
definitions: **oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain**. In the
chlorine + bromide reaction the chlorine takes electrons *away from* the
bromide ions — so it **oxidises** them, while the Cl₂ molecules **gain**
electrons and are reduced to Cl⁻. The electron-thief is called an
**[[oxidising-agent]]**: it oxidises something else by taking its
electrons, and because it accepts those electrons it is itself reduced.
The robbed party — the bromide ion, which gave electrons away and was
oxidised — played the matching role of **[[reducing-agent]]**. Every
halogen displacement reaction is a [[redox-reaction]] with the halogen
molecule as oxidising agent and the halide ion as reducing agent, and the
oxidising strength runs down the group exactly as reactivity does:
chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent than bromine, which is stronger
than iodine. Chlorine can oxidise bromide *and* iodide ions; bromine can
oxidise iodide ions but not chloride ions; iodine can oxidise neither.

One asymmetry to carry carefully: this "most reactive = strongest
oxidising agent" logic works for **non-metals only**. For metals the
ordering flips purpose — the most reactive metal is the best *reducing*
agent. The difference traces to the ions each family forms: non-metals
react to form negative ions (they take electrons), metals react to form
positive ions (they give them away).

And fluorine gets one honest footnote. The trend explanation coming in the
next section would predict, correctly, that fluorine out-reacts chlorine
— but the true microscopic reason does *not* fit the family pattern:
fluorine atoms do not actually accept electrons more easily than chlorine
atoms do. Its exceptional violence traces to the unusually weak bond
between the F₂ atoms. At International GCSE you will not be asked to
explain why fluorine beats chlorine; the standard group-trend argument is
the accepted answer at this level if the question ever appears.

:::exam-hint
Ionic equations are the mark-scheme's favourite way to test whether you
understand displacement or merely watched it. The recipe: write the full
equation with all ions and state symbols, strike out the spectators that
appear unchanged on both sides, keep the rest balanced and charged —
charges must balance too (2− on the left as 2I⁻, 2− on the right as 2Cl⁻).
"Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂" with state symbols and balanced charges is a
full-credit answer; including K⁺ is not — it signals you have not found
the [[spectator-ion]].
:::

:::misconception
"In chlorine + potassium bromide, the potassium is displaced because it
is the less reactive partner." Nothing happens to the potassium at all.
Displacement is a contest **between halogens**: the chlorine molecule
takes electrons from **bromide ions**, and the K⁺ ions drift through the
solution unchanged on both sides. The metal ion is the stage, not a
performer — that is precisely why the ionic equation can leave it out
without losing any chemistry.
:::
