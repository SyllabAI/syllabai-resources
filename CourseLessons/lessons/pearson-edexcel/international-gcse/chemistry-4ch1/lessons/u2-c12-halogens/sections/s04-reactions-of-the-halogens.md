Two reaction families carry the halogens' chemistry, and both are
formula-patterns you can write for any member of the group. Learn the
pattern once, and every individual equation is a substitution exercise.

**Reaction 1: with hydrogen.** Every halogen combines with hydrogen to
form a **[[hydrogen-halide]]**, HX — hydrogen fluoride, hydrogen chloride,
hydrogen bromide, hydrogen iodide:

H₂(g) + Br₂(g) → 2HBr(g)

The hydrogen halides are all **acidic, poisonous gases**. In common with
every compound formed between two non-metals, they are covalent molecular
substances — and, like all acids, they dissolve in water to give acidic
solutions. Hydrochloric acid *is* hydrogen chloride dissolved in water;
the same gas-to-acid story gives hydrofluoric, hydrobromic and hydroiodic
acids. The gas and the solution are different substances with different
structures (molecules in the gas, ions in the solution) — the distinction
the previous section's key point pinned down.

**Reaction 2: with metals.** React a halogen with a metal and you form an
ionic **[[salt]]**. Sodium burning in chlorine is the demonstration to
picture: it burns with its typical yellow flame and the product is white,
solid sodium chloride,

2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)

an ionic solid built from Na⁺ and Cl⁻. The pattern generalises across the
Group 1 and Group 2 metals: when halogens react with these metals, the
metal atoms lose electrons and the halogen atoms gain them, so the
products are ionic — M⁺ and X⁻ packed into a lattice. With non-metals,
by contrast, halogens form covalent molecular compounds (the hydrogen
halides are the standing example). Metal + halogen = ionic; non-metal +
halogen = molecular. That single sentence sorts every halogen product on
the course.

One caution about scope. Most of the group chemistry you meet — the
reactions above, the displacement work coming next — is taught through
the three commonly used halogens, chlorine, bromine and iodine. The
patterns do not stop there: the trend runs on through the rest of the
group, which is what lets you make confident statements about fluorine
above them and astatine below them. Fluorine's extra violence is real but
unprintable in a school context; astatine's behaviour is invisible but
predictable. The three lab halogens are the family's public face.

:::key-point
Chlorine, bromine and iodine are the three commonly used halogens of
school chemistry — but the trends continue for the **rest of the group**.
Reactivity keeps rising towards fluorine and keeps falling towards
astatine; every family pattern you meet in this chapter transfers to the
members you never see.
:::

:::exam-hint
Equation marks live in the details: state symbols and balancing. The
hydrogen halides form from gaseous hydrogen and gaseous halogen
(H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)), and sodium's reaction needs the 2:2:2
balancing (2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)) because chlorine is a **diatomic**
molecule — one Cl₂ delivers two chloride ions. Candidates who write
Na + Cl₂ → NaCl lose the balancing mark on every version of this
question; the diatomic habit is worth practising until it is automatic.
:::

:::misconception
"Hydrochloric acid must be an ionic compound, because acids contain
H⁺ ions." The ions appear only in *solution*. Dry hydrogen chloride gas
is a simple molecular substance — covalent H–Cl molecules, no ions, no
conduction. Dissolve it in water and the molecules react with the water
to form H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq). The acid behaviour belongs to the solution;
the compound in the gas state is molecular like every other non-metal +
non-metal product.
:::
