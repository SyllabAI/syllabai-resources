The atoms of fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine all have
**7 electrons in their outer shell**, and that is the whole reason they are
grouped together. The number of outer-shell electrons determines how an
atom reacts, so every Group 7 element reacts in a similar way — and the
reactions all carry the family signature: forming compounds that contain
the **X⁻ ion**, the halogen having gained one electron.

The elements themselves are non-metallic and **[[diatomic]]** — their
molecules are pairs of atoms, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. Memorise the physical
profile the family walks through as the molecules get heavier:

| Halogen | Formula | Physical state at room temperature | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluorine | F₂ | gas | yellow |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | gas | green |
| Bromine | Br₂ | liquid | red-brown liquid, orange/brown vapour |
| Iodine | I₂ | solid | grey solid, purple vapour |

Read the table as a trend, not a list. Down the group the melting points
and boiling points **increase** — gas → liquid → solid — and the colour
becomes **darker**. Both trends have one cause. The halogens are all
covalent molecular substances, and as the relative molecular mass
increases down the group the **[[intermolecular-forces]]** of attraction
between molecules become stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome
them. That is what melting and boiling actually are: separating molecules
from each other, not breaking the covalent bonds inside them. No covalent
bond is broken when iodine melts — the I–I bonds within each I₂ molecule
survive intact.

Being non-metals, the halogens are poor conductors of heat and
electricity, in both their solid and liquid states — there are no
delocalised electrons to carry charge, unlike the metallic bonding of
Chapter 9 (u1:c09).

One member needs special handling: **astatine**. It is radioactive,
formed during the radioactive decay of other elements such as uranium and
thorium, and most of its isotopes are so unstable that a sample decays
away almost as fast as it forms — which is why no one has ever assembled
a visible quantity of it. Everything you can say about astatine in an
exam is a *prediction* from the group trend, which is exactly what the
closing section of this chapter trains you to do.

:::key-point
Hydrogen chloride the gas and hydrochloric acid the solution are **two
different substances**. Hydrogen chloride is a gas of covalent HCl
molecules; hydrochloric acid is a solution containing H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
The other [[hydrogen-halide]]s follow the same pattern when dissolved:
HF(aq) is hydrofluoric acid, HBr(aq) is hydrobromic acid, HI(aq) is
hydroiodic acid. Gas = molecules; solution = ions.
:::

:::exam-hint
The melting-point explanation is a three-mark skeleton, and the mark
scheme pays each bone separately: (1) the intermolecular forces of
attraction get **stronger** down the group as the relative molecular mass
increases; (2) therefore **more energy** is needed to overcome them;
(3) the covalent bonds within the molecules are **not** broken. Answers
that say "bigger atoms have stronger bonds" score zero — the bonds being
overcome are *between* molecules, not inside them.
:::

:::misconception
"Iodine must have strong covalent bonds, because it is a solid at room
temperature." Swap the word *covalent* for *intermolecular* and the
sentence becomes correct. Iodine is a solid because its molecules attract
each other strongly enough that room temperature cannot pull the lattice
apart — but those I₂ molecules are held together inside by ordinary
covalent bonds, and those bonds never break on melting or boiling. State
changes in molecular substances are a story about **[[intermolecular-forces]]**,
never about the covalent bonds themselves.
:::
