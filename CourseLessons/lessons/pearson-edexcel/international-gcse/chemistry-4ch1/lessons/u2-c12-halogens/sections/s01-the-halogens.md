Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine sit together in **Group 7 of
the Periodic Table**, and the group has a name of its own: the
**[[halogen]]**s. At first sight the members look almost nothing like one
another — one is a pale gas, one a dense coloured liquid, one a shiny grey
solid — and part of this chapter is learning to see the family underneath
the differences.

Hold on to one idea from the start: the outer shell. Every Group 7 atom
carries **7 electrons in its outer shell**, and the outer shell is where
all chemistry happens. That single shared fact is why these five elements
react in similar ways, form similar compounds and show neat, predictable
trends as you descend the group — exactly the pattern you already saw for
Group 1 in Chapter 11 (u2:c11), now played out on the non-metal side of
the table.

The name *halogen* means **"salt-forming"**, and it is earned: react any
halogen with a metal and you get a **[[salt]]**. Calcium fluoride in tap
water chemistry, sodium chloride on your chips, silver bromide in
photographic film, potassium iodide in the lab — all of them are halogen
salts, and every one of them contains the same unit: the halide ion, **X⁻**
(where X stands for any halogen). One letter, one charge, one family.

Two pieces of machinery before the detail. First, the halogens are
**non-metallic** and exist as **[[diatomic]]** molecules — F₂, Cl₂, Br₂,
I₂ — two atoms bonded into one molecule, never lone atoms in the element.
Second, this chapter's evidence engine is the **displacement reaction**:
pitting one halogen against another's salt solution and watching which one
wins. Those reactions are the experimental proof that reactivity *falls*
down the group, and they are where the chapter earns its exam marks.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to state the colours and
physical states of the halogens at room temperature and explain the
physical-property trends; use the group trends to predict the properties
of halogens you have never met; carry out and interpret halogen
displacement reactions, including their ionic equations; and — for Paper 2
chemistry-only content — explain the reactivity trend in terms of
electronic configurations. Each of those builds on the last, so the order
above is the order to learn in.

:::misconception
"The halogens are called salt-formers, so salts are a kind of halogen."
The arrow points the other way. A **[[salt]]** is an ionic compound made
when a metal reacts with a halogen (or with an acid); the halogen is one
*reactant*, not the product category. The halogen itself is either a
reactive molecular element (Cl₂) or, inside its salts, the negative ion
X⁻ — the sodium in sodium chloride is as essential to that salt as the
chlorine is.
:::
