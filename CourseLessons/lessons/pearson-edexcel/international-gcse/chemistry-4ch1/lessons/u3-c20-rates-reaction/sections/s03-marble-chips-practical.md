Marble chips are calcium carbonate, and they react with dilute
hydrochloric acid to give carbon dioxide gas — which escapes — while
calcium chloride solution and water stay behind in the flask:

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

The measurement trick for this reaction is elegant: the carbon dioxide
leaves the flask, so **the flask gets lighter as the reaction proceeds**.
Put the whole reaction on a balance and the falling reading is a direct
record of gas produced. This is the experiment you will modify three times
in this chapter — with smaller chips (surface area), with more dilute acid
(concentration), and with a warmer acid (temperature) — so the method
deserves exact attention.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the acid.
:::

The procedure:

- Use a measuring cylinder to measure 25 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ dilute
  hydrochloric acid.
- Add 5.00 g of large marble chips to a conical flask and place a plug of
  cotton wool at the opening of the flask. The cotton wool lets carbon
  dioxide escape during the reaction but stops any acid spitting out. The
  marble is in [[excess]] — some of it will be left over when the acid has
  all been used up.
- Place everything on a balance and reset the balance to zero.
- Add the acid to the marble chips and record the balance reading every
  30 seconds.

The balance reading goes **negative** as the reaction runs, because carbon
dioxide escapes through the cotton wool and the mixture is losing mass.
Plotting mass of carbon dioxide lost against time gives a curve that rises
steeply at first and then flattens — the shape you will spend this chapter
explaining.

**Reading the graph.** The steeper the slope (gradient) of the line, the
faster the reaction. On this experiment's curve, about
0.47 g of carbon dioxide is produced in the first minute, but only about
0.20 g of extra carbon dioxide arrives in the second minute — the reaction
is already slowing. The curve eventually becomes horizontal: the reaction
has stopped because the acid has been used up, while unreacted marble chips
still sit at the bottom of the flask.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, two method details earn marks by name: the **cotton wool**
(lets gas out, stops acid spray, and is why the mass falls without losing
liquid) and the **excess marble** (guarantees the acid is the limiting
reactant, so gas production stops when acid runs out). If asked why the
balance reading falls, answer "carbon dioxide escapes through the cotton
wool" — never "because the reaction loses mass" without naming the gas.
:::

:::misconception
"The balance measures the mass of carbon dioxide inside the flask." It
cannot — the carbon dioxide is the one substance *leaving*. The falling
reading is the mass of gas that has escaped; the flask still contains all
the calcium chloride, the water and the leftover marble. Students who write
"mass of CO₂ in the flask" mislabel the entire y-axis and lose the graph
interpretation marks that follow.
:::
