A [[catalyst]] is a substance that **increases the rate of a reaction but is
chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction**. Learn that definition
exactly — both halves get tested. Because catalysts are not consumed, a
small amount of catalyst can process a huge amount of reactant, and each
reaction has its own preferred catalyst: different reactions need different
catalysts.

The chapter's working example is the decomposition of
[[hydrogen-peroxide]] solution. Hydrogen peroxide is unstable and slowly
breaks down into water and oxygen:

2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

Left alone, it decomposes so slowly that only a trace of bubbles appears.
Add a pinch of black manganese(IV) oxide powder — MnO₂, also called
manganese dioxide — and oxygen streams off immediately. Lead(IV) oxide,
PbO₂, also catalyses this decomposition. Manganese(IV) oxide is what is
normally used in the lab for this reaction.

Notice what the equation does *not* contain: the catalyst. Catalysts are
never written into the equation, because they are chemically unchanged at
the end. If you want to show one, write its name or formula **over the top
of the arrow**.

**Bombardier beetles** run this chemistry for a living. The beetle defends
itself by spraying a hot, unpleasant liquid at attackers: it pumps hydrogen
peroxide into a chamber with the enzyme **catalase**, which decomposes it
into water and oxygen — almost explosively, releasing a lot of heat. An
[[enzyme]] is a biological catalyst, and the same catalase can be found in
potatoes and in liver tissue: drop a slice of either into hydrogen peroxide
and it fizzes.

:::key-point
The reaction here is an exothermic reaction: the products have lower energy
than the reactants.
:::

**Proving "chemically unchanged".** Looking the same proves nothing — the
powder could have lost mass. The test is by **weighing**: weigh the
manganese(IV) oxide before adding it to the hydrogen peroxide, then
separate, dry and reweigh it at the end. Filter the mixture through a
**weighed filter paper**, allow the paper and residue to dry, and reweigh.
The mass of manganese(IV) oxide you recover equals the mass you started
with. Speeding up *and* unchanged: both halves of the definition
demonstrated.

**Practical: comparing solid catalysts (spec 3.16).** Measure how the
volume of oxygen produced changes with time, using a gas syringe on a
sealed flask:

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the solution.
:::

- Measure 100 cm³ of 2 vol hydrogen peroxide and transfer it to a 250 cm³
  conical flask.
- Weigh out 0.20 g of manganese(IV) oxide on a balance.
- Add the manganese(IV) oxide to the hydrogen peroxide and quickly replace
  the bung, with the [[gas-syringe]] already attached. Swirl the reaction
  mixture at a constant speed.
- Record the volume of oxygen produced every 20 seconds for 3 minutes and
  plot volume of oxygen against time.
- Repeat with 0.20 g of lead(IV) oxide and 0.20 g of copper(II) oxide,
  keeping everything else the same.

The results show manganese(IV) oxide is a **very effective** catalyst for
this decomposition — the volume–time curve climbs steeply to its final
level. Lead(IV) oxide is **less effective**: the same final volume of
oxygen arrives, but the curve is much shallower. Copper(II) oxide is **not
a catalyst** for this reaction at all — its curve barely leaves the axis.
Same equation, three very different helpers: catalytic activity is
substance-specific.

The decomposition itself is exothermic — the products (water and oxygen)
have lower energy than the reactants — so the flask warms slightly as the
oxygen pours off.

:::did-you-know
*Vol*, meaning volume, is a measurement of concentration used for hydrogen
peroxide. 1 cm³ of 2 vol hydrogen peroxide solution decomposes completely
to give 2 cm³ of oxygen. So "2 vol" tells you the oxygen yield directly —
100 cm³ of 2 vol solution can deliver 200 cm³ of gas.
:::

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the catalyst-confirmation question always wants **two**
pieces of evidence: (1) the reaction is faster than without the candidate
solid, and (2) the solid is recovered **unchanged** — same mass, by
filtering through weighed filter paper, drying and reweighing. Providing
only "it makes bubbles come faster" answers half the definition. And when
asked where a catalyst appears in the equation, the answer is: it doesn't
— or above the arrow, never with the reactants or products.
:::

:::misconception
"A catalyst is used up slowly, which is why industrial plants top it up."
A catalyst that were consumed would be a reactant. Catalysts are chemically
unchanged at the end — they may look dirtier, clump, or be **poisoned** by
impurities, which is why plants replace them — but consumption by the
reaction itself is ruled out by definition. The reweighing experiment in
this section is exactly the evidence that settles the question.
:::
