Repeat the original marble-chip experiment — large chips, same mass (5.00 g),
same acid volume (25 cm³) — but use hydrochloric acid of **half the
concentration**: 25 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ instead of 2.00 mol/dm³. The
reaction is **slower**. On the mass-loss graph, the half-concentration run
is the less steep curve: same chemistry, gentler climb.

Remember, the marble chips were in [[excess]], so the smaller amount of
acid is what limits the gas made. In the original experiment, 25 cm³ of
2.00 mol/dm³ acid was used; in the diluted run, 25 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³
acid. The second flask starts with **half the number of acid particles**
(half as many moles) — so it will eventually produce **half as much carbon
dioxide**. Fewer particles *and* slower delivery: the final level is lower
and the climb is shallower.

In general: **increasing the concentration of the reactants makes the
reaction faster**. A higher concentration packs more acid particles into
each cm³ of solution, so the particles are closer together and **collide
more frequently** — there are more successful collisions between acid
particles and the marble surface every second. Picture the same number of
pinballs in a smaller machine: contacts per second rise without any
single contact becoming more violent.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the acid.
:::

:::key-point
To calculate the concentration of a diluted acid, look at the proportions
of acid and water in the mixture:

concentration = (volume of hydrochloric acid ÷ total volume of solution)
× original concentration

For a mixture of 12.5 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ acid made up to 25 cm³ with
water: concentration = (12.5 ÷ 25) × 2.00 = 1.00 mol/dm³. The fraction of
the final volume that is actual acid — here one half — multiplies the
original concentration.
:::

:::did-you-know
*Proportional* and *directly proportional* mean the same thing — and both
require a straight line **through the origin**. A straight line that misses
the origin is *linear*, but not proportional. The distinction matters in
this chapter: the rate–concentration line below must pass through (0, 0),
because with no acid there is no reaction at all.
:::

**Practical: rate against concentration.** Run the original experiment
again with the original large marble chips, but vary the acid
concentration — keeping the mass of chips (5.00 g) and the **total volume
of solution (25 cm³)** fixed. Make each dilution by adding distilled water
to the 2.00 mol/dm³ stock acid with the total still 25 cm³: 12.5 cm³ of
acid + 12.5 cm³ of water gives 1.00 mol/dm³ (half); 5 cm³ of acid + 20 cm³
of water gives 0.40 mol/dm³ (one fifth, 5 out of 25).

For each run, record the mass of carbon dioxide lost in the **first
30 seconds** and divide by 30 s — the average rate during the opening
burst. A loss of 0.32 g, for example, gives an average rate of
0.32 ÷ 30 ≈ 0.011 g/s. Using only the first 30 seconds keeps every run's
rate measurement at the same, steepest part of the curve, where
differences between concentrations show most clearly.

**Reading the result.** Plot rate (y-axis) against acid concentration
(x-axis) and draw the line of best fit. The line goes **through the origin
(0, 0)** — with no acid there is no rate — and it is a **straight line**. A
straight line through the origin means the rate is
[[directly-proportional]] to the concentration of the hydrochloric acid:
double the concentration and the rate doubles; triple the concentration and
the rate triples.

:::key-point
Stretching beyond International GCSE: the rate–concentration relationship
is not always this simple. In some reactions the rate is proportional to
the concentration — the reaction is **first order** with respect to that
reactant (the marble-chip case above). In others, changing the
concentration of one reactant has **no effect** on rate — **zero order**.
And in some the rate is proportional to the **square** of the concentration
— **second order**. If you take chemistry further you will meet all three,
and the order of each reactant becomes a measurable property of the
reaction itself. This is enrichment — you do not need reaction orders at
International GCSE.
:::

:::exam-hint
In the exam, "explain why higher concentration gives a faster rate" needs
the full chain: more particles **in the same volume** → particles closer
together → collide **more frequently** → more successful collisions per
second. The phrase "in the same volume" is the concentration-specific
link — omitting it makes the answer indistinguishable from the
surface-area explanation and loses the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Half the concentration means the reaction makes half the gas, so rate and
yield are the same idea." They are separate axes: concentration changes
both the *speed* of gas production and — when the acid is the limiting
reactant — the *total* made. A quarter-strength acid gives a quarter of the
gas, delivered at a quarter of the pace. Answers that say only "slower" or
only "less gas" each capture half the truth and half the marks; the
question usually wants both.
:::
