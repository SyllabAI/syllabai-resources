Pressure is the fourth lever — and the only one with a **state-of-matter
condition** attached. Changing the pressure of a reaction whose reactants
are only **solids or liquids** makes almost no difference to the rate.
Increasing the pressure of a reaction where the reactants are **gases**
does speed the reaction up.

Why the difference? In solids and liquids, particles are packed closely
together with hardly any space between them. Squeezing them harder barely
changes their volume, so there is hardly any change in how many particles
occupy a given space — the particles were already as close as they get.

:::key-point
In solids and liquids, particles are packed closely together and there is
hardly any space in between. Increasing the pressure does not change the
volumes of solids or liquids much, so there is hardly any change in the
number of particles in a given volume.
:::

For a **gas**, the story is completely different. A fixed mass of gas is
mostly empty space, and that space is compressible. Increase the pressure
by **squeezing the gas into a smaller volume** and the particles are forced
closer together, so they **collide more frequently**.

The insight that ties this chapter together: for gases, increasing the
pressure is **exactly the same as increasing the concentration**. Both
words describe the same physical fact — more particles per unit volume —
so both produce the same collision-theory consequence: more frequent
collisions, more successful collisions per second, faster reaction. In an
industrial plant, running a gas reaction at high pressure is simply the
concentration lever operated with a compressor.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the pressure question is almost always a comparison pair:
"why does pressure affect gaseous reactions but not reactions of solids or
liquids?" Answer both halves — gases have mostly empty space, so
compressing them packs particles closer and increases collision frequency;
solids and liquids are already tightly packed, so pressure hardly changes
their particle spacing. One half without the other loses the second mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Increasing pressure squeezes the particles themselves into smaller
shapes." Pressure never compresses the particles — it compresses the
*space between* them. Particles stay the same size in every state and at
every pressure; only their spacing changes. Descriptions of particles being
"crushed" or "squashed smaller" contradict the particle model itself and
void the explanation.
:::
