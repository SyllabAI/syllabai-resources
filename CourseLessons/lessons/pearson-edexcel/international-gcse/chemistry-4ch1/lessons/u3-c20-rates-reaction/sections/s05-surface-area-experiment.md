The first rate-changing lever is the size of the solid pieces. Repeat the
marble-chip experiment from s03 using **exactly the same quantities of
everything** — same mass of marble (5.00 g), same volume and concentration
of acid (25 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³) — but with **much smaller chips**. The
reaction with the smaller chips happens faster.

Plot both runs on the same axes — mass of carbon dioxide lost against time
— and two facts jump out. First, both curves **finish at the same final
mass** of carbon dioxide, because both experiments started with identical
quantities: the same total amount of acid must make the same total amount
of gas. Second, the small-chip curve **starts steeper and finishes
sooner**: the same chemistry, delivered faster.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the acid.
:::

:::key-point
If you are going to investigate the effect of changing the size of the
marble chips, it is important that everything else stays exactly the same
to make this a valid (fair) test.
:::

The general statement, for any reaction between a solid and a liquid or
gas: **reactions are faster if the solid is present as many small pieces
rather than a few large ones**. The more finely divided the solid, the
faster the reaction. Powder beats chips; chips beat a single lump.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the surface-area question always carries two marks: **describe**
(smaller pieces → faster reaction, same final volume of gas) and **explain**
(larger surface area in contact with the acid → more particles of the solid
exposed on the surface → collisions can happen more often per second). If
the question says "explain", the particle sentence is the mark — "small
chips react faster" alone scores nothing.
:::

:::misconception
"Smaller chips make more carbon dioxide because they react faster." Final
gas volume is fixed by the *amounts* of reactants, not by how the solid is
divided. Speeding the reaction changes the *time* axis — the curve climbs
faster and stops earlier — but both curves end at exactly the same height
when the same quantities are used. Saying "more gas" converts a rate mark
into an unforced error.
:::
