You now hold the complete rate toolkit: the rate definition and its
measurement by mass loss or gas volume; the marble-chip experiment and its
graph, with average rates and tangents; collision theory with its
successful-collision condition; and the four levers — surface area,
concentration, temperature and catalysts — each explained by the same
particle picture. For Paper 2C you can also draw reaction profile diagrams
showing ∆H and activation energy, including the catalysed profile with its
lower alternative route. The question bank for this chapter joins the
platform by the specification points below, so every practice item you are
served targets exactly the skills these sections taught.

Before you open the question bank, audit yourself against the chapter's
core moves. Can you read a mass-loss curve — steepness, final level, when
it flattens, and why? Can you explain each of the four levers in one
collision-theory sentence with *frequency* and *per second* attached? Do
you carry the catalyst definition and the alternative-pathway wording
word-for-word? On the Paper 2C track: can you draw both reaction profiles
and say which quantity the catalyst changed and which it did not? Anything
that wobbled is a section to revisit, not a reason to start over.

:::summary
- **[[rate-of-reaction]]**: change in concentration, volume or mass per
  unit time; measured by mass loss (balance + cotton wool) or gas volume
  (gas syringe). Rate = change ÷ time; the steepest part of the curve is
  the fastest the reaction is ever going to be.
- **[[collision-theory]]**: reactions happen only when particles collide
  with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy — a successful
  collision. Rate changes are changes in the frequency of successful
  collisions, always stated per second.
- **Surface area**: more finely divided solid → more particles exposed at
  the surface → more collisions per second → faster; same final gas volume.
  Honeycomb catalytic converters apply the same idea to exhaust gases.
- **Concentration**: more particles in the same volume → closer together →
  collide more frequently → faster. For marble chips + HCl the rate is
  directly proportional to acid concentration (straight line through the
  origin); dilution arithmetic = (acid volume ÷ total volume) × original
  concentration.
- **Temperature**: particles collide slightly more often, but the dominant
  effect is many more particles with energy ≥ activation energy — a small
  temperature rise causes a large rate rise; roughly, +10 °C ≈ double the
  rate near room temperature.
- **Pressure**: gases only — squeezing a gas into a smaller volume is
  concentration-in-disguise; solids and liquids are already tightly packed,
  so pressure changes almost nothing.
- **Catalysts**: increase the rate but are chemically unchanged at the end
  (prove it by weighing before and after filtering); work by providing an
  alternative pathway with lower activation energy — never "lower the
  activation energy". ∆H is identical with or without the catalyst.
- **Paper 2C profiles**: energy vs reaction progress; activation energy =
  reactants → peak; ∆H = reactants → products; the catalysed curve is a
  lower hill between unchanged levels.
:::

:::can-you
- Define the rate of a reaction and calculate an average rate from mass or
  volume data?
- Describe the marble-chip experiment — apparatus, cotton wool, excess,
  balance readings — and sketch the shape of its graph?
- State the two conditions for a successful collision and use them to
  explain why reactions slow down as reactants are used up?
- Explain all four rate levers (surface area, concentration, temperature,
  catalyst) in collision-theory language, with frequency and per-second
  wording?
- State and prove the catalyst definition, including the unchanged-mass
  test by filtration and reweighing?
- Estimate rate changes with the 10 °C doubling rule, keeping the
  approximation qualifier?
- Draw and label an exothermic reaction profile showing ∆H and activation
  energy — and add the catalysed curve with both activation energies
  (Paper 2C)?
:::

:::exam-hint
Past-paper items for this chapter cluster into three question shapes: a
graph-interpretation item (describe the curve, calculate an average rate,
read a tangent), a describe-and-explain pair on one of the four levers
(the "explain" mark always wants the collision mechanism with frequency
wording), and the catalyst block — definition, proof of unchanged mass, and
the Paper 2C profile diagram. Practise all three shapes against the clock,
and keep the chapter's discipline: description first, particles second,
*per second* always.
:::

:::misconception
"Learning the four effects is enough for this chapter." The effects are the
predictable half. The unpredictable half is the *shape* of the
explanations: which mass goes into an average rate, why the line of best
fit must pass through the origin, why temperature's two factors are not
equal partners, why the catalyst changes Ea but never ∆H. The examiner
tests the mechanism behind every effect; the sections of this chapter were
written so each mechanism is one sentence you can quote.
:::
