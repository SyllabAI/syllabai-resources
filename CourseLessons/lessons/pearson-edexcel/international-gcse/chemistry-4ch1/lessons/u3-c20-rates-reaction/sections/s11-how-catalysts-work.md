Not all collisions result in a reaction. Collisions have to involve at
least a certain minimum amount of energy — the [[activation-energy]]. If a
reaction is slow, it means very few collisions carry that much energy:
when most collisions happen, the particles simply bounce off each other.

A [[catalyst]] works by **providing an alternative route (pathway) for the
reaction, involving a lower activation energy**. With a lower activation
energy on the alternative route, many more collisions are successful: far
more particles now have energy greater than or equal to the activation
energy *for that route*. The particles' own energy has not changed — it has
been made **easier for them to react**.

**The mountain-and-tunnel picture.** Imagine two valleys separated by a
mountain. Only a few very energetic people will climb over the mountain
from one valley to the next. Now build a road tunnel through the mountain:
lots of people can now travel easily between the valleys. The tunnel is the
catalyst's alternative route — the mountain was never lowered, but the
journey no longer requires climbing it.

**Phrasing the answer precisely.** Say: *"Catalysts provide an alternative
route with a lower activation energy."* They do **not** "lower the
activation energy" — any more than building a tunnel lowers the mountain.
The original route still exists, and particles that collide with enough
energy still use it, just as the most energetic climbers can still go over
the top. The mark scheme guards this wording: "lower activation energy"
alone describes a different (wrong) picture; "alternative pathway with
lower activation energy" is the required statement.

:::misconception
"A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the reaction." The reaction's
uncatalysed route keeps its original activation energy. The catalyst adds
a **new route** whose activation energy is lower — that is what "provide an
alternative pathway" means. The tunnel analogy earns its keep here: the
mountain's height never changed; a easier way through appeared.
:::

**Reaction profile diagrams (Paper 2C).** A [[reaction-profile]] diagram
puts **energy on the vertical axis** and the **progress of the reaction on
the horizontal axis**. The reactants sit on a level at the left, the
products on a level at the right, and between them the energy rises to a
maximum — the top of the hill that colliding particles must clear. Two
measured quantities live on this diagram:

- The **activation energy** is the vertical distance from the reactants'
  level **up to the top of the hill**.
- The **enthalpy change ∆H** is the vertical distance from the reactants'
  level **down to the products' level** (down for exothermic, where the
  products end lower).

**Drawing the catalysed profile.** To show the effect of a catalyst, draw a
second curve on the same diagram: a **lower hill** between the same two
levels. Adding a catalyst gives the reaction an alternative way to happen
with a lower activation energy — the new curve's peak sits below the
original one. Label both activation energies (Ea without catalyst, the
smaller Ea with catalyst) with arrows from the reactants' level to each
peak. The reactants' and products' levels are identical on both curves:
same starting chemistry, same ending chemistry, easier journey.

:::key-point
Notice that the difference in energy between the reactants and the products
remains the same with or without a catalyst.
:::

That equality is the diagram's deepest fact. The catalyst changed the
*route*, never the *destination*: ∆H is fixed by the reactants' and
products' energy levels, and those levels belong to the chemicals, not to
the pathway. This is why a catalyst speeds a reaction without changing the
energy change — and why it cannot make an impossible reaction possible, or
shift the position of equilibrium. Route, not destination.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the reaction-profile diagram is marked on labels: both axes,
both energy levels, an arrow for activation energy (reactants → peak) and
an arrow for ∆H (reactants → products). The catalyst version adds a second,
lower curve — and the commonest lost mark is forgetting that **∆H must be
identical on both curves**. If asked "why?", answer: the energy difference
depends only on the reactants and products, which the catalyst does not
change.
:::
