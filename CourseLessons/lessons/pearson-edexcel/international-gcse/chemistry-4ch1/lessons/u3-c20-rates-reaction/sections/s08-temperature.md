Do the original marble-chip experiment once more — everything exactly as
before, but with the acid at a **higher temperature**. The reaction gets
faster: the curve is **steeper and finishes sooner**. The same mass of gas
is given off in the end, because the same quantities of everything were in
the mixture — temperature changed the *speed*, not the *amount*.

There are **two factors** to consider when explaining why increasing the
temperature increases the rate, and the exam wants both:

**1. Particles move faster, so they collide more frequently.** Hotter
particles travel faster between collisions, so collisions happen more
often. This is real, but it only accounts for a **small part** of the
increase in rate.

**2. Many more particles exceed the activation energy.** Recall from s04
that a collision only leads to a reaction — a [[successful-collision]] —
when the particles collide with at least the minimum energy called the
[[activation-energy]]. A small increase in temperature produces a **very
large increase** in the number of particles with energy greater than or
equal to the activation energy. It becomes much more probable that two
particles with sufficient energy to react collide with each other, so the
**frequency of successful collisions** rises sharply. This second factor is
the big one.

So the full explanation ranks its causes: slightly more collisions per
second (factor 1), but — far more importantly — a much larger *fraction* of
those collisions now carries enough energy to react (factor 2). If you
remember only one sentence for the exam, make it: **a small temperature
increase causes a large increase in the number of particles with energy
greater than or equal to the activation energy**.

:::key-point
As an approximation, a **10 °C increase in temperature approximately
doubles the rate** of a reaction. This works for quite a lot of the
reactions met in the lab, as long as the temperature stays within a few
tens of degrees of room temperature.
:::

The doubling rule is a useful sanity check and a favourite exam estimate:
warm a reaction from 20 °C to 30 °C and it finishes in about half the time;
from 20 °C to 40 °C and it is roughly four times as fast. It is an
approximation, not a law — say "approximately doubles" and keep the
"as an approximation" qualifier attached.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the two-factor temperature explanation is marked in a strict
order: collision **frequency** increase first (small contribution), then
the **energy** distribution shift — many more particles with energy ≥
activation energy (large contribution). Answers that give only factor 1
typically score 1 of 2; answers that say particles "have more energy"
without linking to the activation-energy threshold score nothing. Draw the
link explicitly: more particles over the threshold → more successful
collisions per second → faster rate.
:::

:::misconception
"Heating the acid makes the particles bigger, or the activation energy
lower." Neither. Heating changes how fast particles move and how many sit
above the activation-energy threshold — the threshold itself is a property
of the reaction, unmoved by temperature. A reaction at 50 °C still demands
exactly the same minimum collision energy as at 20 °C; it simply has far
more collisions that clear the bar.
:::

:::
