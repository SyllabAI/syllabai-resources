Picture one big lump of marble, then the same lump split into smaller
pieces. The total amount of marble has not changed — but the amount the
acid can reach has. In the big lump, liquid particles **can't get at the
particles hidden in the middle** of the solid; only the outer skin of the
lump is exposed. Split the lump and far fewer particles are hidden away:
the same marble now presents a much greater [[surface-area]] to the acid.

Only the particles **on the surface** are available for collisions. Splitting
the solid multiplies the surface on which collisions can happen, so the
**frequency of successful collisions increases** — and that, in
[[collision-theory]] terms, is exactly what a faster reaction is. Nothing
about the particles themselves has changed: each collision is as effective
as before. What changed is how many places collisions can happen.

:::key-point
When explaining this, do not only say that there are more collisions — you
must always include the idea of **time**. Say "the particles collide more
frequently" or "there are more collisions per second".
:::

:::misconception
"Powdering the solid weakens the bonds inside it, which is why it reacts
faster." Grinding changes nothing inside the particles: the calcium
carbonate in powder is chemically identical to the calcium carbonate in a
chip. Only the *geometry* changed — more of the same particles now sit at a
surface where acid particles can reach them. Explanations that invent new
chemistry lose the mark that the correct geometry sentence would have won.
:::

**Surface area in the wild: the catalytic converter.** Large surface areas
speed up reactions outside the lab too. A [[catalytic-converter]] in a car
coats expensive metals — platinum, palladium and rhodium — onto a
**honeycomb structure** in a very thin layer, giving the maximum possible
surface area for the exhaust gases to flow through. In the presence of
these metals, harmful substances such as carbon monoxide and nitrogen
oxides are converted into relatively harmless carbon dioxide and nitrogen.
The huge surface area is essential because the gases are in contact with
the converter for only a very short time on their way down the exhaust
pipe — every square metre of exposed catalyst surface does its work in the
seconds available.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the honeycomb is the classic applied surface-area question.
The two-mark answer: the honeycomb structure gives a **very large surface
area** for the exhaust gases to flow past, so many reactions can happen in
the **short time** the gases spend in the converter. Notice the time idea
again — "large surface area" and "short contact time" belong in the same
sentence.
:::
