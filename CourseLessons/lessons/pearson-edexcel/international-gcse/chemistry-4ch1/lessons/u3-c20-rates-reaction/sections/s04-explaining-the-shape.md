The mass-loss curve from the marble-chip experiment is steep at the start
and flattens towards the end. Two tools turn that shape into numbers:
**average rate** over an interval, and the **instantaneous rate** at a
single moment.

The average rate over any time interval is the mass change divided by the
time interval. For this experiment's curve: the first minute gives 0.47 g of
carbon dioxide, so the average rate is 0.47 ÷ 1 = 0.47 g/min; the second
minute adds only 0.20 g, an average of 0.20 g/min; over the first two
minutes together the average is (0.47 + 0.20) ÷ 2 = 0.34 g/min. Averages
over short intervals near the beginning climb towards the true starting
rate.

To find how fast the reaction is going *at* a particular moment, draw the
tangent to the curve at that time and find the tangent's gradient. On the
curve above, the tangent at 5 minutes gives a loss of about 0.05 g per
minute — the instantaneous rate at that point. You are unlikely to be asked
to draw tangents and find rates this way in an exam, but you are expected
to know what the gradient at a point means.

:::key-point
Remember, the marble chips were in [[excess]] — there was more than enough
to react with all the hydrochloric acid. There will still be unreacted
marble chips in the flask at the end.
:::

**Collision theory.** The curve's shape is explained by thinking about the
particles present and how they interact — an idea called
[[collision-theory]]. Reactions can happen only when particles collide, and
not every collision ends in a reaction: many particles simply bounce off
each other. For a reaction to actually happen, the colliding particles must
bring a minimum amount of energy, called the [[activation-energy]].
Collisions with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy are
called [[successful-collision]]s, and only these make product.

In the calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid reaction, particles in the
acid must collide with particles at the *surface* of the marble chips. As
the acid particles get used up, the collision rate decreases — so the
reaction slows. That is the whole shape of the curve in one sentence:
**steepest at the start when acid particles are plentiful, flatter as they
are consumed, horizontal when the acid is exhausted**. The marble is in a
large excess precisely so that its surface area barely changes during the
run — keeping the comparison fair.

:::key-point
If you have studied u2:c16, you may realise that the particles doing the
colliding in the acid are H⁺ ions. A reaction happens when H⁺ ions collide
successfully with the CO₃²⁻ ions in the calcium carbonate. You should not
talk about "HCl molecules colliding with CaCO₃ molecules". If in doubt
about which particles are actually colliding, it is safer to say
**particle**.
:::

The same reaction can be watched from the other side: instead of mass of
gas lost, collect the gas and measure **volume produced** — a gas syringe
on a sealed flask does this. Either method tracks the same reaction, and
both produce curves with the same characteristic shape.

At International GCSE you normally plot graphs of product formed (mass or
volume rising). But you may also meet graphs showing the **fall in
concentration of a reactant** — the acid's concentration starting high and
sinking towards zero. Read such graphs by the same rule, mirrored: where
the falling curve is steepest, the reaction is fastest; the curve becomes
horizontal when the reaction stops, because all the acid has been consumed.

:::exam-hint
In the exam, "explain why the reaction slows down" is a three-step mark
chain: (1) the concentration of acid particles falls as they are used up;
(2) so collisions between acid particles and the marble surface happen less
frequently; (3) so the frequency of successful collisions falls, and the
rate decreases. Write all three links — jumping straight from "less acid"
to "slower reaction" skips the collision step the mark scheme wants.
:::

:::misconception
"Fewer collisions per second means each collision is weaker." The slowing
is about *how often* particles collide, not how hard. As the acid is used
up, fewer acid particles remain in each cm³, so collisions with the marble
surface become rarer — but a collision in minute five carries the same
energy spread as one in minute one. Rate falls because collision frequency
falls; activation energy has not changed.
:::
