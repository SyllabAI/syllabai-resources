Chemical reactions happen at enormously different speeds. Explosions are
over in a fraction of a second; a strip of magnesium fizzes away in a few
moments in acid; iron rusts over days and weeks; and some things that look
like they should react simply never do — a gold coin can sit in the open air
for thousands of years without changing at all. This chapter is about what
controls those speeds: how to measure the [[rate-of-reaction]], how to
change it, and how to explain every change with one idea —
[[collision-theory]].

The chapter runs on two tracks. The **core track** (assessed in both Paper
1C and Paper 2C) covers the experiments that measure rates, the four factors
that change them — surface area of solids, concentration of solutions,
temperature, and the use of a catalyst — and the particle explanations
behind each one. Two core practicals anchor the chapter: following the
reaction between marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid, and comparing
solid catalysts in the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide solution. The
**chemistry-only track** (Paper 2C) adds reaction profile diagrams showing
∆H and activation energy, and the precise statement of how a catalyst works
by providing an alternative pathway.

One idea organises everything that follows. Reactions occur when particles
collide — but only some collisions lead to a reaction. Every rate change in
this chapter, from powdered marble to a heated acid to a pinch of black
catalyst powder, is a change in how often successful collisions happen. If
you keep asking "how did that change the collisions?", the whole chapter
stays in one piece.

:::prior-knowledge
You have met the pieces this chapter assembles: particle models of solids,
liquids and gases (u1:c01), the mole and concentration calculations in
mol/dm³ (u1:c05 and u1:c06), acids and carbonates reacting to give carbon
dioxide (u2:c16), and — from the last chapter — activation energy and
exothermic reactions (u3:c19). If mol/dm³ dilution arithmetic feels shaky,
revise it before s07, which uses it heavily.
:::

:::cross-curricular
Rate thinking is everywhere outside chemistry: medicine times drug release,
food science fights spoilage, civil engineering predicts concrete setting
and rusting in bridges. The catalytic converter in section 6 is car
engineering and air-quality policy in one device. Physics reuses the same
collision picture for gas pressure and diffusion.
:::

:::exam-hint
In the exam, the command words for this chapter are *describe* (state the
experiment and the observation), *explain* (give the collision-theory
mechanism) and *know* (recall the catalyst definition and pathway wording
exactly). "Describe" earns marks for the method and the result; "explain"
earns nothing without particles, collisions and frequency. Learn the two
catalyst statements word-for-word — they are marked with surprising
strictness.
:::

:::misconception
"Fast reactions are the ones that give out the most heat." Rate and energy
change are independent: the reaction of hydrogen peroxide decomposing on a
catalyst is fast but only gently warming, while rusting releases a
comparable amount of energy per mole yet takes weeks. Rate is about
*collisions per second*, not about the size of ∆H.
:::
