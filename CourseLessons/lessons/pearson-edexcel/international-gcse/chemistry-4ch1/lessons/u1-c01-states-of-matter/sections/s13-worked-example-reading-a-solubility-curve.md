**CHEMISTRY ONLY.** The book's worked example uses potassium chloride,
with solubilities measured at five temperatures:

| Temperature/°C | 10 | 30 | 40 | 70 | 90 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solubility/g per 100 g of water | 31.2 | 37.2 | 40.0 | 48.5 | 53.9 |

**(a) Plot the curve.** Temperature on the horizontal axis, solubility on
the vertical one; plot the five points and join them smoothly (Figure
1.19).

**(b)(i) Solubility at 50 °C.** Reading the curve at 50 °C gives **42.5 g
per 100 g of water**.

**(b)(ii) Maximum mass dissolving in 50 g of water at 40 °C.** The curve
gives 40 g per 100 g at 40 °C — so half the mass of water dissolves half
the mass: **20 g** in 50 g of water. For any other mass of water, scale
with the equation: mass dissolved = (mass of water ÷ 100) × solubility.
For instance, in 34.6 g of water at 40 °C: (34.6 ÷ 100) × 40 =
**13.84 g** of potassium chloride dissolves.

**(b)(iii) When do crystals first appear?** A hot solution contains 51.0 g
of potassium chloride in 100 g of water, and is left to cool. Crystals
appear as soon as the solution becomes saturated — that is, when 51.0 g
is the maximum that can stay dissolved. Drawing a line across at 51.0 g
shows this happens at **80 °C**: below that temperature the solution is
saturated and crystals form. (In different circumstances you might need to
read the 10 °C value from the graph as well — for example, cooling to
10 °C leaves 31.2 g dissolved, so 51.0 − 31.2 = 19.8 g of crystals form.)

The chapter questions for this book section are on pp. 12–13 (including
the sodium chlorate plotting question) — they stay in the book; this
lesson's practice is wired to the question bank below.

:::worked-example
The three readings above — 42.5 g per 100 g at 50 °C; 20 g in 50 g of
water at 40 °C; crystals first below 80 °C for a 51.0 g solution — are
the full exam skill set: **read the curve, scale to the solvent mass,
find the saturation temperature.**
:::

:::maths-skills
The scaling equation mass = (mass of water ÷ 100) × solubility works in
both directions: given the water mass it predicts the dissolved mass;
given the dissolved mass (51.0 g) it pins the temperature at which
crystals first appear.
:::

:::misconception
"A solubility curve is always a straight line." No — the potassium
chloride curve here is virtually straight, but most curves bend (potassium
nitrate's rises steeply). A line of best fit can be a curve.
:::

:::exam-hint
Graph questions: draw dashed construction lines from the axis to the
curve and quote values to the precision the grid allows. Mark schemes
accept read-off tolerance — but a missing temperature or unit ("per 100 g
of water") loses marks.
:::

:::summary
- Matter is solid, liquid or gas; the [[particle-model]] explains states
  by particle arrangement, separation, motion and energy.
- Interconversions: melting/freezing (solid↔liquid), boiling/condensing
  (liquid↔gas), evaporation (any temperature), sublimation/deposition
  (solid↔gas directly).
- State at a temperature = where that temperature sits relative to the
  melting and boiling points (room temperature = 20–25 °C).
- Diffusion = spreading from high to low concentration; lighter particles
  diffuse faster; liquids diffuse slowly.
- Solute + solvent = solution; a saturated solution holds the maximum
  dissolved solid at that temperature; solubility is quoted in g per 100 g
  of solvent **with a temperature** (Chemistry only, with curves and the
  practical).
:::
