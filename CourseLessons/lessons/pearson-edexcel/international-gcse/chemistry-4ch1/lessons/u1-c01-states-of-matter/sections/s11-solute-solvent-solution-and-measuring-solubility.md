When a solid dissolves in a liquid, the vocabulary is fixed: the substance
that dissolves is the **[[solute]]**; the liquid it dissolves in is the
**[[solvent]]**; the liquid formed is a **[[solution]]**. At the particle
level, dissolving means the attractive forces between the particles in the
solute are broken, while new attractive forces form between solvent
particles and solute particles. Whether a particular solid is soluble in a
particular solvent depends on whether the new forces are strong enough to
overcome the old ones.

Only a certain amount of solute will dissolve in a fixed amount of
solvent at a particular temperature. When that maximum is dissolved —
with some undissolved solid still present — you have a **[[saturated-solution]]**. Keep some undissolved solid there: it
prevents supersaturated solutions forming (an extension idea you do not
need at International GCSE).

> **CHEMISTRY ONLY** — everything from here to the end of §s13 is
> Paper 2 content (spec points 1.5C, 1.6C, 1.7C).

The **[[solubility]]** of a solid in a solvent at a particular temperature
is defined as *the mass of solute which must dissolve in 100 g of solvent
at that temperature to form a saturated solution*. For example, the
solubility of sodium chloride in water at 25 °C is about **36 g per 100 g
of water**.

**Practical: measuring the solubility of potassium nitrate at 40 °C.**
Weigh an evaporating basin; heat water in a boiling tube to just above 40
°C; add potassium nitrate stirring rapidly until no more dissolves (some
solid left over); allow the solution to cool to exactly 40 °C; pour off
some solution into the basin — **solution only, no solid**; weigh basin +
contents; heat gently to evaporate off the water; re-weigh, then heat and
re-weigh again — **heating to constant mass** — to be sure all the water
has gone. With a recorded set of results (basin 25.72 g; basin +
solution 58.00 g; basin + dry crystals 38.00 g): mass of solid = 12.28 g,
mass of water
= 20.00 g. Twelve point two eight grams dissolves in 20.00 g of water, so
five times as much dissolves in 100 g — the solubility of potassium
nitrate at 40 °C is **61.4 g per 100 g of water**. In general:
**solubility (g per 100 g) = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solvent) × 100**.

:::key-point
A saturated solution contains the maximum mass of dissolved solid at that
temperature, with undissolved solid present. Solubility is always quoted
**with a temperature**: "the solubility of sodium chloride at 30 °C is…".
:::

:::maths-skills
From the balance readings: solid = 38.00 − 25.72 = 12.28 g; water =
58.00 − 38.00 = 20.00 g; scale to 100 g: 12.28 × (100 ÷ 20.00) = 61.4 g
per 100 g. Learn the scaling equation — it appears in exam data questions
constantly.
:::

:::misconception
"Solubility is a fixed property — the same value at any temperature." No —
solubility varies with temperature, which is why a temperature must
always be quoted alongside the value.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Why is the solution heated gently?" — so none of it spits out; spitting
would lose solid and give a solubility reading **lower** than the true
value. "Why heat to constant mass?" — to confirm all the water has
evaporated. Mark schemes accept "mass no longer changes / all water
removed".
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection and heat gently — hot solid can spit out of the
evaporating basin and cause burns.
:::
