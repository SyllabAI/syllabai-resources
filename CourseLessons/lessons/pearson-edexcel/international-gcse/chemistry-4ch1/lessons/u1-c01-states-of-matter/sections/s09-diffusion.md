Someone releases a smelly gas — ammonia, say — at the front of the lab.
Within a minute or so everybody can smell it. That is not surprising:
particles in a gas are free to move around.
What needs explaining is why it takes **so long**. At room temperature
ammonia particles travel at about **600 metres per second**, so travelling
in straight lines they would cross the lab in under 0.01 s. Instead, each
particle is bouncing off air particles on the way — by the time the smell
reaches every corner, an ammonia particle may have travelled **30 or more
kilometres**.

That spreading out is **[[diffusion]]**: the movement of particles from
where they are at a **high [[concentration]]** (lots of them in a certain
volume) to where they are at a **low concentration** (fewer in that
volume), ending with particles spread out evenly.

The book demonstrates diffusion twice:

1. **Bromine and air** (Figure 1.13): a lower gas jar holds brown bromine
   gas; the upper jar holds air. Remove the lids and the brown colour
   diffuses upwards until *both* jars are uniformly brown — air particles
   diffuse downwards at the same time. Random movement gives an even
   mixture of both gases everywhere.
2. **Hydrogen and air**: same experiment, but you must lower a lighted
   splint in at the end to find where the hydrogen went. People expect the
   much less dense hydrogen to end up only in the top jar — in fact you
   get **identical explosions from both jars**.

**Different gases diffuse at different speeds.** The classic evidence: a
long glass tube with cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution
at one end and concentrated hydrochloric acid at the other. Ammonia and
hydrogen chloride gases diffuse along the tube and react where they meet:
NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s) — a **white ring** of solid ammonium chloride.
The ring takes minutes to form (diffusion takes time) and forms **closer
to the hydrochloric acid end**, because ammonia particles (relative
molecular mass 17) are **lighter** than hydrogen chloride particles (36.5)
and therefore move faster, travelling further in the same time.

:::safety
These are **teacher demonstrations only**. The bromine and
ammonia/hydrogen chloride demos must be prepared in a working fume
cupboard with eye protection and chemical-resistant gloves; bromine
inhalation can cause delayed reactions needing urgent medical attention.
Skin contact and fume inhalation must be avoided; clean-up happens in the
fume cupboard. (You will learn about relative molecular mass properly in
Chapter 5.)
:::

:::key-point
Diffusion = spreading out of particles from high concentration to low
concentration. Lighter particles (lower relative molecular mass) diffuse
faster.
:::

:::misconception
"With hydrogen in the bottom jar, only the top jar would explode —
hydrogen rises because it is light." No — after the lids are removed both
gases diffuse into both jars, so the experiment gives identical explosions
in *both* jars.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why the white ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end" (2–3
marks): ammonia particles are **lighter** (smaller relative molecular
mass) so they move **faster** and travel **further in the same time**.
Mark schemes accept "lighter/smaller mass particles move faster"; avoid
teleology like "ammonia wants to travel further".
:::
