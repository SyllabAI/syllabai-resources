Here is the rule that turns melting and boiling points into a prediction
machine. A substance is:

- a **solid** at temperatures **below its [[melting-point]]**;
- a **liquid** **between its melting point and its [[boiling-point]]**;
- a **gas** **above its boiling point**.

To decide the state at room temperature, use [[room-temperature]] (20–25
°C) and place the two fixed points on a temperature line — the book's
Figure 1.10 technique. Oxygen's boiling point sits *below* room
temperature, so oxygen is a gas at room temperature. Lithium's melting
point sits *above* room temperature, so lithium is a solid. Bromine, as
the worked example below shows, is a liquid.

:::worked-example
**Bromine from −100 °C to 100 °C** (melting point −7 °C, boiling point 59
°C):

1. At −100 °C — below bromine's melting point — bromine is a **solid**.
2. Heated to −7 °C it **melts**; from −7 °C to 59 °C it remains a
   **liquid**.
3. Room temperature (20–25 °C) falls inside that liquid range, so bromine
   is a **liquid at room temperature**.
4. Above 59 °C bromine is a **gas**.
:::

:::key-point
Below the melting point → solid. Between melting point and boiling point →
liquid. Above the boiling point → gas. The state at room temperature is
decided entirely by where room temperature sits relative to the two fixed
points.
:::

:::misconception
"Anything liquid at room temperature must have a low boiling point." No —
bromine's boiling point is 59 °C, which is not low, yet bromine is liquid
at 20–25 °C because room temperature is above its melting point *and*
below its boiling point.
:::

:::maths-skills
Draw a horizontal temperature line, mark room temperature on it, then plot
the melting and boiling points of the substance. Where the points land
relative to the room-temperature mark gives the state — this is the
fastest reliable exam technique for these questions.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why bromine is a liquid at room temperature" (2 marks): state
that room temperature is **above bromine's melting point** and **below its
boiling point**. Mark schemes accept "melting point below room temperature
/ boiling point above room temperature" — always quote the values you are
using for room temperature.
:::
