[[evaporation]] is different from boiling, and the difference is all in the
particles. In any liquid, the *average* speed of the particles varies with
temperature — but at each temperature some particles are moving faster
than the average and others more slowly. Some very fast particles at the
**surface** of the liquid have enough [[kinetic-energy]] to overcome the
forces of attraction between particles: they break away and form a gas.
That is evaporation. You see no bubbling — an open liquid just slowly
disappears.

In a **closed container** the story gains a second half. Gas particles
collide with particles at the liquid surface; those moving slowly enough
are held by the attractive forces and rejoin the liquid. So in a closed
container evaporation and [[condensation]] are both occurring at the same
time.

Some substances skip the liquid stage entirely. A small number can change
directly from solid to gas — or gas to solid — at normal pressure. Solid
to gas is **[[sublimation]]**; gas to solid is usually called
**[[deposition]]**. Dry ice — solid carbon dioxide — subliming in a
beaker makes the point vividly: the white cloud is *not* carbon dioxide (which is
an invisible gas); it forms because the CO₂ gas is so cold that it makes
water vapour in the air condense.

Finally, a definitions note you will use in §s08: in science,
**[[room-temperature]]** usually means between 20 and 25 °C. There is not
one fixed value, so when a change of state happens near room temperature,
always make clear which value you are using.

:::key-point
Sublimation = solid → gas directly. Deposition = gas → solid directly.
Both happen without a liquid forming, at normal pressure.
:::

:::did-you-know
The white cloud over dry ice is condensed water vapour from the air, not
the carbon dioxide itself — CO₂ gas is invisible.
:::

:::misconception
"The white cloud around dry ice is carbon dioxide gas." No — carbon
dioxide gas is invisible. The cloud is water vapour from the air
condensing because the CO₂ gas is so cold.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why a puddle can disappear even when the temperature is below
5 °C." Mark schemes accept: *some particles (at the surface) move faster
than average / have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction and
escape from the liquid*. Avoid answers that claim the puddle boils.
:::
