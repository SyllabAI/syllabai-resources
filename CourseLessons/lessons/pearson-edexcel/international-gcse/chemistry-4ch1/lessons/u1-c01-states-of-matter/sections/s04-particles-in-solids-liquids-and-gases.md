Figure 1.4 of the book draws the particle pictures for all three states,
and the text around it gives you the exact descriptions to memorise. In a
**solid**, the particles are usually arranged regularly and packed closely
together. They can only vibrate about fixed positions — they cannot move
around — because strong forces of attraction hold them together.

In a **liquid**, the particles are still mostly touching, but some gaps
have appeared between them. This is why liquids are usually less dense
than the solids they come from. The forces between the particles are less
effective, so the particles can move around each other; the arrangement is
random rather than regular.

In a **gas**, the particles are much further apart and there are (almost)
no forces of attraction between them. They move randomly at high speed in
all directions. The energy story runs in the same order: the particles in
a solid have less [[kinetic-energy]] than the particles in a liquid, which
have less [[kinetic-energy]] than the particles in a gas.

:::key-point
Learn the three-part description for each state — **arrangement**
(regular/random), **separation** (touching / touching with gaps / far
apart) and **motion** (vibrate about fixed positions / move around each
other / move randomly at high speed) — plus the energy ordering
solid < liquid < gas.
:::

:::misconception
"There are no forces at all between gas particles." Almost — the book says
*(almost)* no forces. Very weak attractions do exist; they are what let a
gas condense to a liquid when it is cooled enough.
:::

:::exam-hint
A 6-mark "compare the particles in solids and liquids" answer scores by
covering arrangement, separation, motion and energy. Mark schemes accept
phrases like "vibrate about fixed positions", "move around each other" and
"move randomly and fast". "Particles in a liquid are freer" alone is too
vague to score.
:::
