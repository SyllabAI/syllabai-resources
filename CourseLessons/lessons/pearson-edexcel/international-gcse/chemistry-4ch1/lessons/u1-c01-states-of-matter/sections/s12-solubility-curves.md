**CHEMISTRY ONLY.** Solubility changes with temperature, so plot
[[solubility]] against temperature and you get a **solubility curve**.
Most solids have curves like those for potassium nitrate and potassium
chloride: solubility **increases** with
temperature — either dramatically (KNO₃) or only a little (KCl).

Solubility curves earn their keep by predicting **crystals**. Take
potassium nitrate: at 90 °C, 200 g dissolves in 100 g of water; at 30 °C
only 50 g will dissolve. So a solution containing 200 g of potassium
nitrate in 100 g of water, cooled from 90 °C to 30 °C, must release
200 − 50 = **150 g** of solid. We say the potassium nitrate
**[[crystallisation]]s out** of the solution — or precipitates out — as
crystals.

One graph-drawing note: the solubility curve here is
virtually a straight line, but that will not always be the case — if you
are asked to draw a line of best fit, it can be either a straight line or
a curve.

:::key-point
Mass of crystals formed on cooling = difference between the solubility
values at the starting and finishing temperatures (for the same mass of
solvent).
:::

:::maths-skills
Cooling a saturated solution: crystals = solubility(hot) − solubility(cool),
scaled to however much solvent you actually have (see §s13 for the scaling
method).
:::

:::misconception
"Cooling a saturated solution makes more solute dissolve." No — cooling
lowers the solubility of most solids, so the excess solute comes **out**
of the solution as crystals.
:::

:::exam-hint
Read the axes before anything else — temperature on the horizontal axis,
solubility (g per 100 g of water) on the vertical. Use dashed construction
lines to read values; mark schemes allow small read-off tolerances.
:::
