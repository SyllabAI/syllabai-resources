Heat a solid and the energy from the heat source makes its particles
vibrate faster and faster. Eventually they vibrate so fast that the forces
of attraction are no longer strong enough to hold them together — the
particles can move around each other, and the solid **[[melting]]s** into
a liquid. The temperature at which this happens is the **[[melting-point]]**. Energy has to be supplied to get
there, because the liquid particles end up with more [[kinetic-energy]]
than the solid particles had.

Cool the liquid again and its particles move more and more slowly, until
the forces of attraction can hold them in fixed positions — packing more
closely together into a solid. The liquid **[[freezing]]s**. The
temperature at which this happens is the freezing point — and although the
two directions have different names, the melting point and the freezing
point of a substance are **exactly the same temperature**.

Boiling is the liquid-to-gas change that happens when a liquid is heated
so strongly that particles move fast enough to overcome **all** the forces
of attraction between them. The stronger those forces are, the higher the
**[[boiling-point]]** — more energy is needed to beat them. Cool a gas and
its particles eventually slow enough for attractions to form and hold
them together as a liquid: the gas **[[condensation]]s**.

One warning before you leave this section: evaporation is *not* boiling.
Evaporation occurs at **any** temperature; boiling occurs only at the
[[boiling-point]]. UK puddles disappear in winter at
temperatures well below 5 °C — the water certainly is not boiling; it is
evaporating. Water evaporates at, say, 5 °C but boils only at 100 °C.

:::key-point
Melting point = freezing point (same temperature, different direction).
Boiling happens only at the boiling point; evaporation happens at any
temperature.
:::

:::misconception
"A liquid must reach its boiling point before any of it can turn into a
gas." No — evaporation happens at any temperature from the surface;
boiling is the special, faster change that occurs throughout the liquid at
one temperature.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Name the change of state" questions (1 mark each): solid→liquid =
melting; liquid→solid = freezing; liquid→gas = boiling *or* evaporation;
gas→liquid = condensing. Mark schemes accept both spellings/gerunds
("condensation"/"condensing").
:::
