Try these three facts on for size (the book's own "think about these
facts" opener):

- You can't walk through a brick wall, but you *can* move — with some
  resistance, it pushes back against you — through water in a swimming
  pool. Moving through air is easy.
- When you melt most solids their volume increases slightly. Most liquids
  are less dense than the solid they come from.
- If you boil about 5 cm³ of water, the steam that comes off will fill an
  average bucket.

The [[particle-model]] explains all three at once. The particles in a solid
are held by strong forces of attraction and cannot move out of your way —
so the brick wall stops you. In a liquid the particles can be pushed aside
— so you can swim through water. In a gas there are (almost) no forces
between the particles, and they are far apart — so walking through air is
effortless. And when a solid melts, small gaps open up between the
particles, which is why most liquids are slightly less dense than their
solids; when a liquid boils, the particles separate hugely, which is why
5 cm³ of water makes a bucketful of steam.

:::key-point
- You can't walk through a brick wall because of the strong forces of
  attraction between the particles — they can't move out of your way.
- You can swim through water because you can push the particles out of
  the way.
- It is easy to move through a gas because there are (almost) no forces
  between the particles.
:::

:::did-you-know
5 cm³ of water becomes enough steam to fill an average bucket. That is how
much further apart gas particles are compared with particles in a liquid.
:::

:::misconception
"You can walk through air because air has no particles." No — air is full
of gas particles; you pass through easily because they are far apart and
there are (almost) no forces between them, not because they are absent.
:::

:::exam-hint
When you draw particle diagrams: in a solid the particles are close
together and usually regularly packed; in a liquid make sure the particles
are **mostly touching** their neighbours (the packing need not be regular
— what matters is close together with some gaps).
:::
