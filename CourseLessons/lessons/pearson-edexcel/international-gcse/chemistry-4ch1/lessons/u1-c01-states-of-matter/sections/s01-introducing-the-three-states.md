Everything around you — metals, concrete, water, the air you breathe, even
clouds — is made of **particles** that are far too small to see. That single
idea is called the [[particle-model]], and it is the key that unlocks the
whole of this chapter. In it we look at how the particles are arranged in
solids, liquids and gases, and at the ways the particles can move around.
What the particles themselves actually are (atoms, ions or molecules) is a
story for Chapter 3 — this chapter deliberately stays with the model, not
the particle types.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- describe the three states of matter in terms of the **arrangement,
  movement and energy** of their particles;
- understand the interconversions between the three states: their names,
  how they are achieved, and the changes in arrangement, movement and
  energy of the particles;
- define the terms **solvent**, **solute**, **solution** and **saturated
  solution**;
- *(Chemistry only)* define **solubility** in g per 100 g of solvent, plot
  and interpret solubility curves, and describe the solubility practical;
- explain how experiments on the **dilution** of coloured solutions and the
  **diffusion** of gases are evidence for the [[particle-model]].

:::key-point
The three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. The [[particle-model]]
explains their properties in terms of the arrangement, separation and motion
of particles — and the [[kinetic-energy]] of those particles.
:::

:::prior-knowledge
Before this lesson you should be comfortable with **elements, compounds and
mixtures** vocabulary from Key Stage 3, and with reading simple particle
diagrams. This is the first chapter of Unit 1 — there is no earlier lesson
in this course to link back to, so the Prior Knowledge you need is from
KS3, not from SyllabAI.
:::

:::cross-curricular
**Maths:** comparing the density of solids, liquids and gases is ratio and
proportion reasoning; the dilution estimate in §s10 uses powers of ten.
**Physics:** changes of state reappear in thermal-energy transfer — the
same [[particle-model]] vocabulary is used there.
:::

:::misconception
"Heating makes particles expand." No — when a solid is heated and melts, the
**spaces between** particles increase and the particles move faster; the
particles themselves do not get bigger.
:::

:::exam-hint
Command word **"State"** (1 mark): one short line, no explanation needed.
For "describe" questions, mark schemes accept answers in terms of
*arrangement, separation and motion* — e.g. "particles move faster" for
melting. Answers claiming particles "expand" score nothing.
:::
