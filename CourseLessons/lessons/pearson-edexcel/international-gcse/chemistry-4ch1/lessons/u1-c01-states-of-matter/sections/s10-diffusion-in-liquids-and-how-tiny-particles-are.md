[[diffusion]] through a **liquid** is very slow if the liquid is
completely still. Place a small jar of strongly coloured potassium
manganate(VII) solution in a gas jar of water and it can take **days** for
the colour to spread through all the water. Two reasons, straight from the
[[particle-model]]: particles in a liquid move more slowly than particles
in a gas, and they are much closer together — so there is less space to
move into without colliding with another particle.

This coloured solution then powers one of the most beautiful estimates in
the whole course — estimating an upper limit on the mass of a single
particle:

1. Dissolve **0.01 g** of potassium manganate(VII) in **1 cm³** of water —
   a deep purple [[solution]].
2. One drop is about **0.05 cm³**, so there are **20 drops** in 1 cm³, and
   each drop contains 0.0005 g.
3. Dilute the solution until the total volume is **10 000 cm³** — the
   colour is *still just visible*. There are now **200 000 drops**, and
   for each drop to look purple, each drop must contain **at least one
   'particle'**.
4. So at least 200 000 'particles' must fit in 0.01 g — each 'particle'
   can weigh no more than **5 × 10⁻⁸ g** (0.00000005 g).

That estimate is *not even close* to the real answer — a potassium
manganate(VII) 'particle' actually weighs about **2.6 × 10⁻²² g**, and
there are about **38 000 000 000 000 000 000** particles in 0.01 g. In
reality you need enormous numbers of particles in each drop for the colour
to register. Dilution does not remove particles — it only spreads them
further apart.

Why the inverted commas around 'particle'? Potassium manganate(VII) is an
**ionic compound**, so it contains more than one sort of particle — you
will find out what that means in Chapter 7.

:::key-point
Diffusion in a still liquid is slow because liquid particles move slowly
and are close together. The visibility of a dilute colour proves that a
tiny mass contains an enormous number of particles.
:::

:::misconception
"Diluting a solution removes some of the particles." No — dilution only
spreads the same particles through a larger volume; the total number of
particles is unchanged, which is exactly why the colour survives such huge
dilution.
:::

:::maths-skills
Standard form is the honest language for this section: 5 × 10⁻⁸ g upper
limit, 2.6 × 10⁻²² g actual, 3.8 × 10¹⁹ particles. Chain the estimate one
step at a time (mass per drop → number of drops → particles per drop) and
each step is easy.
:::

:::did-you-know
In 0.01 g of potassium manganate(VII) there are about
38 000 000 000 000 000 000 particles — roughly 38 quintillion. You need
very large numbers of particles in each drop for your eye to see the
colour at all.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Suggest what this experiment tells us about the size of particles" (2
marks): the particles must be **extremely small** — a very large number of
them fit into a tiny mass. Mark schemes accept "particles are very small";
avoid "particles are invisible" (no evidence) or "particles disappear".
:::
