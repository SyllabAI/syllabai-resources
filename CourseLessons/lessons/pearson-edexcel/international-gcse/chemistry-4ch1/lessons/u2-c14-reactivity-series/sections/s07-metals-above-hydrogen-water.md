The second half of the chapter ranks metals a different way: by how
they treat water and dilute acids. Hydrogen is the dividing line, and
this section takes everything above it.

Metals above hydrogen in the **[[reactivity-series]]** react with water
(or steam) and produce hydrogen gas. The product depends on which form
of water you use:

metal + cold water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

metal + steam → metal oxide + hydrogen

Cold water leaves the metal as its hydroxide; steam — water too hot to
survive as a hydroxide-forming environment — leaves the oxide. The
hydrogen product is the same in both cases, which is what makes these
reactions such clean tests: collect the gas, apply a lighted splint,
hear the squeaky pop.

One more pattern rides along: as you move down the reactivity series,
the reactions become less and less vigorous. Potassium dices with cold
water at the top of the ladder; magnesium barely argues with it;
somewhere below hydrogen the argument stops altogether. That vigour
gradient is not decoration — it is the evidence an examiner asks you to
rank metals with, and the next several sections walk down it metal by
metal.

:::exam-hint
The hydroxide-versus-oxide product split is a marking point in its own
right. Cold water questions want "metal hydroxide + hydrogen"; steam
questions want "metal oxide + hydrogen". Answering a steam equation
with a hydroxide is one of the most common product errors in this
topic — the water's state symbol (g) in steam equations is the visual
cue that the oxide rule applies.
:::

:::misconception
"If a metal does not react visibly with cold water, it must be below
hydrogen." Not so. Magnesium is well above hydrogen yet barely reacts
with cold water, because it coats itself in insoluble magnesium
hydroxide that shuts the reaction down (and aluminium's oxide layer
does the same job). Cold-water silence is not a verdict — it is one
data point that later sections teach you to test with steam and then
with acids before any ranking is assigned.
:::
