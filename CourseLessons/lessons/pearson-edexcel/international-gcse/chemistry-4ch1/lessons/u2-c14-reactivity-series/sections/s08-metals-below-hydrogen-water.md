Metals below hydrogen do not react with water or steam — no hydroxide,
no oxide, no hydrogen, however long you wait. This is why copper can be
used for both hot and cold water pipes: water flowing through copper
all day, every day, at every temperature a household can produce,
never reacts with it. The pipes last decades because the chemistry
simply refuses to start.

The same below-hydrogen rule explains the precious metals. Gold stays
untarnished in air and water indefinitely; silver keeps its shine far
better than base metals; platinum survives everything a laboratory can
throw at it. Where a metal sits relative to hydrogen decides whether
water is a threat to it or just a spectator.

The top of the series, by contrast, is a place where water reactions
are violent. The reactions of potassium, sodium and lithium with cold
water are described in full in Chapter 11 (u2:c11) — very vigorous,
though becoming less violent in the order potassium > sodium > lithium.
Here in Chapter 14, those reactions return as ranking evidence: the
fact that potassium, sodium and lithium all react briskly with cold
water while magnesium waits for steam is itself an ordering statement,
and the general equation for their cold-water reactions opens the next
section.

:::exam-hint
Application questions pair below-hydrogen chemistry with a use:
"copper is used for water pipes — explain why". The mark scheme wants
two steps: copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it
does not react with water (or steam); therefore the pipes do not
corrode away/react away. Naming the position AND the consequence earns
both marks; naming only "unreactive" is treated as incomplete.
:::

:::misconception
"Copper is completely inert — nothing can react with it." Below
hydrogen means no reaction with water or steam; it does not mean no
reactions at all. Copper yields to the displacement reactions earlier
in this chapter — silver nitrate takes electrons from copper atoms —
and it combines with oxygen on heating. The series is a ranking of
specific reactivities, not a certificate of general inertness.
:::
