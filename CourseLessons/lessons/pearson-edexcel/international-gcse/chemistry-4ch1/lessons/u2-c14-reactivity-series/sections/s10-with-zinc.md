Zinc and iron also react with steam — slowly enough that the hydrogen
comes off and can be collected, and neither metal burns. With zinc, the
oxide forms; zinc oxide is yellow when hot but white on cooling:

Zn(s) + H₂O(g) → ZnO(s) + H₂(g)

With iron, the metal darkens slightly and a complicated oxide called
**[[tri-iron-tetroxide]]**, Fe₃O₄, is produced:

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

Metals below hydrogen — copper among them — react with neither water
nor steam, so the water ladder ends here. But water is only half of the
ranking toolkit. The other half is dilute acids, and this is where the
chapter's listed practical lives.

**The practical: metals with dilute acids.** Set up four test-tubes
with about 2 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid in each, and drop a small
piece of magnesium, zinc, iron or copper into each. Watch for a
reaction; where there is fizzing, collect or trap the gas and apply a
lighted splint — a squeaky pop confirms hydrogen. Then repeat the whole
set with dilute sulfuric acid. With hydrochloric acid the results come
out like this:

| Metal | Reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid |
|---|---|
| Magnesium | Reacts vigorously with lots of fizzing. The gas gave a squeaky pop with a lighted splint. A colourless solution forms. The test-tube gets hot. |
| Zinc | Steady reaction with fizzing. Enough gas eventually collects to give a squeaky pop. A colourless solution forms. The test-tube gets warmer. |
| Iron | Slow fizzing. Very little gas collects in the time available. A very pale green solution forms. The test-tube gets slightly warmer. |
| Copper | No change. |

All of these reactions are exothermic — they give out heat (Chapter 19,
u3:c19, develops exothermic reactions properly). That temperature rise
is not just a detail: it is a measuring opportunity. If magnesium warms
the tube most and copper not at all, the temperature change could rank
the metals quantitatively. To compare metals this way, though, the test
has to be a **[[valid-test]]** — every variable except the metal held
constant — and the obvious control exposes a subtlety.

Equal masses are not equal amounts. 0.24 g of magnesium is
0.24/24 = 0.010 mol, but 0.24 g of iron is 0.24/56 = 0.0040 mol.
Chemical reactions depend on how many particles are present, so the
same mass gives the magnesium experiment more moles — and more
particles — and therefore more heat given out, for reasons that have
nothing to do with reactivity. The comparison must use the same number
of **moles** in each experiment, not the same number of grams.

One more variable: surface area. It does not change the total heat
given out, but it changes how fast the heat is released — and a slow
reaction spreads its heat over a long time, losing more of it to the
surroundings, which drags the measured temperature change down. Metal
powders, with their large surface area, keep the reaction quick and the
heat loss small (rate of reaction is Chapters 19 and 20's territory,
u3:c19 and u3:c20).

The full quantitative procedure: measure out 50 cm³ of 1 mol/dm³
hydrochloric acid with a measuring cylinder — chosen so the acid is in
excess, more than enough to react with all of the metal in every case.
Measure the initial temperature of the acid. Weigh out 0.010 mol of
magnesium powder (0.010 × 24 = 0.24 g). Add the powder to the acid, fit
the lid, stir, and record the maximum temperature reached. Repeat with
the other metals, 0.010 mol each time. Typical data:

| Metal | Mass/g | Initial temperature/°C | Maximum temperature/°C |
|---|---|---|---|
| magnesium | 0.24 | 18 | 40 |
| zinc | 0.65 | 19 | 26 |
| iron | 0.56 | 18 | 22 |
| copper | 0.64 | 18 | 18 |

The temperature rise for each experiment is maximum minus initial, and
the four rises are best displayed as a bar chart — the type of metal is
not continuous data (a metal is magnesium or zinc, never something in
between), so a line graph would claim a continuity the chemistry does
not have. (For completeness: the molar enthalpy change of each reaction
could also be calculated — Chapter 19, u3:c19, shows how.)

One boundary on all of this: the trends here apply to simple acids such
as dilute sulfuric or dilute hydrochloric acid. Nitric acid and
concentrated sulfuric acid behave completely differently with most
metals — those reactions are problems beyond International GCSE.

:::safety
Wear eye protection. Keep hands and face away from the mouths of the
tubes: some acid aerosol — tiny droplets of acid — can be carried out
of the tube by the escaping gas.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid splashing the acid when stirring — the
stirring step that follows the powder addition is the moment most
likely to throw droplets.
:::

:::maths-skills
The fair-comparison arithmetic is the examinable quantitative core.
Convert mass to moles with moles = mass/Mᵣ: 0.24/24 = 0.010 mol of
magnesium (Mᵣ = 24), 0.24/56 = 0.0040 mol of iron (Mᵣ = 56) — equal
masses, unequal moles. Then compute each temperature rise by
subtraction: magnesium 40 − 18 = 22 °C, zinc 26 − 19 = 7 °C, iron
22 − 18 = 4 °C, copper 18 − 18 = 0 °C. Choosing 0.010 mol for every
metal makes the only difference between the experiments the metal
itself — which is what "valid test" means, arithmetically.
:::

:::exam-hint
The listed practical is examined twice over: a describe-the-method
question (volume and concentration of acid, initial temperature, mass
or moles of metal, maximum temperature, control variables) and a
why-same-moles question (same mass gives different moles; reactions
depend on number of particles). The in-excess point is a regular
follow-up: the acid is in excess so that all the metal reacts in every
experiment — otherwise the metal, not its reactivity, would limit the
heat given out.
:::

:::misconception
"Using the same mass of each metal makes the comparison fair, because
fairness means keeping everything equal." Equality of the wrong
quantity is inequality in disguise. Equal masses contain different
numbers of moles — 0.010 mol of magnesium against 0.0040 mol of iron —
so the heat given out differs because of amount, not reactivity. A
[[valid-test]] controls the variable that matters chemically (moles of
metal, acid volume and concentration, surface area), not the one that
merely looks equal on the balance.
:::
