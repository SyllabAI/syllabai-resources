One gram of magnesium powder mixed with one gram of copper(II) oxide,
heated very strongly in a metal bottle cap: usually nothing seems to
happen for a few seconds, and then the mixture erupts in a burst of
sparks and a bright flash of light. Afterwards, traces of white
magnesium oxide are left on the ceramic paper inside. The demonstration
is dramatic precisely because the chemistry is decisive — magnesium is
far above copper, so once the mixture reaches the temperature where the
reaction can start, it races.

This is the defining example of a **[[displacement-reaction]]**: the
less reactive metal, copper, has been displaced from its compound by
the more reactive magnesium. The rule that generalises it is the workhorse
of this chapter — any metal higher in the reactivity series will
displace one lower down from its compound. The rule also cuts the other
way, and the negative case is just as informative: heat copper with
magnesium oxide and nothing happens, because copper cannot prise oxygen
away from a metal above it.

The same rule plays out in the other two matchups. Heating magnesium
with zinc oxide produces zinc metal — magnesium is above zinc, so the
oxygen changes hands:

Mg(s) + ZnO(s) → MgO(s) + Zn(s)

And a black mixture of carbon and copper(II) oxide, heated in a
test-tube, glows red hot with the heat given out by the reaction and
leaves pink-brown copper in the tube. Carbon is above copper in the
reactivity series, so it displaces copper from the oxide:

2CuO(s) + C(s) → 2Cu(s) + CO₂(g)

Carbon's place in the series is not a technicality — it is one of the
most economically important facts in metallurgy. Carbon is included in
the reactivity series because it is used to extract several metals,
including iron, from their oxides: if a metal is less reactive than
carbon, heating its oxide with carbon can be a cheap way of stripping
the oxygen away to leave the metal. (Copper is not actually extracted
this way on an industrial scale — the glowing test-tube is a laboratory
illustration of carbon's position, not a snapshot of a copper works.
Where extraction chemistry goes in detail, Chapter 15 (u2:c15) picks it
up.)

:::did-you-know
Many metals are found in the Earth as metal oxides. Even metals that
occur naturally as other compounds are often converted to oxides first,
before the metal is extracted — the oxide is the standard starting
point for pulling a metal out of its ore.
:::

:::safety
This demonstration needs goggles or a face shield, safety screens, a
well-ventilated room and a bench protected by a large sheet of
hardboard, with everyone at the back of the room. One gram of the
mixture is strongly heated in a metal crown bottle cap; then retire to
the back of the room and wait for the violent reaction. If nothing
happens after a minute or so, turn off the gas, do not touch the
mixture, leave it to cool and dispose of it in water.
:::

:::exam-hint
Displacement answers earn marks in a fixed order: state who is above
whom, apply the rule, name the products, write the equation. For the
carbon reaction, the observation line is worth learning verbatim — the
mixture glows red hot and pink-brown copper is left — because "state
what you would see" questions on this reaction are common and the glow
plus the copper colour are the two observation marks.
:::

:::misconception
"Carbon is a metal because it is in the reactivity series and metals
are the things that react with oxides." Carbon's inclusion is purely
practical: it sits between aluminium and zinc because oxides of metals
below it can be reduced by heating with carbon, which is the basis of
extraction. The series is a ranking of reducing power towards oxygen,
not a club whose membership requires metallic bonding — that is also
why hydrogen, equally a non-metal, gets a slot a few lines further
down.
:::
