Rusting needs two reactants — oxygen and water — and the most obvious
way to stop it is to keep one or both of them away from the iron. That
is the whole logic of the **[[barrier-method]]** family: put a
water-and-air-proof layer between the iron and the world.

Painting is the everyday answer, and oil or grease works for moving
parts where paint would flake; covering with plastic suits everything
from garden furniture to dishwasher racks. A slightly less obvious
member of the family is coating the iron with a metal *below* iron in
the reactivity series — coating steel with tin for food cans is the
standard example. The tin is unreactive enough to act as the barrier
itself.

Barrier methods are usually cheap, which is why they dominate wherever
the coating is likely to stay intact. Their weakness is built in: once
the coating is broken, the iron underneath is exposed to oxygen and
water and rusting starts — and rust spreads under the coating, so even
areas away from the scratch end up under attack. A barrier is only as
good as its continuity, which is why the two methods in the next
sections — galvanising and sacrificial protection — exist: they keep
protecting the iron even when things get scratched.

:::exam-hint
"Give two ways of preventing rusting" is a recall gift, but the
follow-up "explain how each works" separates the grades: every barrier
answer must name what is being kept away — oxygen and water. Paint
prevents rusting *because* it excludes air and moisture, not because
paint has magic anti-rust properties. Tin-plating answers must also
flag that tin is BELOW iron in the series — that position is why tin
plating is a barrier method only, a distinction the next sections
exploit.
:::

:::misconception
"Any metal coating protects iron from rusting." The metal's position in
the reactivity series decides what kind of protection you get. Tin,
below iron, protects only while it is unbroken — once scratched, the
iron rusts away under the hole. Zinc, above iron, protects even when
scratched, because the zinc corrodes in iron's place. Same idea, same
can-shaped result, opposite failure modes — and examiners set the tin
versus zinc comparison precisely to test whether you know which is
which.
:::
