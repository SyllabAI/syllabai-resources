Iron rusts in the presence of oxygen and water — both, together, or not
at all. Rusting affects iron and its most common alloy, mild steel,
and the product is hydrated iron(III) oxide, whose formula is written
Fe₂O₃·xH₂O with x a variable number of waters of crystallisation.
**Rusting** is a redox process: the iron is oxidised, exactly
as the electron story of the worked example predicted — Fe to Fe²⁺ to
Fe³⁺, with water's reactions producing the actual solid. Salt speeds
**[[rusting]]** up, which is why vehicles near salty roads and boats in
seawater corrode with such enthusiasm.

**Barriers** — the first line of defence — were the last section's
subject: paint, oil or grease, plastic, or a coating of a metal below
iron such as tin. They work by exclusion and fail by scratching.

**Galvanising** is the upgrade. **Galvanised iron** — iron coated with a
layer of zinc, a process called **[[galvanising]]** — behaves like an
ordinary barrier to air and water while the zinc layer is unscratched.
The remarkable part is
what happens when the coating IS scratched: the iron still does not
rust. Zinc is above iron in the reactivity series, so it reacts with
oxygen and water more readily than the iron does — the zinc corrodes
instead of the iron, even where the exposed patch is far from the
remaining zinc. The extension view shows the mechanism: the zinc loses
electrons (is oxidised) to form zinc ions, Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻, and
those electrons flow into the iron. Any iron atom that has lost
electrons to form an ion immediately regains them — and if the iron
cannot form ions, it cannot rust. In galvanised steel, the zinc's
electrons prevent the ionisation of the iron; magnesium worked into the
protection corrodes in preference to the iron for the same reason.

**Sacrificial protection** generalises that trick. Zinc, magnesium or
aluminium blocks are attached to the metal hulls or keels of ships to
prevent the iron or steel from rusting. For it to work, the block must
be a metal more reactive than iron: the more reactive metal reacts (is
oxidised) more readily in the presence of oxygen and water, and its
corrosion prevents the iron from rusting. The blocks are called
**sacrificial anodes** — the technique is **[[sacrificial-protection]]**
— and they have to be
replaced occasionally, once all the more reactive metal has been
oxidised. This is the protection of choice on large structures —
pipelines, piers, ship hulls — where a barrier coating would be
impossible to keep continuous. Galvanising itself is best understood as
a combination: a barrier method while intact, sacrificial protection
wherever it is scratched.

The exam-facing summary of the whole defence: keep oxygen and water
away (barriers); or coat with a more reactive metal that corrodes
first (galvanising, sacrificial anodes). Choose by circumstance —
barriers where the coating stays whole and cheap matters; sacrificial
metal where scratching is guaranteed and the structure is too large to
paint perfectly.

:::cross-curricular
This chapter's chemistry runs heavy industry. The rail-welding reaction
that opened it — aluminium taking oxygen from iron(III) oxide — is
thermite welding, done trackside with no furnace. Tin-plated steel
cans keep food safe by barrier chemistry alone. Ship hulls carry
sacrificial anodes that marine engineers size and replace on a
schedule, converting a reactivity ranking into a maintenance budget;
and the discipline of protecting iron structures predates the electron
explanation by a century — the chemistry of Chapter 9 (u1:c09) explains
what centuries of shipwrights already knew worked.

:::exam-hint
Prevention questions are comparison questions in disguise. The safe
template: name the method, state what it keeps away or what it supplies
(oxygen and water for barriers; electrons via a more reactive metal for
galvanising/sacrificial), and match the method to the scenario (barrier
for cans and railings; galvanising for outdoor steel that gets
scratched; sacrificial anodes for ships and pipelines). "Explain why
galvanised iron does not rust even when scratched" wants the electron
sentence: zinc is more reactive, corrodes preferentially, and the
electrons it releases prevent the iron from forming ions.
:::

:::misconception
"A galvanised coating is just a fancy paint — once it is scratched, the
iron underneath rusts like any other scratched barrier." That is true
of tin and false of zinc, and the difference is the entire point:
galvanising's zinc is above iron, so a scratch hands the corrosion job
to the coating rather than to the iron. The protection does not switch
off when the coating is breached — it relocates. Candidates who
classify galvanising as barrier-only lose the explanation mark on
every scratched-coating follow-up.
:::

:::summary
In one page: the reactivity series ranks metals by their reactions
with water and dilute acids (2.15) and by displacement reactions with
metal oxides and salt solutions (2.16); the order itself — K, Na, Li,
Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, (H), Cu, Ag, Au — is learnable and examinable
(2.17). Displacements are redox: oxidation is gain of oxygen/loss of
electrons, reduction is the reverse, and the oxidising agent is reduced
while the reducing agent is oxidised (2.20), with ionic equations and
half-equations as the bookkeeping. Iron rusts only with both oxygen
and water (2.18), and rusting is beaten by barrier methods,
galvanising or sacrificial protection (2.19). The listed practical
compares metals with dilute hydrochloric and sulfuric acids (2.21) —
and a valid comparison uses the same moles of metal, not the same
mass.
:::
