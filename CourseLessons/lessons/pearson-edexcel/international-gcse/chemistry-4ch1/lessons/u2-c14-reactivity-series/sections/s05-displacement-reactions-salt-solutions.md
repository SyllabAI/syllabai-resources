A **[[salt]]** is a compound such as copper(II) sulfate, silver nitrate
or sodium chloride — the full definition arrives in Chapter 17
(u2:c17), but the family resemblance is enough for now. Salts dissolve
in water to give solutions full of separated ions, and that changes how
displacement works: instead of competing for oxygen inside a solid
oxide, a metal dropped into a salt solution competes for the metal ions
themselves.

Drop zinc into blue copper(II) sulfate solution and the copper is
displaced by the more reactive zinc. The blue colour fades as
colourless zinc sulfate solution forms and pink-brown copper settles
out:

Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

The zinc and the copper are metals made of atoms; the copper(II)
sulfate and zinc sulfate are ionic. Rewriting the equation with the
ions shown separately makes the ionic story visible:

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + Cu(s)

Look at the sulfate ions: exactly the same on both sides of the
equation, unchanged by the reaction. They are spectators here — present,
but taking no part. Each one is a **[[spectator-ion]]**: an ion that is
identical on both sides of the equation. Removing them (they do not
belong in the action) leaves:

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

This is called an **[[ionic-equation]]**, and it shows only the things
that change. It is also plainly a redox reaction: zinc atoms become
zinc ions, copper ions become copper atoms.

Because everything in solution is already separated, the reaction can
be split into its two halves — the individual oxidation and reduction
processes:

Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻  (oxidation)

Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)  (reduction)

In reality these half-processes cannot occur one without the other: if
something gains electrons, it has to get them from somewhere, so
something else must lose them. The zinc atoms are oxidised to Zn²⁺
because they lose electrons; the Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and are
reduced to copper atoms.

The agent vocabulary from the oxide reactions translates directly into
electron language. An **oxidising agent** oxidises something else by
taking electrons away from it — oxidising agents accept electrons and
so are reduced themselves. A **reducing agent** reduces something else
by giving electrons to it — reducing agents give away electrons and so
are oxidised themselves. Apply that here: the zinc reduces the Cu²⁺ by
giving electrons to it, so the zinc is the reducing agent — and in the
process it is oxidised, because it gave electrons away. The Cu²⁺
oxidises the zinc by taking electrons away from it, so the Cu²⁺ is the
oxidising agent — and in the process it is reduced, because it took
electrons in.

:::exam-hint
Ionic equations are marked hard on two details: charges must balance as
well as atoms, and state symbols must be there. Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) →
Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) scores because both sides carry charge 2+ and every
species has its state. Writing CuSO₄ instead of Cu²⁺ in the ionic
equation is the classic dropped mark — by the time you have removed the
spectator ions, the sulfate is gone from the answer.
:::

:::misconception
"The sulfate ion reacted, because it changed partner — it ended up
attached to zinc instead of copper." Changing partner is not reacting.
The sulfate ion is identical on both sides of the equation: same
charge, same formula, same state. A [[spectator-ion]] is defined by
taking no part, and the proof is that you can delete every trace of it
from the equation and still have a balanced, truthful statement of the
chemistry.
:::
