The temperature-change data from the practical converts directly into a
ranking. Magnesium caused the greatest temperature change, so it is the
most reactive metal of the four tested; copper caused no change at all,
so it is the least reactive. In between, zinc's 7 °C rise beats iron's
4 °C, and the practical's four metals drop into an order — magnesium,
zinc, iron, copper — without anyone having to observe a single fizz.

That is the quiet power of the quantitative method: the temperature
change acts as a reactivity meter. A bigger rise under controlled
conditions means a more vigorous reaction means a more reactive metal,
and the graph summarises in one picture what a table of observations
can only suggest. The bar chart also makes the copper result vivid —
zero rise, no bar — which is exactly what "below hydrogen, no reaction"
looks like when it is plotted.

The ranking earned here is approximate, and that word matters. Vigour
depends on the metal's position, but also on its surface area and
whether its surface is clean — the next sections put error bars around
the whole method.

:::exam-hint
Data-interpretation questions on this experiment ask for the conclusion
AND the reasoning in one chain: "magnesium caused the greatest
temperature change, so it is the most reactive of the four." Answers
that state the ranking without citing the temperature-change evidence
leave the reasoning mark behind — the measurement is the argument.
:::

:::misconception
"Copper's flat bar means the experiment failed for copper — a fair test
should show something." A zero is a result. Copper is below hydrogen,
so no reaction occurs, no heat is released, and the temperature stays
exactly where it started. Reading a null result as equipment error —
rather than as evidence for the metal's position — inverts the most
useful lesson of the quantitative method.
:::
