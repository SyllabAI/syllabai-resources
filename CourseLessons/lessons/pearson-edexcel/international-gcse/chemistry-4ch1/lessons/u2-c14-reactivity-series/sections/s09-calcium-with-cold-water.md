Potassium, sodium and lithium react with cold water to give the metal
hydroxide and hydrogen, and their equations all share one shape:

2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Replace M by K, Na or Li, depending on which metal you want. Calcium is
the next rung down, and it behaves like a gentler member of the same
family. Calcium reacts with cold water steadily rather than violently:
the grey granules sink, but bubbles of hydrogen form around them and
carry them back to the surface, and the mixture becomes warm as heat is
produced. The product is calcium hydroxide, which is only slightly
soluble — some dissolves to give a colourless solution, but most of it
is left behind as a white, insoluble solid:

Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq or s) + H₂(g)

Magnesium, one rung below calcium, shows how quickly the vigour fades.
In cold water there is almost no reaction: if the magnesium is very
clean, a few bubbles of hydrogen form on it, but the reaction soon
stops, because the magnesium becomes coated with insoluble magnesium
hydroxide that blocks any more water from reaching the metal surface.

Steam changes the picture. Magnesium ribbon heated in steam — the wet
mineral wool at the bottom of the tube supplies water, and the heat
applied to the middle of the tube turns that water to steam which
passes over the hot metal — burns with a bright white flame, and the
hydrogen produced can be ignited at the end of the delivery tube. White
magnesium oxide is formed:

Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)

Two details from this apparatus are worth carrying forward. The mineral
wool is not heated directly: enough heat conducts back along the
test-tube to turn the water to steam. And water in these equations
carries the state symbol (g), because you are dealing with it as steam.

One metal seems missing from the story. Aluminium's reactivity should
place it between magnesium and zinc, yet it has only a very slow
reaction with steam. The reason is a very thin but very strong layer of
aluminium oxide on the metal's surface: aluminium only shows its true
reactivity if that layer is penetrated, and water and steam are poor at
penetrating it. Hold that thought — the acid reactions later in the
chapter tell the same story.

:::safety
Care must be taken during the steam experiment to avoid "suck-back":
stop heating only once the delivery tube is out of the water, because
water can be sucked back into the hot tube while it cools — and a
sudden dose of cold water often cracks the glass.
:::

:::exam-hint
Calcium-plus-cold-water observation questions have four standard marks:
bubbles of hydrogen (effervescence), the metal rises/floats on its own
bubbles, the mixture gets warm, and a white insoluble solid (or slightly
cloudy mixture) of calcium hydroxide forms. Learn them as a set — the
question usually allows three, and knowing four protects you when one
phrasing escapes you under pressure.
:::

:::misconception
"Calcium must be less reactive than sodium because its reaction with
water is so much calmer." It is less reactive — but the observation is
misleading on its own, because both metals react the same way in
principle and vigour is the only visible difference. The comparison
only becomes a ranking statement when the reactions are run side by
side under the same conditions; on its own, "gentle fizzing" tells you
calcium is lower than sodium but not where it stands against, say,
magnesium. Rankings come from comparisons, not from single
observations.
:::
