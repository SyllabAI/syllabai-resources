The acids half of the ranking story reuses the water half's dividing
line exactly. Metals above hydrogen in the **reactivity series** react
with dilute acids and produce hydrogen; the product that joins the
hydrogen is a salt, which makes the whole pattern one line long. There
is a mnemonic for it, and it is worth adopting permanently:

**MASH** — Metal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen.

Say the pattern rather than memorising dozens of individual reactions:
every metal-acid equation you will write in this course is MASH with
the names filled in, and the salt's name comes automatically from the
acid (sulfuric acid gives sulfates, hydrochloric acid gives chlorides —
the next sections fill the table in). The hydrogen, meanwhile, is the
same squeaky-pop gas the water reactions produced, so the test carries
over unchanged.

As with water, being above hydrogen is the ticket: the higher the
metal, the more violent the reaction, and the metals below hydrogen sit
the acid reactions out entirely. The next sections walk the series
through that story — first the metals too reactive to try, then the
middle order where the teaching examples live, then the general summary
that turns the pattern into equations.

:::exam-hint
MASH earns its keep under exam pressure: write it down first, then
substitute the metal and the acid, and the salt name suggests itself
from the acid's family. Examiners accept the mnemonic as working
provided the final equation is complete with state symbols — the
mnemonic is scaffolding, not the answer.
:::

:::misconception
"Acid strength and metal position are two independent factors you must
weigh up." For the dilute acids of this course, the metal's position
decides everything: above hydrogen reacts, below hydrogen does not, and
vigour climbs with position. The acid's identity only chooses the salt
half of the product. Treating the acid as a variable to negotiate with
produces hedged answers ("might react, depending on the acid") where
the mark scheme wants a decision.
:::
