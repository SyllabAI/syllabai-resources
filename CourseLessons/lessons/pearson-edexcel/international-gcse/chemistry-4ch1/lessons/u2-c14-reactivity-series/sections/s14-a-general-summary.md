The acid reactions follow the same pattern as the water reactions, but
each one is much more vigorous. Metals above hydrogen react with acids
to form a **[[salt]]** and hydrogen, and the higher the metal, the more
violent the reaction:

metal + dilute sulfuric acid → metal sulfate + hydrogen

metal + dilute hydrochloric acid → metal chloride + hydrogen

The top and bottom of the series behave as the last two sections
established: metals such as copper, silver and gold do not react with
simple dilute acids, while potassium, sodium and lithium are too
reactive to add safely — calcium only joins in if the acid is very
dilute.

Magnesium reacts vigorously with cold dilute acids and the mixture
becomes hot, leaving a colourless solution of magnesium sulfate or
magnesium chloride. With dilute sulfuric acid:

Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

Aluminium is the spoiler in the middle of the series. Its reactivity
should place it above zinc, yet in the cold it barely reacts with
dilute acids — the same thin, strong oxide layer that muted its
reaction with steam stands in the way. The acid removes that layer, and
once it is gone the aluminium shows its true reactivity. With dilute
hydrochloric acid:

2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)

Zinc and iron react slowly in the cold but more rapidly on heating,
with vigour less than aluminium's. Zinc forms zinc sulfate or zinc
chloride and hydrogen; iron forms iron(II) sulfate or iron(II)
chloride — the iron(II) family, carrying Fe²⁺ ions, not the iron(III)
family. For example:

Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Two cautions keep this summary honest. First, the placement this method
gives is approximate: how vigorously a metal reacts with a dilute acid
depends on its position in the series, but also on its surface area and
on whether the surface is free from dirt or an oxide coating. A dirty
or oxide-armoured metal underperforms its true position. Second, the
method needs the ion charges before any products can be named and
formulated — you need to know the charge on the ion to deduce the
formulae of the compounds, and if the charge-deduction routine is
rusty, Chapter 7 (u1:c07) is the place to sharpen it.

:::key-point
There are two ranges of iron salts — one containing Fe²⁺ ions and one
containing Fe³⁺ ions. These acid reactions produce iron(II) salts,
containing Fe²⁺. Writing FeCl₃ or Fe₂(SO₄)₃ as the product of iron with
a dilute acid names the wrong family.
:::

:::exam-hint
The aluminium narrative is a favourite examination story because it
tests two ideas at once: the oxide layer that hides aluminium's true
reactivity (why the cold reaction is so slow) and the acid's job of
stripping that layer (why the reaction then accelerates). Equations for
aluminium need the extra balancing care — 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂ —
because the +3 ion drags the ratios up. Balance atoms first, then
check the hydrogens last.
:::

:::misconception
"Iron with dilute sulfuric acid makes iron(III) sulfate, because iron
rusts to iron(III)." Rusting and acid reaction are different processes
with different products: the acid reaction produces iron(II) salts
(Fe²⁺) — a pale green solution with dilute sulfuric acid is iron(II)
sulfate forming. The iron(III) family belongs to other chemistry.
Reaching for the more familiar iron(III) name is the single most common
product error in this section.
:::
