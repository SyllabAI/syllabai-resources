Not all metals are equally worth their salt when it comes to reacting.
Drop a lump of potassium into water and it skates about furiously,
bursting into flame; leave a gold ring in the sea for a thousand years
and it comes out unchanged. Chemists capture that contrast in a single
ranked list — the **[[reactivity-series]]** — and this chapter builds
it, uses it and then explains the reactions that hold it together. The
series does more than rank metals: it introduces the idea of a
**[[redox-reaction]]**, in which one substance takes oxygen (or
electrons) and another gives it up, and it powers one of the most
useful reaction types in chemistry, the **[[displacement-reaction]]**,
where a more reactive metal steals a less reactive metal's place in a
compound.

The payoff for learning the series is enormous, and it is not abstract.
When aluminium reacts with iron(III) oxide, the reaction produces
molten iron hot enough to weld railway rails together on site — a
displacement reaction doing industrial work. When zinc is bolted onto a
ship's steel hull, the zinc corrodes so the iron does not — the series
predicting which metal sacrifices itself. Gold stays bright forever
precisely because it sits at the very bottom of the series: in contact
with air or water it stays chemically unchanged, essentially forever.

Here is the route through the chapter. First you will learn how
reactions with water and with dilute acids arrange metals in the series
and how displacement reactions — with metal oxides and with salt
solutions — refine the ordering, meeting oxidation, reduction and the
agent vocabulary on the way. Then comes the order itself, learned
straight, followed by the two flagship applications: why iron rusts and
how rusting is beaten with barriers, galvanising and sacrificial
protection. A listed practical ties the whole idea down: investigating
reactions between dilute hydrochloric and sulfuric acids and metals
such as magnesium, zinc and iron.

:::exam-hint
Every objective in this chapter can be examined in Paper 1C as well as
Paper 2C — nothing here is chemistry-only. The most common question
shapes are: arrange metals in order from reaction observations; predict
whether a displacement happens and write the equation; identify the
oxidised and reduced substances and name the agents; and explain rust
prevention in terms of keeping oxygen and water away or supplying a
more reactive metal. Each shape gets its own coaching as the chapter
goes.
:::

:::misconception
"Carbon and hydrogen are metals, because they appear in the reactivity
series." They are not metals — they sit in the series because they are
useful reference points: carbon earns its place through extraction
chemistry and hydrogen through the water and acid reactions that split
the series in two. An examiner who asks "why are carbon and hydrogen
included even though they are non-metals" wants exactly that answer,
not a re-classification of the two elements.
:::
