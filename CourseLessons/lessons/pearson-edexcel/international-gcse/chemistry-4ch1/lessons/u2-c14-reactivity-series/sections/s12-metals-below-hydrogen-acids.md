The acids narrative splits the above-hydrogen metals into three
neighbourhoods, and knowing the boundaries is as useful as knowing any
individual metal. Below hydrogen: copper, silver and gold do not react
with simple dilute acids such as sulfuric or hydrochloric acid at all —
the MASH pattern never starts. At the very top: potassium, sodium,
lithium and calcium. These are too reactive to add safely to acids —
the reaction is far too violent — which is why no school laboratory
drops potassium into dilute hydrochloric acid. Calcium bends the rule
only slightly: it can be used if the acid is very dilute. In the
middle, from magnesium down to iron: the orderly gradient where the
teaching examples live, vigorous for magnesium and settling to a slow
fizz for iron.

So the acid-based ranking of a familiar set of metals reads: the
top four are ruled by safety rather than by observation; magnesium,
aluminium, zinc and iron can be compared directly by vigour; copper,
silver and gold cannot enter the comparison at all. Keep those three
neighbourhoods in mind and every "which metal reacts faster" question
becomes a placement exercise.

The stirring steps in these experiments carry the usual acid-splash
discipline — droplets of acid travel further than students expect.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid splashing the acid when stirring. The
metals above calcium are never added to acids in a school laboratory:
with potassium, sodium or lithium the reaction is dangerously violent.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why potassium is never added to dilute acid in a school
laboratory" is a one-mark gift if you know the neighbourhood map: the
answer is the violence of the reaction at the top of the series, not
the acid's danger. Candidates who answer "because the acid is too
concentrated" have the chemistry upside down — the acid is ordinary;
the metal is extreme.
:::

:::misconception
"Copper does not react with dilute acids because the acid is too weak
to attack it." The acid's strength is not the deciding variable —
dilute acid happily attacks magnesium and zinc. Copper sits below
hydrogen in the reactivity series, and that position, not any property
of the acid, closes the reaction down. Swap the acid for a stronger
one of the same simple type and copper still declines; swap copper for
magnesium and even a weak start fizzes.
:::
