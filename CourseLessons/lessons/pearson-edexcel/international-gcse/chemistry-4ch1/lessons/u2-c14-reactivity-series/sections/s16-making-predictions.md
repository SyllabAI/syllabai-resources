A ranking you cannot use to predict is just trivia. The payoff of
knowing the order of reactivity — potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium,
magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold — is
that unfamiliar metals become solvable on sight: fix their position,
apply the displacement rule, name the products, write the equation.
The chapter's worked example shows the machinery on a metal most
students have never seen in a bottle.

:::worked-example
Manganese (Mn) lies between aluminium and zinc in the reactivity
series, and forms a 2+ ion. Solutions of manganese(II) salts are very
pale pink, almost colourless.

**(a)** Use the reactivity series to predict whether manganese will
react with copper(II) sulfate solution. If it will react, describe what
you would see, name the products and write an equation for the
reaction.

**(b)** Explain why you would expect manganese to react with steam.
Name the products of the reaction and write the equation.

The method, before the answers: place manganese on the ladder (between
aluminium and zinc puts it above zinc, iron, hydrogen and copper);
apply the displacement rule twice (above copper means it takes copper's
place in a salt solution; above hydrogen means it reacts with steam);
deduce the products from position and ion charge (a 2+ ion means
manganese(II) compounds, so the sulfate product is MnSO₄ and the steam
product is MnO). The next two sections deliver the full answers — part
(a) as a displacement observation, part (b) as a steam-reaction
equation.
:::

The same predictive logic powers one of the most famous reactions in
everyday chemistry: rusting. Before the chapter closes on it, two
precisions. First, a naming discipline: many metals corrode, but only
the corrosion of iron is called rusting — zinc, copper and aluminium
corrode, but they do not rust. Second, the chemistry underneath: forming
rust from iron is a surprisingly complicated process. The iron loses
electrons to form iron(II) ions, Fe²⁺, which are then oxidised by the
air to iron(III) ions, Fe³⁺; reactions involving water then produce the
actual rust. Rusting is redox from the first atom lost — which is why
it belongs to this chapter, and why the ways of stopping it are ways of
blocking electron and oxygen chemistry, not just weatherproofing.

:::key-point
Rust formation is a chain, not a single step: Fe → Fe²⁺ (iron loses
electrons) → Fe³⁺ (air oxidises it further) → hydrated iron(III) oxide
with water's help. Both oxygen and water are needed, and both terms of
that requirement are examinable.
:::

:::exam-hint
Prediction questions are marked on the chain: position → decision →
observation → products → equation. Practise saying the position out
loud first ("manganese is above copper because it lies between
aluminium and zinc, and copper is below iron") — the position sentence
is a marking point in its own right, and it forces the decision to be
right by construction.
:::

:::misconception
"You cannot predict anything about a metal you have never met —
predictions need experiments on the actual metal." That is backwards:
experiments on the actual metal are what the prediction spares you. Two
facts of position (between aluminium and zinc; forms 2+ ions) are
enough to decide both of the manganese questions before any glassware
is touched. The series is an interpolation device — its whole economic
value is converting a few careful measurements into thousands of
confident predictions.
:::
