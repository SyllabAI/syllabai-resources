Watch the model reaction again, this time through the oxygen:

Mg(s) + CuO(s) → MgO(s) + Cu(s)

A substance has been oxidised if it gains oxygen — the process is
**[[oxidation]]** — and reduced if it loses oxygen, which is
**[[reduction]]**. In this reaction the
magnesium gains oxygen and is oxidised; the copper(II) oxide loses
oxygen and is reduced. Because one substance cannot lose oxygen unless
another simultaneously gains it, the two processes always happen
together, and a reaction in which both are occurring is called a
**[[redox-reaction]]**.

Each reactant also gets an agent's name, and the naming rule is worth
memorising as a pair because it feels backwards at first. The
**[[reducing-agent]]** is the substance that reduces something else: here
the magnesium takes the oxygen away from the copper oxide, reducing it,
so magnesium is the reducing agent. The **[[oxidising-agent]]** is the
substance that oxidises something else: the copper(II) oxide supplies
the oxygen to the magnesium, oxidising it, so copper(II) oxide is the
oxidising agent. Each agent is itself the opposite: an oxidising agent
always gets reduced (it gives away its own oxygen), and a reducing
agent always gets oxidised (it gains the oxygen it removes). The same
assignments follow for the other two competition reactions: in
magnesium with zinc oxide, magnesium is the reducing agent and zinc
oxide the oxidising agent; in carbon with copper(II) oxide, carbon is
the reducing agent and copper(II) oxide the oxidising agent.

Now zoom in to the particles. The magnesium and the copper are metals,
made of atoms; the copper(II) oxide and magnesium oxide are ionic
compounds, made of ions — Cu²⁺ with O²⁻, and Mg²⁺ with O²⁻. Writing the
ions into the equation gives:

Mg(s) + Cu²⁺O²⁻(s) → Mg²⁺O²⁻(s) + Cu(s)

The ion pairs stay paired here because the compounds are solids. Only
when particles are dissolved in solution can their ions move and be
treated separately — in a solid the ions are locked in the lattice.

That solid-lattice lock is exactly why the oxide ion is so revealing.
The oxide ion O²⁻ starts in copper(II) oxide and ends up in magnesium
oxide: it changes partner but is itself completely unchanged. So
oxygen — the thing the first definition is built on — is not actually
the important part of this reaction at all. What really happens is that
magnesium atoms turn into magnesium ions: the atoms lose electrons to
form Mg²⁺, and those electrons transfer to the copper ions. That
prompts a deeper definition of the pair:

- oxidation is loss of electrons
- reduction is gain of electrons

The mnemonic **OILRIG** carries it: Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons),
Reduction Is Gain. You now hold two definitions of oxidation and
reduction — one in terms of oxygen, one in terms of electrons — and
both are true.

As a bridge to the next section: when a metal reacts with a solution of
a copper(II) salt, it does not matter which copper(II) salt you chose,
as long as it is soluble — copper(II) chloride or copper(II) nitrate
would react exactly the same way, because the chloride or nitrate ions
would be nothing but spectators: each one a [[spectator-ion]] taking no
part in the reaction. What matters is the copper ion itself.

:::exam-hint
Questions at this level hand you an equation and ask which substance is
oxidised, which is reduced, and which is the agent. Use whichever
definition is simpler for the case in front of you — oxygen movement
for reactions involving oxides, electron movement for ionic equations —
and then apply the agent-reversal rule: the agent is always the
opposite of what it does to the other substance. "The oxidising agent
is reduced" is a complete mark-scheme sentence; learn it.
:::

:::misconception
"The oxidising agent is the substance that gets oxidised." Exactly
backwards. The oxidising agent oxidises *something else* by giving
oxygen to it (or taking electrons from it), so the agent itself is
reduced. Magnesium is oxidised, but copper(II) oxide — the oxidising
agent — is reduced. Candidates who invert this lose the agent mark even
when the oxidation and reduction marks are already banked.
:::
