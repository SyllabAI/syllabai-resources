The chapter's model reaction is a fight over oxygen. Mix magnesium
powder with copper(II) oxide and heat the mixture strongly, and the
magnesium tears the oxygen away from the copper: white magnesium oxide
is left behind, and copper metal is set free. Reactions between metals
and metal oxides like this are often called **competition reactions** —
a [[competition-reaction]] pits the two metals against each other for
the oxygen, and the winner is the more
reactive metal. Since magnesium wins, magnesium must sit above copper
in the **[[reactivity-series]]**; that single observation is one rung of
the ladder that the whole chapter assembles.

The same experiment template comes in three versions, and the print
headings announce them: magnesium with copper(II) oxide, magnesium with
zinc oxide, and carbon with copper(II) oxide. Each is a matchup you can
call in advance once you know the ladder. The ladder itself is worth
meeting in full now: potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium,
aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold, from
most reactive down to least. Although carbon and hydrogen are not
metals, they are usually included in the series, and this chapter shows
you why — carbon because it decides which ores can be cheaply reduced,
hydrogen because the water and acid reactions later in the chapter use
it as the dividing line.

The safety story for the model reaction is real chemistry: the tube
gets very hot and stays hot long after heating stops, so eye protection
goes on before the mixture is warmed and stays on while the tube cools.
The full demonstration — including why it is done behind screens — is
the next section's business.

:::safety
Wear eye protection throughout. The reaction tube will get very hot and
will remain so for some time — treat the tube as hot long after the
flame is off.
:::

:::exam-hint
When an examiner asks "how can this reaction show that magnesium is
more reactive than copper", the marking points are always the same
three: name the reaction type (displacement/competition), state who
takes the oxygen (magnesium takes oxygen from copper(II) oxide), and
conclude the ranking (so magnesium is above copper in the reactivity
series). Practise writing all three as one linked sentence.
:::

:::misconception
"Heating a mixture hard enough can force any metal to displace any
other." Heat supplies activation energy; it does not rewrite the
reactivity order. Heat copper with magnesium oxide for as long as you
like and nothing happens — copper is below magnesium and cannot take
oxygen from it. The ladder decides the outcome before the burner is
even lit; all the heating does is let the winning metal demonstrate it.
:::
