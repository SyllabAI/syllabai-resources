Silver sits below copper in the reactivity series, so copper can
displace it — and watching it happen is one of the prettiest
experiments in the course. Stand a coil of copper wire in silver
nitrate solution and metallic silver grows on the wire, as a mixture of
grey "fur" and delicate spiky crystals, while the colourless solution
slowly turns blue. The blue is the tell: copper is dissolving as
copper(II) nitrate as silver leaves the solution.

Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)

This time the nitrate ions are the spectators — every one of them a
**[[spectator-ion]]**, identical on both sides — and deleting them from
the equation gives the final version of the
**[[ionic-equation]]**:

Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)

Another redox reaction, of course — every displacement is. The half
story for each element:

Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻  (oxidation)

Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Ag(s)  (reduction)

And the agents, in electron terms: the copper is the reducing agent,
because it gives electrons to the Ag⁺ ions to reduce them to silver —
and it is itself oxidised, since it lost those electrons. The Ag⁺ ion
is the oxidising agent, because it takes electrons away from the copper
atoms — and it is itself reduced, since it gained them.

Notice the pattern across the three displacement reactions you have now
met: magnesium with copper(II) oxide, zinc with copper(II) sulfate,
copper with silver nitrate. In each, the more reactive metal starts as
atoms and ends as ions; the less reactive metal starts as ions (or in
an oxide) and ends as atoms. The reactivity series is really a ranking
of how readily a metal surrenders electrons — and every displacement
observation in this chapter is evidence for that ranking.

:::exam-hint
Observation questions love this reaction because it has two changes to
spot, and candidates routinely give only one: the silver deposit on the
wire (grey fur or crystals) AND the solution turning blue. Both carry
marks. If you also call the deposit "silver mirrors", fine — but the
safe description is grey coating/spiky crystals of silver metal on the
copper.
:::

:::misconception
"The solution turns blue because the silver is dissolving into it." The
silver does the opposite of dissolving — it leaves the solution as
metal. The blue colour belongs to the copper(II) ions forming as copper
atoms become Cu²⁺. Colour evidence has an owner: blue solutions here
mean Cu²⁺(aq), and attributing the colour to the wrong metal costs the
observation mark even when the equation is perfect.
:::
