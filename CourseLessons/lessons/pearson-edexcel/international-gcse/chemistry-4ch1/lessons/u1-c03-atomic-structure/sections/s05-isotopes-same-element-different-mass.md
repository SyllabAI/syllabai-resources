The number of neutrons in an atom can vary slightly. Carbon is the book's
example: there are three kinds of carbon atom, called **carbon-12**,
**carbon-13** and **carbon-14** (Figure 3.5 shows the nuclei of the three).
All of them have 6 protons — every carbon atom does, because 6 is carbon's
atomic number — but their neutron counts differ, so their mass numbers
differ too. These different atoms of the same element are called
**[[isotope]]s**:

> Isotopes are atoms (of the same element) which have the same atomic
> number but different mass numbers — the same number of protons but
> different numbers of neutrons.

Does the isotope change how the element behaves? No — and the reason is
worth memorising because it is the bridge to the rest of chemistry. The
fact that isotopes have varying numbers of neutrons makes **no difference
to their chemical reactions**: chemical properties (how something reacts)
are controlled by the number **and arrangement of the electrons**, and
that is identical for all three carbon isotopes. Carbon-12 and carbon-14
are chemically the same element; where they differ is mass (and, for some
isotopes, nuclear stability — a nuclear property, not a chemical one).

:::key-point
Isotopes = same atomic number (same protons), different mass numbers
(different neutrons). Chemistry is unchanged between isotopes because
chemical reactions depend on the electrons, which are identical.
:::

:::misconception
"Isotopes are the radioactive versions of an element." No — isotope just
means *same protons, different neutrons*. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are
stable isotopes; radioactivity is a separate nuclear property that only
*some* isotopes (like carbon-14) have. Don't fold nuclear behaviour into
a definition that is only about particle counts.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain what isotopes are" scores with the paired wording: *same number
of protons (same atomic number), different numbers of neutrons (different
mass numbers)*. Both halves must appear — protons alone, or neutrons
alone, is half an answer and half a mark. And "explain the difference
between two isotopes" wants the neutrons/mass number, never "one is more
reactive".
:::
