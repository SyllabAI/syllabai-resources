Take a piece of copper and imagine cutting it in half, then in half again,
on and on. The book's thought experiment ends at a definite stopping point:
eventually you reach the smallest piece of copper that can exist — and at
that stage you are holding a single copper **[[atom]]**. You *could* split
that atom further, into protons, neutrons and electrons, but the moment you
do, you no longer have copper: the pieces are not copper, they are
sub-atomic particles. That is the book's definition of the atom — *the
smallest piece of an element that can still be recognised as that element*.

The 118 elements are the building blocks from which everything is made,
from a simple substance such as carbon to something as complex as DNA, and
every one of those elements is built from atoms. What makes one element
different from another is what this chapter is about: the numbers of
protons, neutrons and electrons inside the atom. The book also notes where
atoms come from — new atoms are produced in stars (Figure 3.1) — which is
why the story of the atom is also, quietly, the story of the universe.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- say what **atom** and **molecule** mean, in mark-scheme language;
- describe the structure of the atom — where the protons, neutrons and
  electrons are, and their **relative masses and charges**;
- define **atomic number**, **mass number**, **isotopes** and **relative
  atomic mass (Aᵣ)**;
- calculate a **relative atomic mass** from isotopic abundances, exactly as
  the book's worked example does for boron.

:::key-point
An atom is the smallest piece of an element that can still be recognised as
that element. Split it into protons, neutrons and electrons and you no
longer have the element — that splitting happens only in nuclear processes
(nuclear bombs, nuclear reactors, radioactive decay), never in chemistry.
:::

:::prior-knowledge
From Lesson u1:c02 (Elements, compounds and mixtures) you know that an
element contains only one type of atom and that all 118 elements are listed
in the Periodic Table — this chapter finally defines what "type of atom"
means. From Lesson u1:c01 you know particles in solids, liquids and gases;
the electron behaviour you will meet here (§s03) is the same "constantly
moving" picture. Nothing from KS3 beyond "everything is made of atoms" is
assumed — if that phrase was hazy, it stops being hazy today.
:::

:::misconception
"If you keep cutting copper small enough, the tiny pieces are still copper."
No — below the level of the **atom** there is no copper any more: protons,
neutrons and electrons are not copper. The atom is the floor for *chemical*
identity; only nuclear processes (not chemical ones) go below it.
:::

:::exam-hint
"State what is meant by *atom*" is a 1-mark definition: *"the smallest

equivalent mark-scheme line *"smallest particle of an element"*. Do not

different question (spec 1.15) and earns nothing here.
:::
