Atoms rarely stay single. They can be joined together to make
**[[molecule]]**s: a molecule consists of **two or more atoms chemically
bonded (by [[covalent-bond]]s)**. One point repays care, and examiners love
it: the atoms in a molecule can be **the same element or
different elements**. A hydrogen molecule, H₂, is two
hydrogen atoms bonded together — one element only, and still a molecule. A
water molecule, H₂O, is two hydrogen atoms and an oxygen atom bonded
together. In diagrams like these the lines between atoms represent the
chemical bonds.

Splitting an atom into smaller pieces is not a chemical process at all. As
§s01 established, that only happens in **nuclear processes** — nuclear
bombs, nuclear reactors, radioactive decay. Chemistry, the whole subject
you are studying, happens *above* that floor: it rearranges how atoms are
joined, it never splits them.

One caution before we go inside the atom. You may have seen diagrams in
which [[electron]]s are drawn orbiting the nucleus rather like planets around
the sun. That picture is **misleading**. Electrons are constantly
moving, and it is impossible to know exactly where an electron is at any
moment; what you can say is that it has a particular **energy** and is
likely to be found in a certain **region of space** at some particular
distance from the nucleus. Electrons with different energies are found at
different distances from the nucleus — that energy-region picture is the
one to carry through the course.

:::

:::cross-curricular
**Physics:** everything in §s02's nuclear list — nuclear bombs, nuclear
reactors, radioactive decay — is physics territory: chemistry cannot split
the atom, and the energy side of that line belongs to your physics lessons.
**Stars:** new atoms are produced in stars — stellar nuclear fusion is
where the elements' story begins.
:::key-point
A molecule is two or more atoms chemically bonded (covalently). The atoms
may be the same element (H₂) or different elements (H₂O). And the
planet-orbit picture of electrons is misleading: electrons have particular
energies and are likely to be found in certain regions of space, not on
fixed tracks.
:::

:::misconception
"A molecule must contain at least two different elements." No — H₂ is a
molecule of one element only. What makes it a molecule is **two or more
atoms chemically bonded**, not the variety of elements. (One atom alone is
an atom; two bonded atoms are a molecule, same element or not.)
:::

:::exam-hint
Write molecular formulae with subscripts: H₂ and H₂O, never "H2" / "H2O" —
in "explain/draw a molecule" answers the subscript carries the meaning
(two hydrogens in H₂O). If asked to draw the molecules, use the standard
convention: lines between atoms represent the bonds.
:::
