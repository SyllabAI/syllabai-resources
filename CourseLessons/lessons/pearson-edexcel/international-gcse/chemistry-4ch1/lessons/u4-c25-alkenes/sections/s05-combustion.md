Every [[hydrocarbon]] burns, and the [[alkene]]s are no exception:
in air or oxygen they give **carbon dioxide and water** — the same
[[complete-combustion]] products as any other member of the
hydrocarbon club. For ethene the equation is

C₂H₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

Balance-check it once, slowly, because the alkene equations look
slightly odd at first: carbons 2 = 2, hydrogens 4 = 4, and the
oxygens 6 = 2 × 2 + 2 × 1 balance exactly. The odd-looking
coefficient is the 3 in front of O₂ — a consequence of the double
bond leaving *fewer* hydrogens to be turned into water, so the
oxygen splits differently than in an alkane's equation.

What makes this reaction interesting is not the chemistry but the
economics: nobody burns alkenes on purpose. The C=C double bond is
too valuable — it is the reactive handle that lets industry build
alcohols, plastics and dozens of other products, and burning an
alkene simply destroys the handle. Alkanes are cheap fuels;
alkenes are chemical raw materials. That division of labour is why
cracking (u4:c23) is tuned to produce them, and why this chapter's
examinable reactions are the *constructive* ones — addition, not
combustion.

:::did-you-know
The combustion sentence hides an industrial strategy. Refineries
could burn every fraction they make, but the alkene-rich streams
are deliberately saved from the fuel pool: an ethene molecule
converted into ethanol (Section 10) or polythene earns far more
than the same molecule burned in a boiler. Chemistry's version of
"don't spend the seeds".
:::

:::exam-hint
If asked to write the complete-combustion equation of an alkene,
derive it in the fixed order — hydrogens to water first, carbons
second, oxygen last — and you cannot be caught out by the
double-bond stoichiometry. But note the direction of the
specification: statements 4.27 and 4.28 want the *addition*
chemistry, so combustion here is family context, not the
examinable reaction of this chapter.
:::

:::misconception
"Alkenes burn with a smoky flame, so incomplete combustion is
their characteristic reaction." At this level the only combustion
chemistry you need for alkenes is the complete version — carbon
dioxide and water, as for all hydrocarbons. Incomplete-combustion
products (carbon monoxide, soot) belong to the alkanes-and-fuels
story (u4:c23/u4:c24); importing them here muddles two chapters
and earns no marks.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the complete-combustion equation of ethene and
balance it in the fixed order? Can you state the two products of
complete combustion for any hydrocarbon? Can you explain in one
sentence why industry avoids burning alkenes? Three yeses and you
have the family context you need.
:::
