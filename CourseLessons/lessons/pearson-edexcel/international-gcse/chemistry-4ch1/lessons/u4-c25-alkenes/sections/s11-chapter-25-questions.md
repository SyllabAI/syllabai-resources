You now hold the complete [[alkene]] story. The family is defined
by its [[functional-group]]-in-waiting — the C=C double bond —
which makes every member an [[unsaturated-hydrocarbon]] (4.25),
gives the family one [[general-formula]], CₙH₂ₙ (4.24), and powers
the [[addition-reaction]]s that consume the double bond: bromine
making dibromoalkanes instantly (4.27), hydrogen saturating the
molecule, water building alcohols under forcing conditions. The
[[isomer]]s of C₄H₈ showed the double bond as a structural variable
(4.26), and the [[bromine-water]] test turned the whole story into
a one-tube experiment (4.28). Every specification statement of
this chapter — 4.23 to 4.28 — has been taught, and each now joins
the platform question bank through the links below, with practice
items keyed to exactly those statements.

Before opening the bank, audit yourself against the chapter's core
moves. Can you explain *unsaturated* in causal bonding language?
Can you run CₙH₂ₙ in both directions? Can you draw the three
alkene isomers of C₄H₈ — and say what a same-formula compound
without a double bond would have to be instead (a ring)? Can you
write all three named addition equations with the products named?
Any wobble is a section to revisit, not a reason to start over.

The question bank for this chapter trains a predictable set of
shapes, so rehearse the moves rather than the answers. Definition
items open the set: the two characteristics of a homologous series
and the unsaturated explanation in exactly Section 3's causal
shape. Test items then hand you a gaseous hydrocarbon that
decolourises bromine water and ask what that observation *tests
for* — C=C, not "alkene-ness" — before demanding the displayed
formula of the three-carbon member and a balanced displayed
equation of its bromine addition. The contrast block sets the same
reagent against an alkane under UV light and asks you to explain
the difference between addition and substitution *using the two
equations* — product counts and consumed versus exchanged atoms
are the currency there. Prediction items close the set: chlorine
onto propene, hydrogen onto ethene, and the triple challenge of
sending but-1-ene at steam, hydrogen chloride and bromine in turn,
naming each product. And the conditions recall from cracking
(u4:c23) returns once more — two conditions for making alkenes,
then a chemical test to tell propane from propene. The shapes are
the chapter; the hydrocarbon names are just costumes.

:::exam-hint
Balance the practice time against the mark structure. Definition
and formula items are fast marks — bank them first. Drawing items
reward the bond-audit habit: every carbon four bonds, the C=C
drawn as a double line, all eight hydrogens placed. Equation items
reward the fixed derive order and named products. For the
addition-versus-substitution explanation, quote both equations and
compare them explicitly — one product with the double bond
consumed, versus an exchange with a by-product — because that
side-by-side is what "using the equations you have already
written" is instructing you to do.
:::

:::misconception
"The question bank rewards knowing the printed answers." It
rewards knowing the *chemistry* — the same addition can be asked
about through a different alkene, a different halogen or a
different small molecule, and a memorised answer to a different
question will not fit. This lesson trained the transferable
routines: the general-formula substitution, the backbone-then-
double-bond drawing audit, the decode-the-name procedure, the
three-part test sentence. Run the method and the costume does not
matter.
:::

:::summary
- **The functional group (4.23)**: alkenes contain the
  carbon-carbon double bond, >C=C<; no one-carbon member can
  exist, because a C=C group needs two carbon atoms.
- **The general formula (4.24)**: CₙH₂ₙ — hydrogens are twice the
  carbons; every alkene's empirical formula is CH₂; the 11-carbon
  member is C₁₁H₂₂; gases up to C₄H₈, liquids beyond, with the
  alkane-like smooth trend in physical properties.
- **Unsaturated (4.25)**: alkenes are classified as unsaturated
  because they contain a C=C bond; saturated compounds carry
  single C–C bonds only.
- **Isomers (4.26)**: C₄H₈ has but-1-ene, but-2-ene and
  2-methylpropene; draw structural and displayed formulae up to
  four carbons and name the unbranched-chain isomers; cis/trans
  labelling is not required.
- **Addition (4.27)**: part of the double bond breaks to a single
  bond and atoms add across it — two molecules become one. Bromine
  adds with no conditions (CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br,
  1,2-dibromoethane; propene gives 1,2-dibromopropane); hydrogen
  gives the alkane (CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃ — hydrogenation, the
  margarine reaction); water/steam gives the alcohol
  (CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH — hydration at 300 °C, 60–70 atm,
  phosphoric acid catalyst, the industrial ethanol route, u4:c26).
- **The test (4.28)**: shake with bromine water — orange to
  **colourless** (never "clear") means C=C; an alkane keeps the
  colour (it just migrates to the organic layer). The reagent
  tests for the double bond, not for a family name.
:::

:::can-you
- state the alkene functional group and explain why no one-carbon
  alkene exists?
- write CₙH₂ₙ, run it forwards (formula from carbons) and backwards
  (membership from formula), and give the empirical formula of any
  alkene?
- define saturated and unsaturated in bonding language and explain
  the alkenes' classification in one causal sentence?
- draw and name the two unbranched alkene isomers of C₄H₈ and the
  one branched member, with every bond accounted for?
- write the three named addition equations — bromine, hydrogen,
  water — with correct products and names?
- decode a dibromoalkane name into its structure and back?
- state the bromine-water test as reagent, colour change and
  conclusion, and explain what an alkane does in the same test?
- recite the hydration conditions triplet and distinguish
  hydration from hydrogenation on sight?
:::
