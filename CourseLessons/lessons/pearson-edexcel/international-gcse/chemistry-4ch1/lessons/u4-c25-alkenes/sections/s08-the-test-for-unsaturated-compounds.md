Because *any* compound with a carbon-carbon double bond reacts with
bromine in the same way ethene does, the reaction doubles as a
test. Shake the unknown organic compound with **[[bromine-water]]**
— and if the orange bromine water is decolourised (the colour
changes from orange to colourless), the compound contains a
carbon-carbon double bond. If the unknown is a gas, you do not even
need shaking: bubble it through the bromine water and the same
decolourisation means the same thing. This is the specification's
statement 4.28 — the practical discriminator between an alkane and
an alkene.

Run the test on the two families side by side and the contrast is
the whole point. A liquid alkene shaken with bromine water loses
the orange colour completely, because its C=C bond consumes the
bromine in addition. A liquid alkane shaken the same way keeps the
colour: with no double bond to react, nothing removes the bromine
from the water. Two tubes, one reagent, one observable, zero
ambiguity — which is why this test is a favourite in both
questions and practical work.

:::did-you-know
The comparison has one fine print worth knowing. After shaking an
alkane with bromine water, the colour is not destroyed — it
*moves*: most of the orange ends up in the top organic layer,
because covalent bromine, with its simple molecular structure, is
more soluble in the organic compound than in water. The colour
persistence, not its address, is the diagnostic: in the alkene
tube the colour is gone because the bromine has reacted away, in
the alkane tube it survives because the bromine is untouched.
:::

Mark-scheme language matters here more than usual, because one
word loses marks reliably. The final mixture after a positive test
is **colourless** — not "clear". Clear means not cloudy, see-through;
the bromine water was clear *before* the test, and so is any
ordinary solution. The property that changes is *colour*, orange
to colourless, so write "colourless" and the one mark is safe.

:::exam-hint
Write the test as a three-part sentence and it cannot be
mis-marked: add (or shake with) **bromine water**; the observation
is that it goes **from orange to colourless**; the conclusion is
that the compound is an **[[unsaturated-hydrocarbon]]** — it contains a
C=C bond.
And police the vocabulary: the mixture becomes *colourless*, never
"clear", because clear means not cloudy and was true from the
start.
:::

:::misconception
"Bromine water is a test for alkenes." It is a test for the
**carbon-carbon double bond**, wherever it occurs — any unsaturated
compound decolourises it, not only members of the alkene family.
The distinction matters when the question hands you an unknown: a
positive result licenses "contains C=C", and only with further
evidence (formula, family context) the stronger claim "is an
alkene".
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the reagent, the colour change and the conclusion of
the unsaturation test in one sentence? Can you say what an alkane
does in the same test and why? Can you explain why "clear" is the
wrong word even though the mixture is see-through? Three yeses and
statement 4.28 is banked.
:::
