The C=C double bond is the [[functional-group]], and the
functional group determines the chemical properties of a
compound. That is why the [[alkene]]s have characteristic
properties quite different from those of the alkanes — and why
they are much more reactive. The alkane skeleton offers no reactive
handle at all, so alkanes must be bullied into reaction (UV light,
u4:c24); the alkene double bond offers a weak point built into the
molecule, and reagents take advantage of it without ceremony.

That advantage has a name: the **[[addition-reaction]]**. In an
addition, part of the double bond breaks — the two carbon atoms
settle for a single C–C bond — and the electrons that are freed
are used to join *other atoms* onto the two carbons. Two reactant
molecules become **one** product molecule; nothing is ejected,
nothing is swapped. The small molecule simply adds itself across
the double bond, which is why the reaction class is named after
exactly what happens.

Compare the geometry with the substitution you met in the previous
lesson (u4:c24) and the difference becomes visual. Substitution
*replaces* one atom with another and kicks something out
(alkane + Br₂ → halogenoalkane + HBr); addition *consumes* the
double bond and keeps everything (alkene + Br₂ → dibromoalkane,
full stop). Classifier instinct for the rest of organic chemistry:
count the products. One product from two reactants means addition;
two products from two reactants, with an atom exchanged, means
substitution.

The next three sections run the addition cast in full: bromine
(the specification's chosen example), hydrogen (hydrogenation) and
water (hydration). Each follows the same two-step script — break
half the double bond, attach one new atom to each carbon — so
learn the script once and every reagent becomes an instance.

:::key-point
In an addition reaction part of the C=C double bond breaks to
become a single C–C bond, and the freed electrons join other atoms
onto the two carbon atoms. Two molecules become one; the double
bond is consumed.
:::

:::exam-hint
When a question asks you to *describe* an addition reaction, the
mark scheme wants the mechanism sentence and the product count:
**the double bond breaks partially to a single bond, and atoms add
across it, forming one saturated product**. Do not describe the
double bond as "breaking completely" — only half of it breaks; the
C–C link survives, which is precisely why the product is still one
connected molecule.
:::

:::misconception
"Addition and substitution are two names for the same
replacement chemistry." They are different classes with different
product counts: addition turns two molecules into one saturated
product, substitution swaps an atom and produces a small
by-product (HBr in the alkane case). The question bank loves the
contrast — you will meet it head-on in the chapter questions,
where an alkane and an alkene face the same reagent and the
outcomes differ *because* of this distinction.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define an addition reaction in one sentence with both
halves — double bond partially breaks, atoms add across? Can you
say why alkenes are more reactive than alkanes by pointing at the
functional group? Can you classify a reaction as addition or
substitution by counting products? Then you are ready for the
three named additions.
:::
