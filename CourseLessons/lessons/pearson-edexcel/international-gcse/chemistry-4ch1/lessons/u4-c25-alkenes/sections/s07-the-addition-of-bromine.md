Bromine adds to [[alkene]]s without any need for heat, light or a
catalyst — the double bond is reactive enough to do the job alone,
which is the sharpest possible contrast with the alkane family,
where bromine refuses to react without ultraviolet light (u4:c24).
In the laboratory the bromine is usually supplied as **bromine
water**, an aqueous solution of the element, and the reaction is
instant on shaking.

With ethene, one bromine atom attaches to each of the two
carbons:

CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br

Drawn as displayed formulae, the equation shows H₂C=CH₂ with its
double line meeting Br—Br, and the single product H—C—C—H carrying
H atoms above and Br atoms below each carbon; the reactants are
labelled ethene and bromine, the product 1,2-dibromoethane. The
structural form says the same thing in compact code: the C=C of
ethene has become C–C, and each carbon gained one Br. That is
[[addition-reaction]] anatomy — two molecules in, one
[[dibromoalkane]] out, no by-product.

The product's name is a naming exercise worth decoding once,
because the decode procedure generalises. **1,2-dibromoethane**:
'eth' shows a two-carbon chain; 'ane' shows only carbon-carbon
single bonds (the double bond is gone — addition saturated it);
'1,2-dibromo' shows two bromine atoms attached to the first and
second carbon atoms. Every dibromoalkane name reports the same
three facts, and reading them back gives you the structure without
drawing anything.

Propene reacts identically — the double bond is the only part of
the molecule doing the work, and the methyl group is a bystander.
Part of the carbon-carbon double bond breaks, two bromine atoms
add across it, and the product is the [[dibromoalkane]]
**1,2-dibromopropane**:

CH₃CH=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₃CHBrCH₂Br

The structure is based on propane, and the prefix '1,2-dibromo'
again reports two bromine atoms on the first and second carbon
atoms — numbering from the end that gives the halogens the lowest
locants, exactly as the naming rules demand. Any compound with a
C=C bond reacts in this same way, which is precisely what makes
the next section's test possible.

:::exam-hint
Two habits pay on every dibromoalkane item. First, decode the
name before drawing: 'eth'/'prop' gives the chain, 'ane' confirms
the double bond is consumed, '1,2-dibromo' places one Br on each
of the first two carbons — then relate the formula you write to
the full displayed structure, because examiners check that the Br
atoms sit *on the carbons*, not floating beside them. Second,
write the product as one molecule: CH₂BrCH₂Br, not
CH₂Br + CH₂Br.
:::

:::misconception
"Alkenes need UV light to react with bromine, like the alkanes
do." That condition belongs to the alkane *substitution* story
(u4:c24) — bromine adds to C=C bonds with no heat, no light and no
catalyst at all. If a question shows bromine reacting in the dark,
that is not a trap: it is the addition pathway, and it is
instantaneous.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the ethene-plus-bromine equation in structural form
and name the product? Can you decode 1,2-dibromopropane back into
its structure without hesitation? Can you state the condition
required for bromine to add to an alkene — none? Three yeses and
statement 4.27's chemistry is yours.
:::
