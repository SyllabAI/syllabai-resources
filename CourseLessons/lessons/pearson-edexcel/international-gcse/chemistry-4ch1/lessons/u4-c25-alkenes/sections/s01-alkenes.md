Cracking (u4:c23) does more than top up the petrol supply — it also
splits large [[alkane]] molecules into smaller ones that carry a
very different bonding feature: a carbon-carbon **double bond**.
That one structural difference defines a whole new family of
[[hydrocarbon]]s, the **[[alkene]]s**, and it makes them far more
reactive than the alkanes you have just studied (u4:c24). Where an
alkane chain is chemically quiet, an alkene's double bond is a
ready-made handle that other molecules can grab — and the entire
chemistry of this chapter hangs off that handle.

The double bond has a formal name in organic chemistry: it is the
**[[functional-group]]** of the family, written >C=C< to show the
two free valences pointing outwards from the pair of carbon atoms.
Every member of the series carries exactly this group, and the
group decides how the members react. The alkenes are therefore a
**[[homologous-series]]** in the full five-part sense you met in
the introduction to organic chemistry (u4:c22): one functional
group, similar chemical properties, a trend in physical
properties, one general formula, and consecutive members stepping
up by a –CH₂– unit.

The first three members of the family are laid out below — read
the columns the same way you read the alkane table in the previous
lesson.

| Name | Molecular formula | Empirical formula | Structural formula |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|--------------------|
| ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂ | CH₂=CH₂ |
| propene | C₃H₆ | CH₂ | CH₂=CHCH₃ |
| but-1-ene | C₄H₈ | CH₂ | CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ |

Each molecule can also be drawn as a **[[displayed-formula]]**,
with every bond shown as an explicit line. For ethene that is two
carbon atoms joined by a double line, each carbon also bonded to
two hydrogen atoms by single lines — a flat H₂C=CH₂ arrangement.
Propene adds a –CH₃ group on the second carbon, and but-1-ene
lengthens the chain by one more carbon with the double bond still
between the first two. Drawing these three, and naming the
straight-chain members, is precisely the skill specification
statement 4.26 trains, and Sections 2 and 4 of this lesson drill
it.

Notice one consequence hiding in the table: every alkene has the
same **empirical formula**, CH₂, no matter how big the molecule
gets. That is a direct fingerprint of the double bond — it will be
promoted to the family's general formula in Section 4. Another
consequence sits at the very start of the series: there is no
one-carbon alkene, because a C=C group needs two carbon atoms to
exist. The smallest possible alkene is ethene, full stop.

:::key-point
It is not possible to have an alkene with one carbon atom: alkenes
must contain a C=C group, so a minimum of two carbon atoms is
required. Ethene, C₂H₄, opens the series.
:::

:::exam-hint
When asked what functional group the alkenes contain, write it in
the mark-scheme shape: **the C=C carbon-carbon double bond** (often
written >C=C<). Do not write "a double bond" and leave it there —
say which atoms it joins, because the statement 4.23 wants the
carbon-carbon double bond by name.
:::

:::misconception
"Alkenes and alkanes are both hydrocarbons, so they burn and react
in the same way." The first half is true — both families contain
carbon and hydrogen only — but the conclusion is not. The C=C
functional group makes the alkenes *much more reactive* than the
alkanes: an alkane needs UV light and a substitution mechanism to
react with a halogen, while an alkene attacks bromine instantly
with no conditions at all (Section 7). Same elements, different
bonding, different chemistry.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the alkene functional group as a carbon-carbon
double bond? Can you write the molecular formulae of ethene,
propene and but-1-ene, and explain why no one-carbon alkene can
exist? Can you say in one sentence why the family is more reactive
than the alkanes? If all three are yes, move on — the rest of the
chapter is the double bond doing its work.
:::
