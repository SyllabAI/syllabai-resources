The alkane family obeyed CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; the alkene family obeys
**CₙH₂ₙ**. One double bond costs exactly two hydrogen atoms, so the
"plus two" of the alkane formula disappears: an alkene's hydrogen
count is simply **twice** its carbon count. Two carbons carry four
hydrogens (C₂H₄), three carry six (C₃H₆), four carry eight (C₄H₈)
— the [[general-formula]] of statement 4.24, working forwards from
a carbon count to a formula with nothing to memorise per member.

The formula runs backwards too, and the backward direction is the
examinable move. An alkene with 11 carbon atoms must carry
2 × 11 = 22 hydrogen atoms, so its [[molecular-formula]] is
C₁₁H₂₂; handed any candidate formula, divide the hydrogen count by
the carbon count and the family membership question answers
itself — a ratio of exactly two admits the molecule to the alkene
series (with the caveat that ring compounds can mimic the ratio,
a subtlety the question bank likes to probe).

A second consequence flows from the 1:2 ratio: **every alkene has
the same [[empirical-formula]], CH₂**, because that is the
simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms. Ethene, propene,
but-1-ene and C₁₁H₂₂ all reduce to CH₂. If a question asks for the
empirical formula of *any* alkene, the answer is one chip: CH₂ —
no arithmetic required, because the general formula guarantees it.

The same size rule that organised the alkanes organises their
unsaturated cousins. The physical properties of the alkenes are
very similar to those of the alkanes: the small members with up to
four carbon atoms are gases at room temperature, the next dozen or
so are liquids, and the members show the same smooth trend in
properties as the series grows. One general formula, one stepping
unit, one trend — the homologous-series frame doing its job on a
new family.

:::worked-example
An alkene has 11 carbon atoms. What is its molecular formula?
Apply CₙH₂ₙ: hydrogens = 2 × 11 = 22, so the formula is C₁₁H₂₂.
Check: 22 ÷ 11 = exactly 2, so the molecule fits the alkene
pattern — and its empirical formula is CH₂, like every member of
the family. One line of arithmetic, one verification, full marks.
:::

:::exam-hint
Know the two directions of CₙH₂ₙ. Forwards: given 11 carbons,
write C₁₁H₂₂ by doubling. Backwards: given C₆H₁₂, divide 12 by 6,
announce the ratio 2, and claim alkene membership. Questions
typically set one of these two moves, and confusing the alkene
formula with CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ is the single most expensive error in this
topic — the "plus two" belongs to the *alkanes* (u4:c24) only.
:::

:::misconception
"CₙH₂ₙ is the general formula of *organic compounds*." It is the
general formula of the *alkenes* (and, as the question bank
enjoys pointing out, of cycloalkanes — ring-shaped alkane-family
molecules with the same hydrogen ratio). A formula alone never
proves a double bond; the family claim needs the C=C structure,
which is why the bromine-water test of Section 8 exists.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the alkene general formula and say what n counts?
Can you produce the molecular formula for the 11-carbon member in
one line? Can you state the empirical formula of every alkene
without computing? Can you place the state boundaries — gases up
to four carbons, liquids beyond? Four yeses and this section is
banked.
:::
