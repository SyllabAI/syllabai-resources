A **[[hydration]]** reaction is the [[addition-reaction]] in which
water adds across the double bond: an alkene reacts with water to
form an **alcohol**. With ethene:

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH

The script is familiar by now — C=C becomes C–C, one carbon gains
an H, the other gains an –OH — but the conditions are not optional
this time, because water and ethene are not eager partners at room
temperature. In industry the reaction runs with **ethene and
steam** at **300 °C**, a pressure of **60–70 atmospheres** (the
atmosphere is a unit of pressure, equal to 101 325 pascals), and a
**phosphoric acid** [[catalyst]]. Three conditions, one catalyst,
one product: ethanol.

The product is worth naming twice. **[[ethanol]]**, C₂H₅OH, is the
alcohol of the next chapter — and this reaction is precisely how
industry makes high-purity ethanol, with the ethene supplied by
cracking (u4:c23) and the process conditions tuned for yield. The
alcohol family itself — its functional group, its members, its
oxidation chemistry — is the story of a later lesson in this
course (u4:c26); what this section contributes is the *route in*:
the petrochemical industry builds its alcohols from alkene
feedstock by hydration.

As with every addition, the double bond does the deciding.
Hydration of propene would put the –OH on one of two carbons, and
in practice the middle carbon wins: much more propan-2-ol forms
than propan-1-ol, because in the major pathway the hydrogen from
water adds first onto the *end* carbon, leaving the –OH attached
to the middle carbon. The rule governing which way an unsymmetrical
alkene adds is a story for a more advanced level — at IGCSE the
takeaway is simply that the addition goes where the stability is.

:::did-you-know
Hydration is the bridge between two industrial worlds. The ethene
side is petrochemistry: cracking heavy fractions (u4:c23) to make
the small alkene. The ethanol side is everything from solvents to
sanitisers. One reaction — steam, 300 °C, 60–70 atmospheres,
phosphoric acid — is the door between them, which is why the
conditions triplet is one of the most examined condition sets in
the whole organic unit.
:::

:::exam-hint
Learn the hydration conditions as a fixed triplet — **steam,
300 °C, 60–70 atmospheres, phosphoric acid catalyst** — and deploy
them verbatim; "warm with water" earns nothing. If asked to name
the product, say **ethanol** (C₂H₅OH) for ethene. And keep the
reagent word honest: the alkene reacts with *water in the form of
steam* — writing "dilute acid" or "aqueous alkali" signals
confusion with the salts chapter, not hydration.
:::

:::misconception
"Hydration means adding hydrogen, like hydrogenation." One letter,
two different reactions: hydration adds **H–OH** (water) and makes
an alcohol; hydrogenation adds **H–H** (hydrogen) and makes an
alkane. The products differ by exactly one oxygen atom — and by
an entire functional group. Sort the two names now and the
question bank cannot spoof you later.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the ethene-plus-steam equation and name the alcohol?
Can you recite the three conditions and the catalyst? Can you say
why the reaction needs forcing conditions when bromine addition
needs none? Can you distinguish hydration from hydrogenation in
one sentence? Four yeses and the final addition is banked.
:::
