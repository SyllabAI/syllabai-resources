A **[[hydrogenation]]** reaction is the [[addition-reaction]] in
which the added small molecule is hydrogen: an alkene reacts with
hydrogen, the double bond takes on one H atom at each carbon, and
the product is an [[alkane]]. With ethene:

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃

Watch the anatomy — it is the same script as the bromine addition,
with a different partner. The C=C becomes C–C; each carbon gains
one hydrogen; two reactant molecules become one product. Ethene
(C₂H₄) becomes ethane (C₂H₆), and the general-formula bookkeeping
confirms it: the product sits in the CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ alkane family, exactly
two hydrogens richer than the alkene it came from. Hydrogenation
is, in other words, the practical answer to the Section 3 closing
question — how does an unsaturated molecule become saturated?

Industry runs this reaction at scale for one household product:
**margarine**. Plant oils — sunflower oil is the classic example —
are *polyunsaturated*, meaning their molecules carry many C=C
groups; that is what keeps them liquid and "oil"-like. Hydrogenate
some of those double bonds and the molecules straighten out and
pack together better, turning the liquid oil into a solid spread.
The degree of hydrogenation is a dial the manufacturer sets:
partially hydrogenated oils stay soft, fully hydrogenated ones go
hard — food chemistry by addition reaction.

:::did-you-know
The same reaction links the supermarket aisle to the chemistry
drawer. "Polyunsaturated" on a label is literally a statement
about C=C bond count — many double bonds per molecule — and it is
why those oils decolourise bromine water vigorously. A drop of
bromine water on sunflower oil would lose its orange colour
faster than on almost any kitchen liquid: the test from Section 8
works on your worktop too.
:::

:::exam-hint
Hydrogenation questions usually want three things: the word
"addition" (classify the reaction), the product family (an
**alkane**, saturated), and the equation with the double bond
consumed. If the question supplies a named alkene — propene, say —
name the alkane product too: propene + hydrogen gives propane.
Never leave the product as "the alkane"; the name is the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Hydrogenation needs UV light, like the alkane-halogen reaction."
No radiation is part of this reaction's story: hydrogenation is
addition across the reactive C=C, and the double bond needs no
bullying. The UV condition is the *substitution* requirement from
the alkane chapter (u4:c24) — carrying it into addition answers
mixes the two reaction classes the examiner is testing you to
separate.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define hydrogenation as the addition of hydrogen across a
C=C bond? Can you write the ethene-plus-hydrogen equation and name
the product? Can you explain why polyunsaturated oils harden when
hydrogenated? Three yeses and this addition is yours.
:::
