The word *unsaturated* is a classification with a precise bonding
meaning, and the mark scheme pays out only when you give the
bonding version. A **[[saturated-hydrocarbon]]** contains single
carbon-carbon bonds only; an **[[unsaturated-hydrocarbon]]**
contains one or more double or triple carbon-carbon bonds. The
[[alkene]]s are unsaturated compounds *because they contain a C=C
bond* — that causal shape (classification because bonding) is the
sentence specification statement 4.25 asks you to explain, and it
is worth memorising in exactly that order.

A helpful way of thinking about the word is that the alkenes are
*not saturated with hydrogen*: they do not carry the maximum
number of hydrogen atoms possible for their carbon count. An
alkane with two carbons holds six hydrogens; ethene, with the same
two carbons, holds only four — the missing pair is the second
bond between the carbons. The thinking aid is for your
understanding; on its own it is not the definition, and an answer
built only on "they don't have many hydrogens" earns nothing.

The classification also powers a lab skill you will meet in
Section 8: because unsaturated molecules carry a reactive double
bond, they can be told apart from saturated ones with a single
test-tube reagent. Saturation is not just vocabulary — it is a
property you can *detect*. Keep the two definitions paired in your
notes: saturated = single bonds only, unsaturated = at least one
double or triple bond, alkenes = the unsaturated family you meet
in this course.

:::key-point
Saturated means single C–C bonds only; unsaturated means the
molecule contains at least one double (or triple) C–C bond. The
alkenes are classified as unsaturated hydrocarbons because they
contain a carbon-carbon double bond.
:::

:::exam-hint
To *explain why alkenes are classified as unsaturated
hydrocarbons*, write the causal sentence: **they contain a C=C
bond** (a carbon-carbon double bond), unlike saturated compounds,
which have only C–C single bonds. A popular thinking aid says
alkenes are "not saturated with hydrogen atoms" — use it only as a
supporting remark after the bonding statement, never as the
answer, because the mark scheme rewards the bonding language.
:::

:::misconception
"Unsaturated means the molecule is missing atoms." It is missing
*hydrogen atoms relative to the maximum* — not missing atoms in
general — and the formal definition says nothing about counts at
all: it is a statement about *bonds* (a C=C is present). Anchor
every unsaturated answer to the double bond and the term can never
slip.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define saturated and unsaturated in pure bonding language?
Can you explain the alkenes' classification in one causal sentence
with C=C in it? Can you say what a hydrocarbon would need to gain
to turn from unsaturated back to saturated? That last one — two
hydrogen atoms across the double bond — is the bridge to
hydrogenation in Section 9.
:::
