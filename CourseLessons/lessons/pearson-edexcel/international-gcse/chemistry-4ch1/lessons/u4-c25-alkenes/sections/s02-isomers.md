Once a family reaches four carbon atoms, the double bond starts
generating **[[isomer]]s** — and it generates them in a way the
alkanes never quite did, because the position of the C=C group
becomes a structural variable of its own. Take the molecular
formula C₄H₈. There are two straight-chain alkenes with exactly
these atoms: **but-1-ene**, whose double bond sits between the
first and second carbons (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃), and **but-2-ene**, whose
double bond sits between the second and third (CH₃CH=CHCH₃). Same
[[molecular-formula]], different structures — the definition of an
[[isomer]] doing its work.

There is also one branched-chain alkene with this formula:
**2-methylpropene**, in which a central carbon carries the double
bond to a CH₂ group and two single bonds to methyl groups — the
CH₂=C(CH₃)₂ arrangement. Drawn as a displayed formula, every one
of the three structures accounts for all eight hydrogens and all
four bonds at each carbon, which is the self-check that keeps
alkene drawing honest: a carbon in a double bond has only two
bonds left for other neighbours, not three.

The naming code decodes exactly as the alkane code did, with one
extra dial. The prefix (but-) counts the longest chain carrying
the double bond; the infix (-ene) announces the double bond; the
number (but-**1**-ene) says *which* carbon the double bond starts
at, numbering from the nearest end. Move the double bond, change
the number, change the compound — but-1-ene and but-2-ene are as
different as two isomers can be, even though their formulae are
identical.

One scope line matters here: with up to four carbon atoms you are
asked to draw the structural and displayed formulae and to name
the **unbranched-chain** isomers — but-1-ene and but-2-ene —
rather than any structure that differs only in the geometry *about*
the double bond. The cis/trans style of labelling is explicitly
not required, so if two drawings of but-2-ene differ only in which
side the end groups sit, treat them as one structure for this
course. Draw the three constitutional isomers of C₄H₈, name the
two unbranched ones, and statement 4.26 is fully served.

:::exam-hint
In "draw the alkene isomers of C₄H₈" items, examiners look for the
double bond *moving*, not just the chain branching. A complete
answer set is but-1-ene, but-2-ene and 2-methylpropene — and each
drawing must show the C=C explicitly. Two structures that differ
only by the geometry about the double bond earn no extra credit:
the course scope excludes that distinction.
:::

:::misconception
"But-1-ene and but-2-ene are the same molecule drawn slightly
differently." They are different compounds: in but-1-ene the
double bond joins carbons 1 and 2, in but-2-ene it joins carbons 2
and 3, so the atoms sit in genuinely different arrangements — the
defining test of an isomer pair. Compare them by asking where the
reactive C=C handle sits, and the difference becomes impossible to
unsee.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you draw and name the two unbranched alkene isomers of C₄H₈
with the double bond in the right place each time? Can you draw
the branched member of the set and check every carbon's four
bonds? Can you state what the number in but-1-ene tells you? Then
you own the isomer skill for this family.
:::
