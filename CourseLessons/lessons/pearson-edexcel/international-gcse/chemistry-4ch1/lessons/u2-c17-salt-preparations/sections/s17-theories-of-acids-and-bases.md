Chapter 16 (u2:c16) defined acids and alkalis by their ions: an acid is
something that produces H⁺(aq) ions in solution, an alkali something that
produces OH⁻(aq) ions. This ion-in-water definition is called the Arrhenius
theory of acids and bases — and it works perfectly for aqueous chemistry. When
hydrogen chloride gas dissolves in water to form hydrochloric acid, the
molecules dissociate to form hydrogen ions:

HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

A neutralisation reaction is then H⁺ from an acid reacting with OH⁻ from an
alkali to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). The problem with the Arrhenius
definition is its boundary: it only applies to reactions that occur in aqueous
solution, and chemistry does not stop at the water's edge. A more general
theory covers more situations:

- **An acid is a proton (hydrogen ion) donor.**
- **A base is a proton (hydrogen ion) acceptor.**

This is the [[bronsted-lowry-theory]], and understanding acids and bases in terms
of [[proton-transfer]] is what this chapter's objectives ask of you. (The
examination does not require you to remember the names of the theories — but
you do need the donor/acceptor definitions cold.) The vocabulary is honest:
a hydrogen atom is one proton with one electron, so if an electron is removed,
only a proton is left — H⁺ *is* a proton.

Rewritten in proton-transfer terms, the reaction when hydrogen chloride
dissolves in water becomes more truthful:

H₂O(l) + HCl(aq) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

The HCl donates (gives) a proton to an H₂O molecule — so the HCl is the acid
and the H₂O is the base, because it accepts the proton. The H₃O⁺(aq) ion is
called the [[hydroxonium-ion]] — this is the ion we usually write simply as
H⁺(aq). You can think of it as a hydrogen ion joined to a water molecule. The
Cl⁻ has its negative charge because the proton left its electron behind when
it transferred to the water.

The ammonium ion you already know is the same idea in another costume: NH₄⁺
is what you get when H⁺ is added to ammonia, NH₃ — and the hydroxonium ion is
the equivalent ion for water. In aqueous solution, ammonia reacts with water
to form OH⁻ ions (NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻), which is how ammonia fits the
ion-in-water picture; in proton-transfer terms, the ammonia is simply accepting
a proton from the water. Ammonia and hydrogen chloride do not even need the
water: the two gases react directly to form the white smoke of solid ammonium
chloride — NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s) — a proton handed from HCl to NH₃ with
no solution in sight.

There is even a dot-and-cross story inside the hydroxonium ion. The covalent
bond that forms between the H₂O and the H⁺ is unusual: both of the shared
electrons come from the oxygen. This is called a [[dative-covalent-bond]], or
a co-ordinate covalent bond — and once it has been formed, it is exactly the
same as a "normal" covalent bond.

:::exam-hint
The examination asks you to identify the proton donor and the proton acceptor
in a given reaction — never to name the theories. Read the equation, find who
gives the H⁺ (that is the acid) and who takes it (that is the base). Water
plays both roles across this chapter: accepting a proton from HCl, donating
one to ammonia and to oxide ions. The safest exam sentence is structural:
"the acid donates a proton and the base accepts it."
:::

:::misconception
"An acid must contain H⁺ ions ready-made, so ammonia can never be a base
because it has no hydrogen ion to give." The proton does not have to pre-exist
in the base — the base only has to *accept* one. Ammonia's lone pair reaches
out and takes H⁺ from water (making NH₄⁺) or from HCl (making NH₄Cl); it is
one of the cleanest proton acceptors in the course. Acids donate, bases accept
— contents of the formula are irrelevant to the definition.
:::
