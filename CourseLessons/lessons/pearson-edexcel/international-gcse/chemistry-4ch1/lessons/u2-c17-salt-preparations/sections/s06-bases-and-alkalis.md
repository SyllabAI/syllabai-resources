An [[alkali]] is a base that dissolves in water — and the soluble metal oxides
show how one becomes the other. Some metal oxides are soluble in water and
react with it to form solutions of metal hydroxides:

Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)

All the Group 1 oxides do this reaction, so for potassium oxide:

K₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq)

Most other metal oxides are not soluble in water. Calcium oxide dissolves
slightly to form calcium hydroxide:

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq)

Another alkali you will meet is [[ammonia-solution]] — a solution of ammonia.
The ammonia reacts with the water to form ammonium ions and hydroxide ions:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

All these solutions contain hydroxide ions — and alkalis are solutions that
are a source of hydroxide (OH⁻) ions, so every one of them is an alkali. The
reversible arrow on the ammonia equation is doing real work: only a fraction
of the dissolved ammonia reacts at any moment, which is why ammonia solution is
weakly alkaline while sodium hydroxide solution is strongly alkaline.

All metal hydroxides react with acids in a [[neutralisation]] reaction. Mixing
sodium hydroxide solution with dilute hydrochloric acid gives a colourless
solution, so not much seems to have happened — but repeat it with a thermometer
in the beaker and the temperature rises several degrees, showing that a
chemical change has occurred. Sodium chloride solution has been formed:

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

The [[ionic-equation]] for this reaction is the one that covers every acid–
alkali neutralisation:

OH⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) → H₂O(l)

All neutralisation reactions between an acid and an alkali share it: the
hydroxide ions from the alkali react with the H⁺ ions from the acid to form
water. Everything else in the equation is spectators.

**Carbonates and acids.** Carbonates react with cold dilute acids to produce
carbon dioxide gas, with a summary equation of their own:

**carbonate + acid → salt + carbon dioxide + water**

Green copper(II) carbonate reacts with the common dilute acids to give a blue
or blue-green solution of copper(II) sulfate, copper(II) nitrate or copper(II)
chloride, with carbon dioxide gas given off — recognisable because it turns
limewater milky. All three equations have the same form:

CuCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

CuCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

CuCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

Sodium carbonate with hydrochloric acid follows the same pattern:

Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

Show the ions and the carbonate story deepens. By not including the spectator
ions (the sodium and the chloride), the ionic equation for carbonate + acid is:

CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

Because carbonate ions react by accepting hydrogen ions, carbonate ions can be
classified as [[base]]s in proton-transfer terms — a preview of the theory at
the end of this chapter. In the practical specification, though, only metal oxides,
metal hydroxides and ammonia count as bases; carbonates are their own
reagent class.

:::exam-hint
The examiner rewards matching reagent to product: metal + acid → salt +
hydrogen; base + acid → salt + water; carbonate + acid → salt + carbon dioxide
+ water. State the observations too — carbonate reactions fizz in the *cold*,
while metal oxides usually need heating, and the gas that turns limewater milky
is carbon dioxide, never hydrogen. For the ionic equation, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O
scores for every acid–alkali pair; the carbonate ionic equation CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ →
CO₂ + H₂O is the extension-level version.
:::

:::misconception
"Sodium oxide must be an acid because it contains oxygen, or at least
neutral." Group 1 oxides are strongly basic: they react with water to make
hydroxide ions, which is the alkaline signature. The oxygen in Na₂O ends up as
O²⁻ ions that grab H⁺ from water — oxide ions are proton acceptors, the most
basic behaviour there is. Solubility, not oxygen content, decides acid/base
behaviour here.
:::
