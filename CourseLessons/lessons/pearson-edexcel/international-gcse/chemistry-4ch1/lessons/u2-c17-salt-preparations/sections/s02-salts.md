Every acid contains hydrogen. When that hydrogen is replaced by a metal, the
compound formed is called a [[salt]]. Magnesium sulfate is a salt, and so is
zinc chloride, and so is potassium nitrate. Sodium chloride — common salt — is
a salt too, but you do not normally make it in the lab because it occurs
naturally in enormous deposits; it is the exception that proves the rule.

Each acid founds its own family of salts. Sulfuric acid can be thought of as
the parent acid of all the sulfates: the salts formed from sulfuric acid are
all called sulfates. Hydrochloric acid gives chlorides, nitric acid gives
nitrates, ethanoic acid gives ethanoates and phosphoric acid gives phosphates.

| Acid | Formula | Example of salt | Name of salts |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric acid | HCl | NaCl | chlorides |
| nitric acid | HNO₃ | KNO₃ | nitrates |
| sulfuric acid | H₂SO₄ | CuSO₄ | sulfates |
| ethanoic acid | CH₃COOH | CH₃COONa | ethanoates |
| phosphoric acid | H₃PO₄ | K₃PO₄ | phosphates |

It does not matter if the replacement of the hydrogen cannot be done directly.
You cannot make copper(II) sulfate from copper and dilute sulfuric acid,
because they simply do not react — copper sits below hydrogen in the reactivity
series, as the next section explains. There are, however, other ways of making
copper(II) sulfate from sulfuric acid, and this chapter teaches all of them:
copper(II) sulfate is still a salt, whichever route delivers it.

Salts are also formed when the hydrogen in an acid is replaced with the
ammonium ion, NH₄⁺. These are [[ammonium-salt]]s — for example NH₄Cl (ammonium
chloride) and (NH₄)₂SO₄ (ammonium sulfate). Ammonium salts matter far beyond
the lab bench: ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate are two of the world's
most important fertiliser salts, which is why the nitrogen from nitric acid and
sulfuric acid ends up in the soil.

:::key-point
Of the common acids in the lab, nitric acid has much more complex reactions
with metals. You will not be asked about nitric acid reacting with metals at
International GCSE — the acid + metal rule in this chapter applies to
hydrochloric acid and sulfuric acid.
:::

:::exam-hint
The examiner expects you to move between an acid and its salt family without
hesitation: given H₂SO₄, say "sulfates" and quote CuSO₄; given "nitrates", say
"from nitric acid, HNO₃". Learn the table above as five matched pairs. A
favourite follow-up asks why copper(II) sulfate cannot be made by adding copper
to dilute sulfuric acid — the answer (no reaction, copper below hydrogen) earns
the mark, and "make it with copper(II) oxide instead" earns the respect.
:::

:::misconception
"'Salt' is a vague word, so any white powder counts." A salt is precisely
defined: the compound formed when the hydrogen in an acid is replaced by a
metal or by the ammonium ion. Copper(II) sulfate is a salt even though it is
blue, and ammonium chloride is a salt even though no metal atom appears
anywhere in it — the NH₄⁺ ion plays the role the metal ion plays in other
salts.
:::
