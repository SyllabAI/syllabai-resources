The next sections teach the practical methods for making salts — and before any
apparatus is picked up, one decision has to be made, because the method you use
depends entirely on solubility. Which of the starting substances are [[soluble]]
in water, and is the salt you want [[soluble]] or [[insoluble]]? Answer that
and the method chooses itself; answer it wrongly and no amount of careful
technique will rescue the experiment.

The decision tree has exactly three branches, and every salt-preparation
question in the course is secretly asking you to walk it:

1. **Soluble salt from an insoluble reactant.** Add the insoluble solid
   (a metal, a metal oxide, a metal hydroxide or a carbonate) to acid in
   excess, filter off the leftover solid, crystallise the solution. This
   covers copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide and the other workhorse
   preparations.
2. **Soluble salt from two soluble reactants.** When both reactants are
   solutions — as with all sodium, potassium and ammonium salts — there is no
   solid excess to filter, so an [[excess]] trick is impossible and a
   titration with an indicator does the measuring instead.
3. **Insoluble salt from two soluble reactants.** When the *product* is the
   insoluble one, no acid is needed at all: mix two solutions and the insoluble
   salt precipitates as a solid you can filter, wash and dry.

Each branch gets its own section, with the full practical detail the
examination statements demand.

:::exam-hint
"Describe an experiment to prepare a pure, dry sample of..." is a *describe*
question — the marks are for the method in order, not for chemistry theory.
The first sentence of your answer should signal the branch: "add excess
copper(II) oxide to warm dilute sulfuric acid" (branch 1), "titrate, then
repeat without indicator" (branch 2), "mix lead(II) nitrate solution with
sodium sulfate solution" (branch 3). Choosing the branch correctly is worth
more marks than any single step, because every later mark depends on it.
:::

:::misconception
"The method for making a salt depends on the acid you start from." It depends
on solubility — of the reactants and the product. Sulfuric acid appears in all
three branches: warming it with copper(II) oxide gives crystals (branch 1),
titrating it against sodium hydroxide gives sodium sulfate (branch 2), and
using it as the sulfate source for barium sulfate gives a precipitate (branch
3). Solubility decides; the acid follows.
:::
