It would seem much easier to just boil off all the water rather than
crystallising the solution slowly — but evaporating a copper(II) sulfate
solution to dryness would not give you blue copper(II) sulfate crystals at all.
Instead, you would produce a white powder of [[anhydrous]] copper(II) sulfate.
The blue crystals and the white powder are the same compound with the same
formula unit — what differs is the water that the crystals carry inside their
structure.

When many salts form their crystals, water from the solution becomes
chemically bound up with the salt. This is called [[water-of-crystallisation]].
A salt which contains water of crystallisation is said to be [[hydrated]]. The
crystallisation half of the copper(II) sulfate preparation is therefore a
chemical change of its own, with its own equation:

CuSO₄(aq) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)

Five water molecules lock into the lattice for every copper sulfate formula
unit — the blue colour of the crystals belongs to that combined water. Boil
past this point and the water of crystallisation is driven off, the lattice
collapses into the anhydrous form, and the blue gives way to white. Slow
cooling is what lets the crystals take their water up in an orderly way; the
glass-rod saturation test from the last section tells you when to stop heating
and start cooling.

You have met this arithmetic before: Chapter 5 (u1:c05) worked the molar mass
of CuSO₄·5H₂O and the water-of-crystallisation calculations that go with it.
This chapter is where that dot-and-five-H₂O notation is actually made on the
bench — the practical in the last section exists precisely to deliver the
hydrated crystals, and stopping at the right moment is what separates a tray of
blue crystals from a spoonful of white powder.

:::exam-hint
Two equations, two products, one error to avoid. Crystallisation:
CuSO₄(aq) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) — blue hydrated crystals. Over-heating:
the water of crystallisation is lost and the white anhydrous solid remains.
Questions award the marks for naming water of crystallisation, writing the
dot formula correctly, and stating that evaporating to dryness gives the
anhydrous salt instead. The dryness rule — never evaporate to dryness — is now
chemistry, not just safety: it protects the very water the crystals need.
:::

:::misconception
"The blue crystals are copper(II) sulfate, and the white powder is some
different sulfate made by a side reaction." There is no side reaction and no
different compound — both are copper(II) sulfate. The crystals are the
*hydrated* form, CuSO₄·5H₂O, with five waters of crystallisation built into
each lattice; the powder is the *anhydrous* form with none. The colour change
from blue to white is the water leaving, which is why anhydrous copper(II)
sulfate turns blue again the moment water touches it.
:::
