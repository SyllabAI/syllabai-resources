The magnesium sulfate preparation runs the same seven beats as the copper(II)
sulfate practical, with one welcome difference: no heating is needed at the
reacting stage. Add [[excess]] magnesium to dilute sulfuric acid — the acid
does not have to be warmed, because magnesium sits high enough in the
reactivity series to react in the cold. As you add the magnesium, the reaction
mixture fizzes (hydrogen is given off). Keep adding magnesium until the fizzing
stops and some magnesium is left in the beaker — that leftover tells you all
the acid has reacted. The equation for the formation of the solution is:

Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

Again the solution is concentrated by heating it gently and then allowed to
crystallise. The crystallisation reaction is:

MgSO₄(aq) + 7H₂O(l) → MgSO₄·7H₂O(s)

Seven waters of crystallisation this time, not five — each hydrated salt
carries its own fixed number, which is why the crystallisation equations are
never interchangeable between salts.

**How do you know whether you need to heat the mixture?** Carbonates react with
dilute acids in the cold, and so does magnesium. Most other things you are
likely to use — copper(II) oxide chief among them — need to be heated for the
reaction to start. The rule of thumb: high-reactivity metals and carbonates
react cold; metal oxides and the slower metals need the warm acid.

:::key-point
[[methyl-orange]] is red in acidic solutions and yellow in alkaline ones. When
it is used as an indicator in titrations involving adding acid to alkali, an
orange colour is usually obtained at the [[endpoint]] — orange is a mixture of
the red and yellow forms. This detail matters two sections from now, when
methyl orange announces the endpoint of the sodium sulfate titration.
:::

:::exam-hint
Comparing the two crystal preparations is a reliable exam question. Magnesium
sulfate: excess magnesium, no heating to react, fizzing tells you the acid is
still there, stop when fizzing stops with metal left over. Copper(II) sulfate:
excess copper(II) oxide, warm the acid, no fizzing to watch — the leftover
black solid is your signal. The marks live in the *why*: excess guarantees all
the acid reacts, and the visible leftover proves it. Both crystallisations end
the same way: concentrate, glass-rod test, cool slowly, filter, dry.
:::

:::misconception
"Magnesium sulfate and copper(II) sulfate crystals must have the same formula
shape, so it is MgSO₄·5H₂O as well." The number of waters of crystallisation
is fixed for each salt and measured for each salt: magnesium sulfate heptahydrate
is MgSO₄·7H₂O, copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate is CuSO₄·5H₂O. Copying the
5 across is exactly the kind of slip that loses the equation mark — each
crystallisation equation states its own water count.
:::
