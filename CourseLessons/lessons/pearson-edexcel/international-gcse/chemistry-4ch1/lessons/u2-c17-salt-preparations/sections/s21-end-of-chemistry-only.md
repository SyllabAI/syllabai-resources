This section closes the chapter — and with it, the stretch of Unit 2 that
carries chemistry-only content. Everything the chapter taught now compresses
into ten abilities, one per objective, and each is a question shape you will
meet on the page.

:::summary
A salt is an acid with its hydrogen replaced — by a metal, or by the ammonium
ion — and each acid names its own family: chlorides, nitrates, sulfates,
ethanoates, phosphates. Dilute acids react with metals above hydrogen (metal +
acid → salt + hydrogen, MASH), with bases (metal oxide + acid → salt + water),
and with carbonates in the cold (carbonate + acid → salt + carbon dioxide +
water); the ionic equation Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂ shows why every simple acid
reacts identically. The solubility rules — sodium/potassium/ammonium and
nitrates always soluble; silver and lead(II) chlorides; barium, calcium and
lead(II) sulfates; carbonates and hydroxides insoluble except Na/K/NH₄ — choose
the method: excess solid gives crystals (copper(II) sulfate from copper(II)
oxide, hydrated as CuSO₄·5H₂O); a [[titration]] gives the sodium, potassium and
ammonium salts, repeated without indicator; a [[precipitation-reaction]] gives
the insoluble salts, filtered, washed with distilled water and dried. Beneath it all, acids
are proton donors and bases are proton acceptors: HCl gives H⁺ to water (the
[[hydroxonium-ion]] in disguise), ammonia accepts H⁺ straight from the acid,
and water itself plays both roles.
:::

:::can-you
Keyed to the ten objectives, in order: state the solubility rules and their
exceptions, classifying any named compound (2.34); explain acid–base reactions
as proton transfer (2.35); identify the proton donor and acceptor in a given
equation (2.36); write the equations for hydrochloric, sulfuric and nitric
acids with metals, bases and metal carbonates — excluding nitric acid with
metals (2.37); list what can act as a base and define an alkali (2.38);
describe the excess-solid preparation of a pure, dry soluble salt (2.39);
describe the acid + alkali preparation via titration, indicator-free second
run included (2.40C, Paper 2C only); describe the precipitation of an
insoluble salt from two soluble solutions (2.41C, Paper 2C only); walk the
copper(II) sulfate crystal practical end to end (2.42); and describe the
lead(II) sulfate preparation with its distilled-water washing (2.43C, Paper 2C
only).
:::

:::misconception
"'Describe an experiment' questions are about the chemistry, so the marks live
in the equation." The marks live in the *sequence*. Every preparation question
in this chapter is scored as an ordered method — reagents, excess or titration
or two-solution mix, filtration, washing, crystallising, drying — and the
equation is one mark among many. Write the method as numbered steps, say *why*
at the two justified moments (excess solid proves the acid is gone; distilled
water protects the dry sample), and the equation lands on top of an answer
that is already most of the way scored.
:::

:::cross-curricular
Salt preparation is an industrial art as much as a lab skill. The ammonium
sulfate route you titrated this chapter is run at tonne scale to make
fertiliser that feeds a measurable share of the world's grain; barium
sulfate's total refusal to dissolve — the solubility rule you learned — is
exactly why it is safe to swallow as a contrast agent for gut X-rays, coating
the tract without entering the blood; and silver chloride's photosensitivity,
first noticed by lab chemists studying precipitates, seeded the entire
photographic industry. The burette technique, the filtration discipline and
the distilled-water standard are the same in a school beaker and a pharmaceutical
plant.
:::
