Before the practical methods come the reactions they are built on. Simple
dilute acids react with metals depending on their positions in the
[[reactivity-series]], and the rules are worth stating exactly:

- Metals below hydrogen in the series do not react with dilute acids. Copper,
  silver and gold are safe in dilute acid — which is why copper pipes survive
  decades of water service.
- Metals above hydrogen in the series react to produce hydrogen gas.
- The higher the metal sits in the reactivity series, the more vigorous the
  reaction. You would never mix metals such as sodium or potassium with acids,
  because their reactions are too violent to be useful or safe.

For metals above hydrogen, the reaction has one summary equation:

**metal + acid → salt + hydrogen**

A mnemonic holds the whole rule together: **MASH** — **M**etal + **A**cid →
**S**alt + **H**ydrogen. Magnesium ribbon dropped into dilute sulfuric acid is
the classic demonstration, and the next section watches it happen observation
by observation.

:::exam-hint
Questions love the boundary cases. A metal below hydrogen (copper) + dilute
acid: no reaction — and "no reaction, because copper is below hydrogen in the
reactivity series" is the full-mark answer. Sodium or potassium + acid: reacts
so violently it is never done. A mid-series metal (magnesium, zinc, iron):
reacts steadily to give a [[salt]] and hydrogen. If asked to compare two
metals, the higher one in the series fizzes faster — rate of fizzing tracks
reactivity.
:::

:::misconception
"'Metal + acid' works for every metal." It works only for metals above hydrogen
in the reactivity series, and it is only *practical* for the moderately
reactive stretch from magnesium to iron — below that the reaction is too slow
to bother with, and far above it the reaction is explosively fast. Copper,
silver and gold give no hydrogen with dilute acids at all, however long you
wait.
:::
