The excess-solid method fails completely for sodium, potassium and ammonium
salts, and the reason is the first line of the solubility rules: all sodium,
potassium and ammonium compounds are soluble. The solid you add to the acid
would not only react with it — any excess would simply dissolve in the water
present. You would have no visible excess to filter off, and no simple way of
seeing when you have added just enough solid to neutralise the acid.

You normally make these salts from sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide
solution, or from ammonia solution — the carbonates work too. Fortunately the
solutions of all of these are alkaline, which means an indicator can tell you
when you have a neutral solution. The method for finding out exactly how much
of two solutions you need for them to neutralise each other is the
[[titration]] you learned in Chapter 16 (u2:c16): carry out the titration with
an indicator to find the reacting volumes, then mix those same volumes again
without the indicator. This objective is chemistry-only — the whole method is
assessed in Paper 2C only.

**The practical, step by step.**

1. Transfer 25 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution to a conical flask using a
   pipette, and add a few drops of [[methyl-orange]] as the indicator.
2. Run dilute sulfuric acid in from the burette until the indicator just turns
   from yellow to orange.
3. Note the volume of acid needed, then mix the same volumes of acid and alkali
   together in a clean flask **without any indicator**. The equation for the
   formation of the solution is:

   2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

4. Heat the solution to evaporate off some of the water until a saturated
   solution is formed, then leave it to cool so that crystals form.
5. Separate the crystals from any remaining solution by filtration (or pour
   the remaining solution away).
6. Dry the crystals by leaving them in a warm place.

The crystallisation process has its own equation:

Na₂SO₄(aq) + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂SO₄·10H₂O(s)

Ten waters of crystallisation for sodium sulfate. To make sodium chloride
crystals instead, you would run dilute hydrochloric acid from the burette
rather than dilute sulfuric acid, then follow the same procedure:

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Ammonium salts come off the same production line. Using
[[ammonium-salt]]s rather than metal salts makes no difference to the method
itself — ammonia solution plays the role the sodium hydroxide solution played:

2NH₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq)

Why repeat the titration without the indicator? Because an indicator is an
impurity by design — dye molecules have no business inside a "pure, dry
sample". The first, indicator-carrying titration is a measurement; the second,
indicator-free mixing is the actual preparation.

:::key-point
Why methyl orange can never mark the exact neutral point: below pH 3.1 it is
red and above pH 4.4 it is yellow, so between those pH values it shows a
mixture of the two — orange. That means it is actually yellow at pH 7, so the
colour change does not sit exactly at neutrality. It does not matter: the pH
changes very rapidly near the [[endpoint]] of a titration, so a carefully done
titration still finds the neutralisation point to the nearest drop — which is
excellent.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Describe an experiment to prepare a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt,
starting from an acid and alkali" (2.40C, Paper 2C only) scores in beats:
pipette a fixed volume of alkali into a conical flask; add a few drops of
indicator; titrate with acid from the burette to the endpoint; record the
volume; repeat the same volumes with **no indicator**; evaporate some water to
saturation; cool to crystallise; filter; dry in a warm place. Two mark-magnets
inside the method: say *why* the titration is repeated without indicator (the
indicator would contaminate the product), and write the equation for the salt
actually named in the question.
:::

:::misconception
"Sodium chloride crystals contain water of crystallisation, like sodium
sulfate does — so the method must be identical in every detail." Simple sodium,
potassium and ammonium salts such as NaCl and (NH₄)₂SO₄ have **no** water of
crystallisation, so in principle you could evaporate all the water in one go
and save time. The disadvantage is the product: powdery crystals or a solid
crust instead of proper crystals. It is always better to concentrate the
solution and let crystals form slowly. And for ammonium salts there is a
second reason: heating dry ammonium salts tends to break them up.
:::
