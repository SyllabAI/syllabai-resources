Acids do not just sit in bottles waiting to be classified — they react. Chapter
16 (u2:c16) gave you the map: how to tell an acid from an alkali, how the pH
scale bands them, and what neutralisation looks like ion by ion. This chapter
is about what acids actually **do**: how they attack metals, how they are
neutralised by bases and carbonates, and how those reactions are turned into a
laboratory skill — making salts, on purpose, pure and dry. When the hydrogen of
an acid is replaced by a metal (or by the ammonium ion), the compound that forms
is called a [[salt]] — and by the end of this chapter you will be able to make
several of them from scratch.

Four ideas organise everything that follows. First, **the reactions of acids**:
with metals above hydrogen, with metal oxides and hydroxides, and with
carbonates — each with its own summary equation and its own observations.
Second, **the solubility rules**: which ionic compounds dissolve in water and
which do not, because the method you choose for making a salt depends entirely
on whether your starting materials and your product are [[soluble]] or
[[insoluble]]. Third, **the three preparation methods**: crystallising a
soluble salt from an insoluble reactant, running a titration when both
reactants are solutions, and precipitating an insoluble salt out of two
solutions. Fourth, **the theory underneath it all**: acids and bases defined by
proton transfer — an idea that explains why ammonia, with no hydroxide ions of
its own, still neutralises an acid.

:::prior-knowledge
Four earlier chapters load this one. From Chapter 16 (u2:c16) you need acids as
sources of hydrogen ions, alkalis as sources of hydroxide ions, and the
neutralisation ionic equation H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). From Chapter 14
(u2:c14) you need the reactivity series, because whether a metal reacts with a
dilute acid — and how violently — is decided by its position in that series.
From Chapter 7 (u1:c07) you need ionic compounds existing as separated ions in
solution, which powers every ionic equation here. From Chapter 5 (u1:c05) you
need balanced equations with state symbols, plus water of crystallisation —
the CuSO₄·5H₂O you calculated there is made for real in this chapter.
:::

:::misconception
"'Salt' means the salt you put on your chips." Sodium chloride is one salt out
of an enormous family — magnesium sulfate, zinc chloride, potassium nitrate,
ammonium chloride and lead(II) sulfate are all salts too. In chemistry, a salt
is any compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal or
by the ammonium ion. Some salts are [[soluble]], some are stubbornly
[[insoluble]], some hold water of crystallisation in their crystals — and the
table-salt on your chips is simply the one your body happens to need in
largest quantity.
:::
