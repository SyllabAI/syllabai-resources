Bases are substances that neutralise acids by combining with the hydrogen ions
in them to produce water. When referring to a [[base]] at this level, you
usually mean a [[metal-oxide]], a metal hydroxide or ammonia — that trio is
exactly what the specification names, and keeping it in your head is an exam
point in itself.

Metal oxides, such as copper(II) oxide and magnesium oxide, are bases. Heat
black copper(II) oxide powder with hot dilute sulfuric acid and the black solid
reacts to produce a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate:

CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

All the metal oxide and acid combinations you will meet at International GCSE
behave in the same way as copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid: they
produce a [[salt]] and water. Most need to be heated for the reaction to
start — the cold beaker of acid and black powder does nothing until the
Bunsen flame arrives.

Copper(II) oxide is an ionic compound containing the O²⁻ ion, and that ion
explains the reaction: the H⁺ ions from the acid have combined with the O²⁻
ions to form water, H₂O. This is a [[neutralisation]] — the base has consumed
the acid's hydrogen ions. The general equation for the reaction of a metal
oxide with an acid is:

**metal oxide + acid → salt + water**

:::exam-hint
Two facts about bases earn marks again and again. First, the list: metal
oxides, metal hydroxides and ammonia can all act as bases (2.38 names exactly
that list — carbonates are *not* on it, even though they neutralise acids).
Second, the products: base + acid always gives a salt and water, never
hydrogen and never carbon dioxide — those belong to metals and carbonates
respectively. Most oxide–acid reactions need heating to start; carbonate–acid
reactions do not.
:::

:::misconception
"'Base' and 'alkali' are two spellings of the same word." They are not. A base
neutralises an acid; an alkali is a base that is *soluble in water*. Copper(II)
oxide is a base but not an alkali — it will not dissolve, so it never gives an
alkaline solution. Sodium hydroxide is both. The next section turns this
distinction into equations you can write.
:::
