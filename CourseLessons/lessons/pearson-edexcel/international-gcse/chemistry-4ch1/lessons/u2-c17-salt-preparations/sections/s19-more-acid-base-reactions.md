Acids in solution are acidic because they donate a proton to water, forming
the [[hydroxonium-ion]]. Take a general acid, HA — this is what happens when
it is put into water:

HA(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq)

The HA donates a proton to the H₂O, which acts as a base. For nitric acid this
would be:

HNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)

Bases behave in the mirror-image way. When bases such as metal oxides react
with water, they accept a proton *from* the water — for sodium oxide:

Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)

The O²⁻ ion accepts protons from the water, and in this reaction the water acts
as an acid, because it donates the protons. The same donor/acceptor reading
covers the neutralisations of the earlier sections: when sulfuric acid reacts
with copper(II) oxide,

CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

the H₂SO₄ is the acid — it donates protons to the CuO, the base. And ammonia
is also a base: it accepts a proton from water,

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

What about the classic acid–alkali equation, HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) +
H₂O(l)? [[proton-transfer]] chemistry says what *actually* happens: the H₃O⁺
ion formed when the acid reacts with water donates a proton to the base, OH⁻.
But for International GCSE purposes you almost always use the simplified
version:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Both sentences describe the same [[neutralisation]] — the simplified equation is
the hydroxonium story with H₃O⁺ abbreviated to H⁺, exactly as the theory
section established. Water's double role is the theme of the whole section: it
accepts a proton from every acid (acting as a base), and donates one to oxide
ions (acting as an acid). It never stops being neutral — these labels describe
what water does *in each reaction*, not what it is in the bottle.

:::exam-hint
Given any equation, the question "state which substance is the acid and which
is the base" is answered by following the H⁺: whoever loses it is the acid,
whoever gains it is the base. Expect water to be cast in either role —
HNO₃ + H₂O makes water the base; Na₂O + H₂O makes water the acid. The
simplified ionic equation H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O is what you write for acid–alkali
neutralisation marks; the hydroxonium version is the understanding behind it,
and saying so earns the explain mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Water must be the base in every equation it appears in, because acids are
aqueous." Water's role is decided equation by equation. With an acid (HA +
H₂O → H₃O⁺ + A⁻), water accepts the proton — base. With an oxide ion
(Na₂O + H₂O → 2Na⁺ + 2OH⁻), water donates the proton — acid. The substance
that *donates* in one reaction can *accept* in the next; only the direction of
proton flow in the specific equation decides the labels.
:::
