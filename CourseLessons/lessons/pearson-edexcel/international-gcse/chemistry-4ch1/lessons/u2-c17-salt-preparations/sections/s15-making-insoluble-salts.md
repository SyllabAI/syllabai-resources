The basic procedure for making an [[insoluble]] salt is to mix solutions of
two soluble salts. The insoluble salt forms as a [[precipitate]], and a
solution of a soluble one is left behind. For instance, to make insoluble
silver chloride you mix solutions of silver nitrate and sodium chloride — the
reaction met two sections ago, now with its mechanism opened up.

Silver nitrate solution contains silver ions and nitrate ions in solution. The
positive and negative ions attract each other, but the attractions are not
strong enough to make them stick together. Sodium chloride solution is the
same story: sodium ions and chloride ions, attracted but not stuck. When you
mix the two solutions, the various ions meet each other — and when silver ions
meet chloride ions, the attractions are strong enough that the ions clump
together into a solid. The sodium and nitrate ions remain in solution because
they are not sufficiently attracted to each other. Which ions clump and which
stay free is decided by the solubility rules you already know: silver chloride
is an insoluble exception, sodium nitrate rides the all-soluble first line.

The [[ionic-equation]]s for precipitation reactions are the easiest in the
course to write, because all that happens is that the ions of the insoluble
salt come together to form the solid — the ionic equation simply shows exactly
that. The method has three moves:

- write the formula for the precipitate (the insoluble salt) on the
  right-hand side of the equation
- write the formulae for the ions that come together to produce it on the
  left-hand side
- do not forget the state symbols

For the formation of silver chloride:

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

For the formation of barium sulfate:

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

For the formation of lead(II) iodide — the bright yellow precipitate:

Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s)

You do not need to worry about the spectator ions in these equations, because
they are not doing anything — the sodium, nitrate and leftover ions stay
exactly where they were, dissolved.

:::safety
Wear eye protection. Lead nitrate and lead sulfate are toxic, and all skin
contact must be avoided. Lead(II) iodide shares the hazard — treat every lead
compound in this section as one to measure with a spatula, never with fingers,
and wash hands after the practical even if nothing touched them.
:::

:::exam-hint
The ionic-equation marks are near-automatic if you follow the three moves and
keep the state symbols: aqueous ions on the left, solid precipitate on the
right. Balance charges as you go — lead(II) needs two iodide ions, so
Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s). A favourite extension asks for the ionic
equation of a precipitation you have never seen: the recipe is always "formula
of the insoluble salt on the right; its ions, in solution, on the left."
:::

:::misconception
"The full equation and the ionic equation are two versions of different
reactions." They are the same reaction at two zoom levels. The full equation
records everything present — Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2NaI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq) —
while the ionic equation records everything *happening*: only the two ions
that build the precipitate. The sodium and nitrate ions appear in the full
equation as spectators, unchanged; leaving them out is not simplification for
beginners, it is the honest description of the chemistry.
:::
