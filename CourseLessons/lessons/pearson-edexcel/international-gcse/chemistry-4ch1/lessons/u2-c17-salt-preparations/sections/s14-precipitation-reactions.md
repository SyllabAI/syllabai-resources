Mix silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution and the mixture
suddenly clouds: a white solid appears as if from nowhere, hanging in the
liquid. That solid is silver chloride — an [[insoluble]] salt forming from two
soluble ones — and the process that made it is a [[precipitation-reaction]].
This objective is chemistry-only: preparing insoluble salts this way is
assessed in Paper 2C only.

A [[precipitate]] is a solid that is formed by a chemical reaction involving
liquids or gases. A precipitation reaction is simply a reaction that produces a
precipitate. In this reaction, a fine white precipitate of silver chloride is
produced — white precisely because silver chloride will not dissolve in water,
so the solid appears as countless tiny particles suspended in the mixture
before settling. The same reaction appears again in the next chapter wearing a
different hat: mixing a solution containing chloride ions with acidified silver
nitrate solution is *the test* for chloride ions, and the white precipitate is
the positive result.

The chemistry-only flag matters for route planning: Paper 1 never asks you to
describe an insoluble-salt preparation, so if you are sitting Paper 1 only,
this section and the next two are context, not examinable technique. If you are
sitting Paper 2, this is marks on the table — the description questions are
formulaic once you know the pattern.

:::key-point
Chemists' drawings of ions in solution leave the water molecules out to avoid
cluttering the diagram. The water is still there — that is what the (aq) state
symbol means — it is just not drawn. When you sketch ions mixing in a
precipitation reaction, the same convention applies: draw the four ions of
interest, leave the water molecules implied.
:::

:::exam-hint
Name the solid *and* its colour: "a white precipitate of silver chloride"
scores; "a white solid forms" does not. The precipitate's identity comes from
the solubility rules — chlorides except silver and lead(II), so silver
chloride must be the insoluble one. Watch for the gas-trap confusion: nothing
fizzes in this reaction, nothing pops, nothing turns limewater milky — the
only event is a solid appearing in solution.
:::

:::misconception
"The solid must have been sitting in one of the two solutions all along, just
invisible." Nothing was hiding: silver nitrate solution is perfectly clear, and
so is sodium chloride solution. The solid exists only *after* the two
solutions meet — silver ions and chloride ions were both dissolved, free and
separated, until the mixing put them face to face and the attractions became
strong enough to build a lattice. Precipitates are products, not stowaways.
:::
