The procedure for making insoluble salts is to mix two solutions of soluble
salts — but how do you know *which* two? Two really useful facts from the
solubility rules decide it:

- all nitrates are [[soluble]]
- all sodium and potassium salts are soluble

So all you have to do is mix the **nitrate** of the metal part of your
[[insoluble]] salt with the **sodium or potassium salt** of the non-metal part.
Both chosen solutions are guaranteed soluble — the nitrate by rule one, the
sodium or potassium salt by the all-soluble first line — and the two unwanted
spectator ions are therefore guaranteed to stay dissolved.

Take lead(II) iodide, the yellow [[precipitate]]. The metal part is lead, so
use a solution of lead(II) nitrate; the non-metal part is iodide, so use a
solution of sodium iodide. The equation for the reaction is:

Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2NaI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq)

Barium sulfate works the same way: use barium nitrate solution and potassium
sulfate solution.

Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + K₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)

The barium sulfate is a white precipitate. There are other valid pairings too:
barium chloride is soluble, so it works in place of barium nitrate — and the
sulfate part does not have to come from a salt at all, because dilute sulfuric
acid contains sulfate ions, so it can supply them directly.

**The specification's own practical (2.43C): pure, dry lead(II) sulfate.**

1. Take 25 cm³ of lead(II) nitrate solution in a beaker and add 25 cm³ of
   sodium sulfate solution. The amounts do not really matter, because any
   excess will be got rid of later.
2. A white precipitate of lead(II) sulfate forms. The equation is:

   Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq)

3. Filter the reaction mixture. A white residue of lead(II) sulfate is left on
   the filter paper; a colourless solution of sodium nitrate — plus any excess
   reactants — passes through. The white residue is the compound wanted, but
   it is contaminated with the solution clinging to it.
4. Wash the residue with distilled water: pour a 20 cm³ portion (the amount is
   not important) onto the filter paper and let it run through, carrying the
   sodium nitrate and excess reactants with it. Repeat several times so the
   sample is not contaminated with anything.
5. Transfer the filter paper and the lead(II) sulfate to a warm oven to dry —
   the water evaporates and the pure, dry solid remains.

The washing step has a reason worth stating: tap water contains dissolved
substances, so washing with tap water and then drying would leave those
dissolved solids behind on the crystals as they evaporate — contaminating the
very sample the preparation exists to make. Distilled (pure) water leaves
nothing behind.

:::exam-hint
"Choose solutions to make [insoluble salt] X" is a template question: metal
part → its nitrate; non-metal part → the sodium or potassium salt; write the
full equation with the precipitate's state symbol. Marks then move to the
purification beats — filter, wash with *distilled* water, repeat the washing,
dry in a warm oven — and the justify-mark belongs to the word distilled: tap
water would leave its dissolved solids behind on the product. This is also the
lead(II) sulfate practical (2.43C), so it can be asked as a describe-the-
practical with all six beats required.
:::

:::misconception
"Any soluble salt of the metal will do for step one — lead(II) chloride, say."
Lead(II) chloride is *insoluble* — it is on the exception list — so it cannot
be one of your two starting solutions, and it is not "a solution" to begin
with. The nitrate route is not a stylistic preference: the metal's nitrate is
the one soluble salt you can name for *every* metal, which is why the
convention is nitrates for the metal part, sodium or potassium salts for the
non-metal part.
:::
