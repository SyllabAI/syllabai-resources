The solubility rules in the next section compress a whole lab's worth of
experience into six lines, and two honest caveats make them easier to use.
First, to keep the rules simple, they include one or two compounds — aluminium
carbonate, for example — that do not actually exist. Do not worry about these:
the problem will not arise at International GCSE. If a question names a
compound, treat the rules as if it exists and answer from the pattern.

Second, there is no sharp line between "[[insoluble]]" and "almost insoluble"
compounds. Nothing dissolves exactly zero. The substances picked out as almost
insoluble — calcium hydroxide is the famous example — include the more common
ones you will need elsewhere in the course, which is precisely why they get
their own special mention instead of being swept into the insoluble column.

The reason the exceptions cluster where they do is worth understanding rather
than memorising: the exceptions in the carbonates and hydroxides exist because
all sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds are soluble, so a sodium, potassium
or ammonium carbonate or hydroxide inherits that solubility and drags its whole
family towards the soluble side. Except for the carbonates and hydroxides,
most of these compounds are [[soluble]] — so the rules that actually need
learning are the exceptions in the sulfates and the chlorides. Learn those two
exception lists cold and the rest follows from the pattern.

:::exam-hint
Examiners set solubility questions straight from these rules: "state whether
silver chloride is soluble" (no — chloride exception), "is barium sulfate
soluble?" (no — sulfate exception), "is sodium carbonate soluble?" (yes — all
sodium compounds are). Note how the "almost insoluble" subtlety is itself
examinable through calcium hydroxide, whose slight solubility is what lets
limewater exist at all.
:::

:::misconception
"'Insoluble' means none of it dissolves — ever." Even the most stubborn
"insoluble" salts leave a trace of ions in solution; that trace is exactly what
acidified silver nitrate detects in the chloride test, and exactly what makes
calcium hydroxide solution (limewater) possible. Insoluble in practice means
"so slightly soluble that a solid is seen when you mix the solutions" — the
precipitate line you will meet later in this chapter.
:::
