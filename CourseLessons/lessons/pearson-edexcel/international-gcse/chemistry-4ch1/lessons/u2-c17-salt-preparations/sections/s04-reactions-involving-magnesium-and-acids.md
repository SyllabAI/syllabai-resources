Drop magnesium ribbon into dilute sulfuric acid and every observation arrives
at once: rapid fizzing, a colourless gas that pops with a lighted splint (the
test for hydrogen), a reaction mixture that becomes noticeably warm as heat is
produced, and the magnesium gradually disappearing to leave a colourless
solution of magnesium sulfate. The equation tells the same story:

Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

This is a displacement reaction. The more reactive magnesium has displaced the
less reactive hydrogen from the acid. Remember that ionic compounds are present
as separate ions in solution, so in the magnesium sulfate solution the
Mg²⁺(aq) and SO₄²⁻(aq) ions are separated from each other — which is exactly
what makes the ionic-equation view below possible.

Repeat the experiment with dilute hydrochloric acid and the reaction looks
exactly the same — same fizzing, same pop, same warmth. The only difference is
the salt formed: a solution of magnesium chloride this time.

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Why do two different acids give identical reactions? Because both contain the
same reactive particle. Acids in solution form ions: dilute sulfuric acid
contains hydrogen ions and sulfate ions; dilute hydrochloric acid contains
hydrogen ions and chloride ions. Write the sulfuric acid reaction out in full
and the sulfate ion sits on both sides, untouched:

Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + H₂(g)

The sulfate ion has not been changed by the reaction. It is a
[[spectator-ion]], and so it is left out of the [[ionic-equation]]:

Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)

Repeating this with hydrochloric acid shows the chloride ions are spectators
too — strike them out and the same ionic equation appears again. The reactions
look the same because they **are** the same: all solutions of acids contain
hydrogen ions, so magnesium reacts with any simple dilute acid in the same way.

Zinc tells the same tale more slowly. Sitting lower down the reactivity series
than magnesium, zinc reacts with both acids at a gentler pace:

Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

And once the spectators are struck out, zinc's ionic equation matches
magnesium's exactly — only the metal's symbol changes:

Zn(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)

:::exam-hint
The observation marks come in fours: *fizzing* (hydrogen given off), *pops
with a lighted splint* (the gas test), *temperature rises* (exothermic), *metal
disappears leaving a colourless solution* (the salt). For ionic-equation
marks, the examiner wants the spectator struck out — writing the sulfate or
chloride into the final ionic equation loses the mark. And the explain-mark is
one sentence: acids react identically because all acid solutions contain
hydrogen ions.
:::

:::misconception
"Fizzing when a metal meets an acid means carbon dioxide is bubbling off."
Carbon dioxide comes from carbonates, not from metals. Metal + acid gives
**hydrogen**, identified by the lighted splint pop. The limewater test never
enters a metal–acid question — if the gas pops with a lighted splint, hydrogen
is the only candidate, and the salt that forms contains the metal, not any
carbon.
:::
