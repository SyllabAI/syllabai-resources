Here are the rules for predicting the solubility of ionic compounds in water —
the specification's list, stated as you will use them:

- all sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds are [[soluble]]
- all nitrates are soluble
- most common chlorides are soluble, except lead(II) chloride and silver
  chloride
- most common sulfates are soluble, except lead(II) sulfate, barium sulfate,
  silver sulfate and calcium sulfate
- most common carbonates are [[insoluble]], except sodium, potassium and
  ammonium carbonates
- most metal hydroxides are insoluble (or almost insoluble), except sodium,
  potassium and ammonium hydroxides; calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble in
  water

Two of these lines are the specification's own exceptions to know by heart:
common chlorides are soluble except those of silver and lead(II), and common
sulfates are soluble except those of barium, calcium and lead(II). The fuller
sulfate list above adds silver sulfate, which is also extremely reluctant to
dissolve — worth knowing when the ion tests arrive in the next chapter, because
that reluctance is exactly what those tests exploit.

Notice the load-bearing first line: sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds
are always soluble, no matter what the rest of the rule says. That single line
rescues sodium carbonate from the insoluble-carbonate family, keeps potassium
hydroxide among the soluble hydroxides, and — as the next sections show — makes
the excess-solid method impossible for every sodium, potassium and ammonium
salt.

:::safety
Direct heating of the acid is not recommended in any of these preparations.
Wear eye protection and never evaporate a salt solution to dryness over a
direct flame. The acid is best heated in a boiling tube placed in a water
bath. Avoid skin contact with the crystals — several prepared salts, including
copper(II) sulfate, are harmful and irritating to both skin and eyes.
:::

:::exam-hint
Solubility marks are the cheapest marks in the paper — provided you quote the
exceptions exactly. The three insoluble chlorides-and-sulfates stars are
**silver**, **lead(II)** and **barium**; calcium joins the sulfate exceptions,
and calcium hydroxide is the special case that is only *slightly* soluble. A
full-credit answer to "is lead(II) chloride soluble?" is "no — common chlorides
are soluble except those of silver and lead(II)".
:::

:::misconception
"Calcium hydroxide must be insoluble, because most hydroxides are." Calcium
hydroxide is the deliberate exception-within-the-exception: slightly soluble.
That slight solubility is not a footnote — it is why limewater exists, why it
can turn milky in the carbon dioxide test, and why the solubility rules flag it
separately instead of filing it with the truly insoluble hydroxides.
:::
