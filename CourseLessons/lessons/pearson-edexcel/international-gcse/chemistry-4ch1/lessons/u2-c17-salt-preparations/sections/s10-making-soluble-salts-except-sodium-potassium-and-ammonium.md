Making a [[soluble]] salt whose parent solid is insoluble always involves reacting a solid with an acid. You can use any of the following mixtures:

- acid + metal (but only for the moderately reactive metals from magnesium to
  iron in the reactivity series)
- acid + metal oxide or hydroxide
- acid + carbonate

Whatever mixture you choose, the method is basically the same: react the solid
with acid, remove the leftover solid, then crystallise the salt from the
solution. The classic worked preparation is copper(II) sulfate crystals from
copper(II) oxide — the specification's own practical (2.42).

**The practical, step by step.**

1. Measure 50 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and heat it on a
   tripod and gauze using a Bunsen burner.
2. Add a spatula-full of black copper(II) oxide and continue heating. If all
   the copper(II) oxide disappears, add more until some is left in the beaker,
   stirring well to make sure no more will react. You have now added the solid
   in [[excess]] — more than enough to react with all the acid present. When
   copper(II) oxide is still left over, you know all the acid has been
   neutralised.
3. Filter off the excess copper(II) oxide and transfer the filtrate — the blue
   solution, which is copper(II) sulfate — to an evaporating basin. The
   formation equation is:

   CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

4. Heat the solution to boil off some of the water and concentrate it. Keep
   heating until a saturated solution forms: dip a glass rod into the solution,
   and if crystals form on the rod when you remove it, the solution is close
   to saturation and crystals will soon form in it too.
5. Stop heating and allow the solution to cool slowly at room temperature so
   that larger crystals can form.
6. Remove the blue crystals by [[filtration]], or simply pour off the
   remaining solution.
7. Dry the crystals by blotting them with a paper towel, or leave them to dry
   in a warm place.

The same seven beats work for magnesium sulfate (acid + magnesium), for zinc
chloride (acid + zinc) and for any other soluble salt whose reactant leaves a
visible unreacted excess. Excess solid is the whole trick: the visible leftover
is the indicator that the acid is gone, and the filtration step then removes it
along with any insoluble impurities.

:::key-point
"'Anhydrous' means 'without water'." The word will matter in the next section —
evaporate a copper(II) sulfate solution all the way to dryness and you get the
anhydrous white powder, not the blue crystals the practical is asking for.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection throughout. Heating must be very gentle when concentrating
the solution — boiling salt solutions spit hot droplets of liquid and solid.
The water-bath rule from the solubility section applies here too: gentle,
indirect heat, never evaporate to dryness over a direct flame.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Describe an experiment to prepare a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt,
starting from an insoluble reactant" (2.39) is scored in beats: warm the acid;
add the insoluble reactant in excess (say *why* — to ensure all the acid
reacts); filter to remove the excess solid; heat the filtrate to concentrate;
test for saturation with a glass rod; cool slowly to crystallise; filter the
crystals; dry them on paper towel or in a warm place. Miss the word *excess*
or forget to say why, and the neutrality of your product is unproven — that is
where the marks sit.
:::

:::misconception
"Add exactly the right amount of copper(II) oxide, then no filtering is
needed." Exactly-right amounts cannot be judged by eye, and unreacted acid
ruins the preparation. Adding excess solid and filtering it off is not a
safety-net habit — it is the proof that every drop of acid reacted, which is
what makes the final crystals pure copper(II) sulfate rather than a mixture
with acid hiding among the water.
:::
