Chapter 7 (u1:c07) explained compounds built by electron **transfer** — metals
handing electrons to non-metals to make ionic lattices. Chapter 8 (u1:c08)
explained compounds built by electron **sharing** — non-metals holding each
other with covalent bonds. This chapter answers a different question entirely:
what holds a **metal** together? A metal is an element on its own. There is no
non-metal to hand electrons to and nothing to share with — yet copper keeps its
shape, steel frames carry whole buildings, and aluminium survives being beaten
into foil. The bonding inside a metal is the third and final bonding model of
the course, and it reuses ideas you already own: ion formation from Chapter 7
plus electrons that are free to move.

All three objectives of record for this lesson are **chemistry-only**
specification statements — assessed in **Paper 2 (2C) only**; they do not
appear on Paper 1 (1C) and they are **not** shared with Science Double Award:

- know how to represent a metallic lattice by a **2D diagram** (spec 1.52C);
- understand metallic bonding in terms of **electrostatic attractions**
  (spec 1.53C);
- explain typical **physical properties of metals**, including electrical
  conductivity and malleability (spec 1.54C).

The lesson runs in three movements after this map: the model itself — how
sodium atoms become a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised
electrons, and exactly how you draw it; what the model predicts about melting
points, by climbing the sodium-to-magnesium comparison; and the two properties
examiners love — conductivity and malleability — ending with the close that
ties every property back to the one structure sentence. Work through the
sections in order; each one's model feeds the next one's explanations.

:::prior-knowledge
Four pieces of earlier work carry this lesson. **Electronic configurations**
from Chapter 3 (u1:c03) — sodium is [2, 8, 1] and magnesium is [2, 8, 2], and
you should read those strings instantly, because the number of outer electrons
runs the whole chapter. **Groups** from Chapter 4 (u1:c04) — the group number
tells you the outer-electron count and the ion charge: Group 1 gives 1+ ions,
Group 2 gives 2+. **Ionic bonding** from Chapter 7 (u1:c07) — the words
"lattice" and "ion" are already yours, along with the rule that ionic
substances conduct only when molten or dissolved because then their ions are
free to move. **Covalent bonding** from Chapter 8 (u1:c08) — the contrast
model: metals share nothing; their outer electrons are released to roam.
:::

:::exam-hint
Everything in this lesson is chemistry-only: Paper 2C territory, never Paper
1C, and Double Award candidates can set the chapter aside. The questions are
short and extremely predictable — draw a labelled 2-D metallic lattice; define
metallic bonding in terms of electrostatic attraction; then a chain of
"explain why" parts: why magnesium's melting point beats sodium's, why the
metal conducts when solid, why it can be hammered into new shapes. Learn the
three explanations as three stories with fixed opening lines and the marks
follow mechanically.
:::

:::misconception
"A metal is just a pile of metal atoms touching each other." No — the atoms do
not survive intact inside a metal. Each atom's outer electron is released into
a shared pool, so what the lattice actually contains is **positive ions**, not
atoms, with the released electrons moving between them. Learners who picture
neutral atoms in a pile cannot explain conduction or ion charges. Learners who
picture ions plus a mobile electron sea can answer every question this chapter
can ask.
:::

:::quick-check
Question-bank join: spec 1.52C, 1.53C and 1.54C items — the chemistry-only
route badge, the three-objective map, and the contrast with the transfer and
sharing models of Chapters 7 and 8. If you can say in one sentence why neither
ionic bonding nor covalent bonding can hold a piece of copper together, you
are ready for the model itself.
:::
