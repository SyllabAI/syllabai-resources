A metal is **[[malleable]]**: hammer it and it flattens into a new shape
instead of shattering. The model explains this with a single motion. Applying
a force makes the **layers of positive ions slide over each other** — and
nothing about the bonding breaks when they do. After the slide, every ion is
still surrounded by the same sea of delocalised electrons attracting it exactly
as before, because every ion attracts **all** the electrons, wherever the ions
end up. The structure rearranges; the attraction never switches off.

Set that beside the ionic crystals of Chapter 7 (u1:c07) and the difference
becomes unforgettable. Slide a layer of an ionic crystal and like charges are
dragged face to face — attraction flips to repulsion, and the crystal shatters,
which is why ionic crystals are brittle. In a metal no such disaster is
possible: the electron sea surrounds every ion in its new position just as it
did in the old one, so the layers glide and the lattice re-seals itself. Same
lattice idea, same electrostatic attraction — but the mobile electrons make
one material bend and the other break.

Metals are also **[[ductile]]**: they can be drawn out into wires. The
explanation is the same sliding-layers story told lengthways. Pull the metal
and its layers slip past each other while the [[delocalised-electron]]s keep
every ion bonded to its surroundings, so the wire stretches and thins instead
of snapping. Copper's job inside a cable uses the pair of properties at once:
ductile enough to be drawn into fine wire, conductive enough to carry the
current through it.

This chapter completes the bonding picture of Unit 1. **Transfer** builds
ionic lattices; **sharing** builds covalent molecules and giant covalent
structures; **release** builds metallic lattices. Three ways of arranging
electrostatic attraction, three families of structure, three sets of
properties. Every structure-and-bonding question from here to the end of the
course is one of those three stories — or, in the best questions, a comparison
that asks you to tell them apart.

:::summary
- A metal is a **giant lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised
  electrons**: each atom releases its outer electron to a pool that moves
  freely through the whole structure.
- **Metallic bonding** is the electrostatic attraction between those positive
  ions and the delocalised electrons — the force that holds the lattice
  together.
- Ion charges follow the group — Group 1 metals form 1+ ions, Group 2 metals
  form 2+ — so releasing more outer electrons means a higher ion charge,
  stronger attraction and a **higher melting point**: magnesium above sodium.
- Metals **conduct when solid or molten** because the delocalised electrons
  are free to move; the ionic contrast is ions moving only when molten or
  dissolved.
- Metals are **malleable and ductile** because layers of ions slide over each
  other without breaking the bonding — the property ionic crystals lack.
:::

:::exam-hint
Malleability answers are two sentences, always: **"the layers of positive ions
slide over each other"** and **"the bonding is not affected — the ions are
still attracted to the delocalised electrons."** If the question asks why
metals differ from ionic crystals, add the third sentence: sliding an ionic
layer brings like charges together and the crystal shatters, while in a metal
the electron sea keeps every ion bonded wherever it moves. "Layers slide" on
its own earns the first mark and stops — the intact-bonding sentence is where
the second one lives.
:::

:::misconception
"Bending a metal breaks its bonds and re-forms them." No — the bonding never
breaks, and that is precisely why the metal survives. As the layers slide, the
delocalised electrons re-arrange around the moving ions instantly, so the
attraction between ions and electrons is continuous throughout. If the bonds
actually broke, the metal would crack like an ionic crystal. The phrase to
avoid in answers is "bonds break and re-make"; the phrase to use is "the
bonding is unaffected".
:::

:::cross-curricular
The properties you have just explained are the reason metals run the modern
world. Steel frames stand because strong metallic bonding makes steel hard.
Copper wins the wiring job because it conducts electricity and draws into
wire. Aluminium foil and car-body panels exist because the metal is malleable.
Materials engineers choose a metal exactly the way you answer the exam
question — property first, with the structure-and-bonding explanation running
underneath.
:::
