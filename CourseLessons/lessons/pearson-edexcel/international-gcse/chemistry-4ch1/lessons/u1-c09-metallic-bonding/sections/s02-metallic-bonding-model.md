Watch sodium build a metal, because one atom tells the whole story. A sodium
atom has the electronic configuration [2, 8, 1] — a single outer electron. When
sodium atoms bond together to form the solid metal, that outer electron
**leaves its atom** and becomes free to move throughout the whole structure.
The electron is said to be **[[delocalised-electron]]**: no longer attached to
any particular atom or pair of atoms — picture it flowing around through the
entire metal. The atom it left behind is no longer neutral: 11 protons now
pull on only 10 electrons, so a sodium ion, Na⁺, remains.

Multiply that event by the enormous number of atoms in even the smallest piece
of metal and you reach the structure sentence of the whole chapter: **a
metallic structure consists of a [[lattice]] — a regular arrangement — of
positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons**. The bonding holding it
together, **[[metallic-bonding]]**, is the **electrostatic forces of
attraction between each positive ion and the delocalised electrons**. Every
ion pulls on the electrons around it; the electrons pull back on every ion;
and that two-way attraction — the same electrostatic idea that held ionic
lattices and covalent molecules together — locks the whole metal into shape.

Spec 1.52C asks you to put this structure on paper in two dimensions. Draw a
grid of Na⁺ symbols in neat rows and columns, then scatter dots or small
circles in the gaps between them for the delocalised electrons, and label one
of each: "Na⁺ ion" at a grid point, "delocalised electron" in a gap. The
feature the mark scheme insists on is **regularity** — the ions must sit in a
regular repeating arrangement, not a random scatter. Different metals arrange
their ions in slightly different patterns, but nobody expects you to reproduce
a real crystal: a tidy grid is always accepted.

Two structural facts complete the model. First, a metal is a **giant
structure**: there are no individual molecules anywhere in it, and every
positive ion in the lattice attracts **all** the delocalised electrons — not
just its nearest neighbours. Second, the charge on each ion is fixed by the
atom's outer-shell count: every Group 1 metal forms 1+ ions and every Group 2
metal forms 2+ ions. Hold on to that charge rule — it becomes the engine of
the melting-point story in the next section.

:::key-point
Keep the conduction rules of the two structure types separate, because
questions live on the contrast. **Ionic compounds** conduct when **molten or
in aqueous solution**, because their ions are free to move; they do **not**
conduct when solid, because the ions are locked in place in the lattice.
**Metals** conduct when **solid or molten**, because the delocalised electrons
are free to move. The moving particle is different in each case — ions there,
electrons here — and mixing up the two is the single most expensive error in
this topic.
:::

:::exam-hint
Diagram marks are mechanical. Score them by checking four things: (1) the ions
are drawn in a **regular arrangement**; (2) the ion symbols carry their
**charges** — Na⁺, Mg²⁺; (3) electrons appear **between** the ions, not
inside them; (4) the electrons are labelled "delocalised electrons". For the
definition part of the question, both halves must appear: the force
(**electrostatic attraction**) **and** the two players it acts between
(**positive ions** and **delocalised electrons**). One half without the other
drops the mark.
:::

:::exam-hint
When you write symbol equations, a metal is always written as the plain atom
symbol — Na, Mg, Cu — never as an ion: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. The structure as a
whole carries no charge: thinking about the metal as a whole, the number of
delocalised electrons exactly balances the number of positive charges on the
ions. Learners who have just met metallic bonding sometimes start decorating
metal symbols with + signs; the ion charges belong inside the structure
diagram, not in the equation.
:::

:::misconception
"The sea of delocalised electrons makes the metal negative — or the ions make it positive."
Neither: the metal is **neutral**. Count the charges in sodium: every Na⁺
contributes exactly +1, and every atom's released electron contributes exactly
−1 to the pool. Each positive charge is balanced by a negative one, so the
piece of metal carries no charge overall. The charges only divide the labour —
ions hold the lattice points, electrons roam between them — they never sum to
anything but zero.
:::
