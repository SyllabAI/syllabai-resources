Most metals are hard, with high melting points — and the model explains both
facts in a single move. The electrostatic forces of attraction between the
positive ions and the delocalised electrons are **strong**. Pulling the
structure apart means fighting that attraction everywhere at once, so it takes
an enormous amount of energy, and the metal stays solid until it is heated
strongly. Whenever an exam asks you to justify hardness or a high melting
point, the answer is that attraction, and the energy needed to overcome it.

The comparison of sodium with magnesium turns the model into a prediction
machine. Both atoms release their outer electrons into the shared pool, but
they do not release the same number. Sodium atoms have one outer electron
each, so sodium's lattice is held together by 1+ ions and one delocalised
electron per atom — the bonding, as metals go, is quite weak, which is why
sodium is fairly soft with a low melting point for a metal. Magnesium atoms
have two outer electrons, both of which are delocalised, leaving ions that
carry a 2+ charge — and there is a much stronger electrostatic attraction
between the 2+ ions and the denser sea of delocalised electrons. Stronger
attraction means
more energy needed to break it, so magnesium's melting point sits well above
sodium's.

| Metal | Outer electrons released per atom | Ion charge | Attraction to the electron sea | Melting point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 1 | 1+ | weaker | lower — soft for a metal |
| Magnesium | 2 | 2+ | much stronger | higher |

Attach a piece of metal to a power source and the [[delocalised-electron]]s move
**through the metal**. Electrons are attracted towards the positive terminal
of the source and flow away along the wire; electrons flow in from the
negative terminal to replace them. A steady stream of electrons passes through
the lattice — and because the electrons were already free to move everywhere
in the structure, the metal conducts in the **solid** state, with no melting
required. Nothing in the lattice itself needs to move for a metal to carry a
current.

:::maths-skills
Read the sodium–magnesium comparison as a chain of linked quantities, not four
separate facts: **outer electrons released → ion charge → strength of
attraction → melting point**. Change one link (1 becomes 2) and every later
link steps up with it. Data questions hand you melting points or boiling
points for three consecutive metals and expect exactly this chain as the
explanation — quote the links in order, ending at the property the question
names.
:::

:::key-point
The one-line law of the whole section: **stronger attraction between the ions
and the delocalised electrons means a higher melting point**. Sodium's single
outer electron buys it a 1+ ion and weak bonding — soft, low melting point for
a metal. Magnesium's two buy a 2+ ion and much stronger bonding. When an exam
asks you to explain a melting-point difference between metals, that law plus
the charge arithmetic is the complete answer.
:::

:::exam-hint
Conduction answers have a fixed three-step shape: name the mobile particle
(**the delocalised electrons**), state that they are **free to move** through
the structure, and finish with **carry charge** through the metal. If the
question contrasts structure types, add the ionic side: ionic substances
conduct only when molten or dissolved, because then it is the **ions** that
move. "Ions move" written about a solid metal, and "electrons move" written
about a solid ionic compound, are the two answers that cost the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Metals conduct because their ions can move through the metal." In a **solid**
metal the ions cannot move at all — they are locked at their lattice points,
which is exactly why the metal keeps its shape. The only mobile charge
carriers are the delocalised electrons. In a **molten ionic compound** the
ions really do move and conduct — but that is the ionic story from Chapter 7
(u1:c07), and it must not be pasted onto metals.
:::
