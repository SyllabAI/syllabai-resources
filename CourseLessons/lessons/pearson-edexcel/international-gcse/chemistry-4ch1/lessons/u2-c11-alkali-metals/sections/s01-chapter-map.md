Drop a tiny piece of lithium into a bowl of water and it fizzes politely as
it scoots around the surface. Swap it for a same-sized piece of sodium and
the scooting turns frantic. Swap the sodium for potassium and the hydrogen
that bubbles off actually catches fire. Three metals, one family, one clear
message: these elements behave alike, and they behave *increasingly
violently* as you descend their group. That double observation — same
behaviour, graded intensity — is the entire subject of this chapter. The
elements of Group 1, lithium to caesium plus the ghost at the bottom, are
the **alkali metals**, and they are the first family of the Periodic Table
you will study as a family rather than as individuals.

Every objective of record for this lesson, so you can see the whole
territory before we start:

- understand how the **similarities in the reactions of these elements with
  water** provide evidence for their recognition as a **family of elements**
  (spec 2.1);
- understand how the **differences between the reactions with air and
  water** provide evidence for the **trend in reactivity** down Group 1
  (spec 2.2);
- **use knowledge of trends in Group 1** to **predict the properties of
  other alkali metals** — francium is the exam's favourite target (spec
  2.3);
- **explain the trend in reactivity in terms of electronic configurations**
  (spec 2.4C).

One routing note before you start, because this lesson is different from
the last three you have met. Specifications 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 are
**Paper 1 (1C) and Paper 2 (2C) statements**, shared with Science Double
Award. Specification 2.4C — the deep *explanation* of the reactivity trend —
is **chemistry-only**: Paper 2C only, and Double Award candidates do not
need it. So most of this chapter is core territory, with one marked
Paper-2-only summit near the top.

The lesson runs in five movements. First, the physical profile: a family of
low-density, low-melting, knife-cuttable metals, with a trend running down
the group. Second, storage and handling — why these metals live under oil,
and what the name *alkali* actually records. Third, the family evidence:
the shared electronic configuration and the shared reactions, from water to
air to the white compounds they form. Fourth, the explanation summit: why
the reactivity increases down the group, told in terms of distance and
attraction. Fifth, the payoff: predicting every property of an element
nobody has ever seen.

:::prior-knowledge
Four earlier chapters feed this one. **The Periodic Table** from Chapter 4
(u1:c04) — groups are the vertical columns, and elements in the same group
have the same number of outer-shell electrons. **Atomic structure** from
Chapter 3 (u1:c03) — electronic configurations such as 2,8,1, and the rule
that chemistry happens at the outer shell. **Ionic bonding** from Chapter 7
(u1:c07) — Group 1 atoms lose their single outer electron to form 1+ ions,
and the hydroxide ion carries a 1− charge. **Electrolysis** from Chapter 10
(u1:c10) — you wrote half-equations there; one of them returns at the end
of this chapter to explain *why* these metals react with water at all.
:::

:::exam-hint
The question shapes in this chapter are unusually fixed. You will be asked
to write word or symbol equations for the water reactions; to write
*observations* using the right vocabulary (fizzing, floats, melts into a
ball); to compare two members of the family using comparative phrases; to
state or explain the reactivity trend; and — the classic — to predict a
property of francium, or of a fictional element an examiner invents, by
continuing the group trend. The prediction skill is worth mastering
deliberately: it converts every fact in this chapter into an exam answer
about an element you have never studied.
:::

:::misconception
"A metal is a hard, shiny, heavy thing with a high melting point — so the
alkali metals must just be extra-reactive versions of iron or copper." The
physical profile collapses that stereotype completely: these metals are
**soft enough to cut with a knife**, several of them **float on water**, and
their melting points are so low that sodium melts into a ball from its own
reaction's heat. "Very low for metals" is not a footnote in this family —
it is a headline. Judge Group 1 by its own measured profile, not by the
transition-metal template in your head.
:::
