Why is Group 1 a *family* at all? Two reasons, and the second follows from
the first.

**Reason 1: they all have one electron in their outer shell.** The
**[[electronic-configuration]]** read like a
chant, each member adding one filled shell:

| Element | Symbol | Configuration |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium | Li | 2,1 |
| Sodium | Na | 2,8,1 |
| Potassium | K | 2,8,8,1 |

Rubidium adds one more shell (2,8,8,18,1) and caesium one more again — the
outer shell is always a lone pair-free single electron sitting on top of a
complete kernel. That shared outer-shell count is the structural fact
behind everything else in this chapter.

**Reason 2: they have similar chemical properties — and reason 2 is really
just a consequence of reason 1.** Chemistry happens at the outer shell, so
atoms with identical outer shells react identically. Set the family
reactions side by side and the pattern is total:

- they **all react with water** in the same way, forming a **[[hydroxide]]**
  with the **formula MOH** (LiOH, NaOH, KOH…) and **hydrogen** gas;
- they **react with oxygen** to form an **[[oxide]]** with the
  **formula M₂O** (Na₂O, K₂O…);
- they **react with [[halogen]]** to form salts with the
  **formula MX** (LiCl, KBr, CsI…);
- they **form [[ion]] containing an M⁺ ion** (Na⁺, K⁺…).

In every case the metal atom sheds its single outer electron onto
something else — water, oxygen, a halogen — and every case produces the
same 1+ ion. Even the charges are predictable: the **hydroxide ion carries
a 1− charge**, so M⁺ pairs with OH⁻ one-to-one to give MOH (that is the
ionic-charge bookkeeping from Chapter 7, u1:c07, at work). The family
equations are not approximations; they are the outer-shell fact written in
symbols.

This is the evidence structure the exam wants you to articulate for
spec 2.1: the **similarities in the reactions with water** — same products
(hydroxide + hydrogen), same ionic shape (M⁺ and OH⁻), same equations with
only the metal's letter changing — are what justify treating these six
elements as one family. A family is a claim about *shared behaviour*, and
shared behaviour traces back to the shared outer shell.

:::key-point
Two letters, one grammar. **M = any alkali metal; X = any halogen.** Any
equation or formula written with them is a *family statement*: MOH, M₂O,
MX, M⁺. If you learn the family equations with M and X, you have learned
every member's chemistry simultaneously — and you can write lithium's,
sodium's or potassium's equation on demand just by substituting the
letter.
:::

:::exam-hint
Examiner vocabulary for this section: "family of elements" and "evidence"
are the mark-bearing words. A full-credit answer to "how do the reactions
with water provide evidence that these elements form a family?" names the
**shared products** (metal hydroxide + hydrogen), the **shared formula
pattern** (MOH in every case) and the **graded similarity** (same
behaviour, differing only in speed). Vague answers like "they all react
the same" earn the first mark and stop; the formula MOH is the evidence
the mark scheme is fishing for.
:::

:::misconception
"These elements react the same way because they have similar masses." The
masses are nothing like similar — lithium (7) and caesium (133) differ by
nearly twenty-fold, yet their chemistry is the same pattern at different
speeds. The lever that produces the family resemblance is the **outer
shell**: every member carries exactly **one outer electron**, so every
member loses one electron in every reaction and lands on the same M⁺ ion.
Same outer shell — not similar mass — is the family's cause.
:::
