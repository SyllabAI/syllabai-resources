This is the summit of the chapter — and the specification marks it
explicitly: **explain** the trend in reactivity in Group 1 **in terms of
electronic configurations** (spec 2.4C). Understand it as a chain, because
the exam awards the links.

**The fact to explain:** as you go down the group, the metals become more
reactive.

**Step 1 — what the reaction actually is.** In all these reactions the
metal atoms are **losing their outer electron** and forming metal ions.
For sodium going into water:

**Na(s) → Na⁺(aq) + e⁻**

Everything else — the hydroxide forming, the hydrogen bubbling off —
follows from that one loss. So the reactivity question is really a
question about **how easily the outer electron is lost**.

**Step 2 — what makes the electron easy or hard to lose.** That depends on
**how strongly the outer electron is attracted to the nucleus**. The
nucleus is **positive** (it contains the protons), so it attracts the
negative electrons — and the closer an electron sits to that positive
nucleus, the harder it is to pull away.

**Step 3 — follow the atoms down the group.** As you move down Group 1,
the atoms have **more shells of electrons** and therefore **get bigger**: a
sodium atom is bigger than a lithium atom, a potassium atom bigger than a
sodium atom. In the bigger atom, **the outer electron is further from the
nucleus**.

**Step 4 — the conclusion, stated cleanly.** Because the outer electron is
further from the positive nucleus, **the attraction on it is weaker** — so
it is **lost more easily** — so the reaction happens **faster**. Bigger
atom down the group → weaker pull on the outer electron → more reactive
metal. That chain, in that order, *is* the 2.4C answer.

Notice what the explanation is *not*. It is not about mass, and it is not
about eagerness. Potassium is not more reactive than lithium because it is
heavier, and atoms do not *want* anything; reactivity is a geometry-and-charge
story — distance between the outer electron and the nucleus, and the
strength of the electrostatic attraction that distance allows. The
electronic configurations from earlier in this chapter are the map: each
extra shell pushes the outer electron one layer further out.

:::misconception
"Potassium is more reactive than lithium because it is heavier" — the
mass-guess. Mass is not in the chain anywhere; **atom radius is**. A
heavier nucleus with the charge buried under more shells holds its outer
electron *more weakly*, not more strongly. The correct lever is distance:
more shells → bigger atom → outer electron further from the nucleus →
weaker attraction → electron lost more easily → faster reaction. If your
explanation does not mention distance or attraction, it is not the 2.4C
explanation.
:::

:::exam-hint
This whole section is **Paper 2C only** — specification 2.4C is
chemistry-only, so Paper 1 can ask you to *state* the trend but never to
*explain* it this way. The full-credit explanation has four checkpoints:
more shells down the group; atom bigger; outer electron further from the
nucleus; attraction weaker so the electron is lost more easily. Write the
chain as a sentence chain — "the outer electron is further from the
nucleus and so less strongly attracted, so it is lost more easily" — and
the marks follow. (An extension footnote for completeness: reacting with
air can also form **peroxides (M₂O₂)** and **superoxides (MO₂)** — still
M⁺ inside, different oxygen species — and lithium uniquely forms a
**nitride, Li₃N**. Beyond the spec; useful general knowledge, never
compulsory marks.)
:::
