Here is the reaction that defines the family. **All the alkali metals react
in the same way with water, producing a metal [[hydroxide]] and
hydrogen gas.** Written as the family equation, with M standing for any
member of the group:

**2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)**

Read what the products are telling you. The **MOH(aq)** is the metal
hydroxide dissolved in the water — and a solution of any metal hydroxide
is strongly **[[alkaline]]**, which is why the family carries the
name *alkali metals*. The **H₂(g)** is hydrogen, and it is the visible
star of the show: hydrogen gas bubbling off is what makes the reaction
look alive. The hydroxide is the quiet product — colourless, dissolved —
but it is the one that turns the water into an alkali, and it is the one
the equations must never omit.

The reaction is also **exothermic** — a lot of heat is produced — and that
fact does quiet explanatory work in every observation you are about to
meet in the next section. Melting a ball of sodium into shape and igniting
potassium's hydrogen both run on the reaction's own released heat.

So if the reactions are all the same *kind*, what makes the family
interesting? **The main difference between the reactions is how quickly
they happen.** As you go down the group — lithium, then sodium, then
potassium — the metals become **more reactive** and the reactions occur
**more rapidly**. Lithium fizzes briskly but needs a few patient seconds;
sodium skates around its dish in an urgent hurry; potassium ignites its
own hydrogen on the spot. Same equation, same products, steadily
escalating speed. That escalation is the **trend in reactivity**, and
evidencing it with these water reactions is exactly what specification
2.2 asks you to understand: the *differences* between the reactions —
speed, violence, heat output — provide the evidence for the trend.

Keep the logic of the trend-evidence sharp, because it is a favourite
examination framing. A trend is not demonstrated by one reaction; it is
demonstrated by a *comparison across the family*. Sodium versus lithium:
sodium's reaction is faster. Potassium versus sodium: potassium's is
faster again. The comparison *is* the evidence — the reactions with water
are the standard measuring stick the family gets judged against.

:::key-point
One equation, four facts. **2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)** tells
you: the products (hydroxide + hydrogen), the solution's character
(strongly alkaline — the source of the family's name), the state symbols
(aqueous hydroxide, gaseous hydrogen), and the observation you should
predict (fizzing — hydrogen coming off). Down the group the *only* thing
that changes is rate: more reactive, more rapid, more violent.
:::

:::exam-hint
Equation marks are strict here. The product with water is the **hydroxide
(MOH)** — writing the oxide (M₂O) loses the product mark, because that is
the *air* reaction. State symbols earn their own marks: **2M(s) +
2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)** — (aq) on the hydroxide because it dissolves,
(g) on the hydrogen because it bubbles off. If asked for the solution's
character, the word is **alkaline** (or "strongly alkaline") — "soapy" and
"basic" without pH context earn less.
:::

:::misconception
"The hydrogen produced comes from the metal." It cannot — the metal
contains **no hydrogen at all**. Every hydrogen atom in the bubbles comes
**from the water**: the metal's outer electrons reduce water molecules,
releasing H₂ and leaving **OH⁻** behind (which pairs with M⁺ to make the
hydroxide). The metal contributes the electron and the M⁺; the water
contributes the hydrogen. That is why *every* alkali metal gives hydrogen
with water, no matter what the metal is made of.
:::
