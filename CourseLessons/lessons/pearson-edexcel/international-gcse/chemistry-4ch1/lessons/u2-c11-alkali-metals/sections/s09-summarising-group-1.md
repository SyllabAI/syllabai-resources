Every fact of this chapter now fits on one card. Here is the family
portrait, assembled from the sections above — this list is the exam's
checklist for "state the main features of the Group 1 elements":

- they **are metals**;
- they are **soft**, with **melting points and densities that are very low
  for metals**;
- they have **to be stored out of contact with air or water** (under oil
  for lithium, sodium and potassium; sealed glass tubes for rubidium and
  caesium);
- they **react rapidly with air** to form coatings of the **metal oxide**;
- they **react with water** to produce an **alkaline solution of the metal
  hydroxide** and **hydrogen gas**;
- they **increase in reactivity as you go down the group**;
- they form compounds in which the **metal is a 1+ ion**;
- their compounds are **mainly white or colourless**, dissolving to give
  **colourless solutions**.

Check each line against where you met it. Softness, low melting points and
low densities came from the physical profile; storage from the handling
regime; the oxide coatings from the air reaction; the alkaline hydroxide +
hydrogen from the water reaction; the trend from both — tarnishing rate
and fizzing rate agreeing; the M⁺ ion and the white/colourless compounds
from the ionic chemistry. Nothing on the card is new; the card is the
chapter compressed.

Two lines of that card deserve a second look because exams like to probe
them by contrast. "Very low for metals" is a *comparative* claim — these
metals are soft enough to cut with a knife and light enough to float,
which no everyday metal is, and that is precisely what makes them
remarkable. And "increase in reactivity down the group" is the line that
converts the whole list into a prediction engine: if every property slides
smoothly down the group, then a property you cannot measure can be *read
off* the slide.

Which brings the chapter to its natural question. The summary card
describes five elements you can put on a bench — but the family has a
sixth member, at the very bottom of Group 1, of which **nobody has ever
seen a single piece**. You cannot observe its softness, melt it, cut it
or drop it into water. Does the prediction engine still run? The next —
final — section runs it, and the run is the payoff of everything above.

:::key-point
The one-line family logic, worth memorising as a sentence: **one outer
electron → same reactions (MOH with water, M₂O with oxygen, MX with
halogens) → graded only by speed down the group → so the whole family
portrait is a sliding scale.** Every bullet on the summary card is either
a shared property (from the outer electron) or a slide position (from the
growing atom size).
:::

:::exam-hint
"State the main features of the Group 1 elements" is a list-recall
question — the eight-line card above *is* the mark scheme, and answers
score one mark per correct feature, so write more lines than the marks
available. Two phrasings earn their keep: "**low for metals**" (the
comparative claim — melting points, densities, hardness) rather than
naked numbers; and "**reactivity increases down the group**" stated as a
*direction*, not as a value. If the question adds "explain why they must
be stored under oil", that is the s02 answer — air (oxygen and water
vapour) reacts with the metal, oil keeps both away.
:::

:::misconception
"A summary card means every alkali metal behaves *identically* in every
respect." Read the card again with the trend glasses on: some lines are
**shared properties** (soft, M⁺ ion, white compounds — every member, same
pattern), while others are **slides** (melting point *decreases* down the
group, reactivity *increases*, density *rises irregularly*). The family
agrees on the pattern and disagrees on the speed. An answer that treats
the slide-lines as constants — "they all melt at low temperatures, the
same temperature" — has misread the card.
:::
