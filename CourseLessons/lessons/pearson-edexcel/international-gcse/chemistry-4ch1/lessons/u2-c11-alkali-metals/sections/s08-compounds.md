The metals themselves are dramatic; their compounds are almost invisible —
and that contrast is itself a family property worth knowing. **All Group 1
metal [[ion]] are colourless.** That single fact settles the colour of
essentially every alkali-metal compound you will ever meet: **their
compounds are colourless or white — unless they are combined with a
coloured negative ion.**

The logic runs one way only, and it is worth getting exactly right. The
metal's own ion contributes **no colour**, ever. So:

- sodium chloride, potassium bromide, lithium sulfate — white or colourless
  solids, colourless in solution. The positive ion is colourless and the
  negative ion (Cl⁻, Br⁻, SO₄²⁻) is colourless too.
- **potassium dichromate(VI) is orange** — not because of potassium, which
  contributes nothing, but because the **dichromate(VI) ion, Cr₂O₇²⁻, is
  orange**;
- **potassium manganate(VII) is purple** — again, not the potassium, but
  the **manganate(VII) ion, MnO₄⁻**.

The diagnostic habit this builds is genuinely useful across the course:
when a Group 1 compound shows *any* colour, the colour belongs to the
**negative ion**, never to the metal. Potassium is a passenger in both of
the famous coloured compounds above; the chromium and the manganese are
doing all the showing.

Beyond colour, the compounds behave as a family too: **Group 1 compounds
are typical ionic solids and are mostly soluble in water.** The strong
ionic bonding (Chapter 7, u1:c07 — positive metal ion to negative ion) is
there in the solid, but put the solid in water and the lattice gives way:
the ions separate and the solid dissolves. That solubility is what made
the water reactions of earlier sections produce *solutions* — the MOH
product does not sit as a solid, it disperses as aqueous ions, which is
why the hydroxide solutions are strongly alkaline throughout.

So the compound portrait completes the family: soft reactive metals that
build white, mostly-soluble, colourless-ion compounds. If an exam question
hands you a white solid that dissolves to give a colourless solution and
asks what the metal ion could be, Group 1 is always on the candidate list —
and if the solid is orange or purple with potassium inside, you now know
exactly which ion is wearing the colour.

:::key-point
Colour assignment rule: **Group 1 ions are colourless; the negative ion
colours the compound.** Potassium dichromate(VI) is orange because of
**dichromate(VI)**; potassium manganate(VII) is purple because of
**manganate(VII)** — the potassium contributes nothing. And the physical
default: Group 1 compounds are **typical ionic solids, mostly soluble in
water**.
:::

:::exam-hint
Colour-prediction questions are free marks once the assignment rule is
firm: **the colour of a Group 1 compound belongs to its negative ion**.
"Explain why potassium dichromate(VI) is orange" wants: potassium ions are
**colourless**; the **dichromate(VI) ion is orange**. If asked for two
properties of Group 1 compounds, the safe pair is "**typical ionic
solids**" and "**mostly soluble in water**" (giving colourless solutions
when all ions are colourless).
:::

:::misconception
"Potassium must be an orange element, because potassium compounds come in
orange." That reverses the colour assignment twice over. First, potassium
the **element** is a silvery metal (with a lilac flame in its water
reaction — a *flame colour*, not a compound colour). Second, in potassium
dichromate(VI) the potassium ion is **colourless**; the orange belongs
entirely to the **dichromate(VI) ion**. Swap the potassium for
colourless-ion sodium and the compound stays orange. The negative ion
wears the colour, always.
:::
