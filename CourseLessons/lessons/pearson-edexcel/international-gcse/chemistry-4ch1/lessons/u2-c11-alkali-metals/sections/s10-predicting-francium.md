Meet the family's ghost. **Francium**, at the bottom of Group 1, is
**extremely [[radioactive]]**: one of its [[isotope]]
is produced during the radioactive decay of uranium-235, and it is
extremely short-lived. At any one moment scientists estimate that only
about **20–30 g of francium exist in the whole of the Earth's crust**, and
**nobody has ever seen a piece of it**. There is no jar in any lab on
Earth labelled "francium".

And yet, by the end of this section you will be able to describe it in
detail — because **as you move down a group in the Periodic Table, the
properties of the elements change gradually**, so if you know most of a
group, you can predict the rest. This is specification 2.3 in its purest
form: **use knowledge of trends in Group 1 to predict the properties of
other alkali metals**. The summary card from the last section is the
machine; francium is the demonstration. Take the trend *through* caesium
and the predictions fall out:

- it **is very soft**;
- its **melting point is around room temperature** — careful prediction
  work puts it between **21 °C and 27 °C**, continuing the slide
  (181 → 98 → 63 → 39 → 29 → …);
- its **[[density]] is probably just over 2 g/cm³**, continuing the
  density slide (0.53 → 0.97 → 0.86 → 1.53 → 1.88 → …);
- it **will be a silvery metal — but will tarnish almost instantly** in
  air, because tarnishing rate slides *up* the group;
- it **will react violently with water** to give **francium hydroxide and
  hydrogen** — more violently than any member above it;
- it **will be more reactive than caesium**, the current record-holder;
- its **hydroxide, FrOH, will be a soluble, strongly alkaline
  hydroxide** — the MOH pattern does not break at the bottom of the group.

Study the *method*, because the method is the transferable skill. Each
prediction is a single move: **find the property's slide, place the
unknown at the bottom of it, read off the next value.** The examiner can
run the same trick with a fictional element — call it *edexcelium*, placed
below potassium — and the machinery works identically: its reaction with
water gives an **alkaline** solution (hydroxide + hydrogen, family
pattern); its compound with air has the formula of an **oxide**; its
chloride has the formula **MCl**; its ion carries **1+**. You were never
taught edexcelium. You were taught the slide, and the slide answers for
it.

That is the deepest lesson of Group 1, and the reason this family opens
Unit 2: **a trend is a prediction engine.** Learn the family portrait and
its direction of travel, and you own every member of the group — seen and
unseen alike.

:::summary
One structural fact (a single outer electron), three family equations
(MOH with water, M₂O with oxygen, MX with halogens), two independent
trend measurements (water rate and tarnishing rate, agreeing), one
four-link explanation of the trend (more shells → bigger atom → outer
electron further out → weaker attraction → lost more easily), and one
prediction method (slide + next position) that turns all of it into
answers about elements you have never met.
:::

:::exam-hint
Prediction questions mark the **method**, so show it. For francium — or
the examiner's fictional element — the full-credit pattern is: name the
**trend** ("melting points decrease down the group"), then apply it
("so francium's melting point is below caesium's, near room
temperature"), then land the **family pattern** ("it reacts with water to
give an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen"). Two-sentence answers that skip
the trend statement score half. And remember the formula patterns
transfer: a Group 1 chloride is always **MCl**, an oxide **M₂O** — the
fictional element's salts are already decided by its group.
:::

:::misconception
"Francium's properties cannot be predicted, because nobody has ever seen
it." That confuses *observing* a property with *knowing* it. The
Periodic Table is a prediction machine precisely because properties
**change gradually down a group** — so the unobserved bottom member sits
at the end of five measured slides and inherits their direction. Every
francium prediction in this section is a disciplined extension of
measurement, not a guess; that is exactly what specification 2.3 means by
"using knowledge of trends".
:::

:::cross-curricular
Francium's story is a physics story too. It exists only as the product of
**radioactive decay** — uranium-235 decays through a chain that passes
through francium — and its most stable isotopes last seconds to minutes
before decaying further. The 20–30 g estimate for the entire crust is a
radioactivity calculation, not a mining survey: it comes from working out
how much francium is being continuously created and how fast it vanishes.
Chemistry fixed francium's place in Group 1; nuclear physics explains why
you will never hold it.
:::

:::can-you
Close the chapter against all four objectives. Can you — (1) **explain why
the water reactions make Group 1 a family**: same products, same MOH
pattern, same ionic shape, differing only in rate? (2) **use the air and
water reactions together as trend evidence**: fizzing rate and tarnishing
rate rank the family identically? (3) **predict properties down the
group**: melting point, density, softness, tarnish speed, water violence,
hydroxide formula — for francium or any fictional Group 1 metal? (4)
**explain the trend in terms of electronic configurations**: more shells,
bigger atom, outer electron further from the nucleus, weaker attraction,
lost more easily (Paper 2C)? If all four are yes, francium holds no
secrets — and neither does any element the examiner invents below it.
:::
