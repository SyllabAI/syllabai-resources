The sodium reaction is the family's standard demonstration, and you need to
be able to write it and narrate it. The equation:

**2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)**

One subtlety in that equation is worth a whole mark of care. Strictly
speaking, most of the time the sodium is reacting it is present as
**[[molten]] sodium**, not solid sodium — the reaction's own heat
has melted it into a ball. It is still correct, and expected, to write
**(s)** in the equation: writing **(l)** for sodium has the potential to
confuse an examiner and is best avoided. Use the state symbol for the
metal as it *started*, not as the reaction temporarily reshaped it.

Now the observations — the list the exam keeps asking for, each with its
physical cause:

- **The sodium floats**, because it is **less dense than water**.
- **It melts into a small ball**, because its melting point is low and the
  reaction produces **a lot of heat** — enough to take the metal past its
  melting point almost instantly.
- **There is fizzing**, because **hydrogen gas** is produced.
- **The sodium moves around on the water's surface**, because the hydrogen
  is not given off symmetrically — the escaping gas pushes the ball in
  random directions like a jet.
- A **white trail** forms behind it: that is the **sodium hydroxide**,
  which dissolves in the water to form a **strongly [[alkaline]]**
  solution (add universal indicator and the water turns purple).

**Lithium** runs the same reaction with the same equation shape —
**2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g)** — but **slower**. Lithium's
melting point is higher and the heat is produced more slowly, so the metal
**does not melt**; it simply fizzes and slides around as a solid piece,
taking noticeably longer to disappear.

**Potassium** runs faster than sodium — **2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) +
H₂(g)** — and there is one headline difference: **enough heat is produced
to ignite the hydrogen, which burns with a lilac flame**. The reaction
often ends with the potassium spitting around and exploding. With sodium
the hydrogen usually does *not* light; with potassium it nearly always
does. That flame is the single most examinable sodium-versus-potassium
contrast.

At the very top of the family the reaction leaves the classroom:
**rubidium and caesium react so violently that the reactions are too
hazardous to attempt in school — they would explode.**

:::exam-hint
Observation vocabulary is marked precisely. Write **"fizzing"**,
**"bubbling"** or **"effervescing"** — not "a gas is given off", because
fizzing is what you actually *see* (the gas is an inference). When asked
to **compare** lithium and sodium, use visible-rate phrases: *"fizzes more
slowly"*, *"moves around more slowly"*, *"takes longer to disappear"*.
When comparing **potassium with sodium**: *"fizzes more vigorously"*,
*"moves around more quickly"*, *"disappears more quickly"* — and the key
difference, *"the hydrogen bursts into flames (lilac), which sodium's does
not"*. Comparative sentences, not vibes: each phrase is a mark.
:::

:::safety
The reactions of rubidium and caesium with water would be **too hazardous
to attempt in school** — they would explode. Demonstration work stays with
lithium, sodium and potassium, in very small pieces, behind a screen, with
eye protection: these reactions are energetic and produce strongly
alkaline, corrosive hydroxide solutions.
:::

:::misconception
"The alkali metals react with water because water is hot or acidic." No —
pure, cold, neutral water reacts with every member of the family. The
driver is not the water being hostile; it is the metal's outer electron
being *loseable*. Watch what the electron actually does: the electrons
released by the metal are gained by the **water molecules**, producing
**hydroxide ions** and **hydrogen gas**:

**2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻(aq) + H₂(g)**

You met equations shaped exactly like this in the electrolysis chapter
(u1:c10) — gain of electrons at the cathode. The metal supplies the
electrons; the water receives them. That is the whole reaction, one
electron-transfer at a time.
:::
