Ask a simple question: what does a metal that reacts vigorously with *both*
of the two most common substances on Earth — air and water — need in order
to survive? The answer is isolation. **All the alkali metals are extremely
reactive, and they get more reactive as you go down the group.** They react
quickly with the oxygen in air to form **[[oxide]]**, and they react
rapidly with water to form **strongly [[alkaline]] solutions** of
the metal **[[hydroxide]]**. That second reaction is where the
family's name comes from: an *alkali* is a soluble base, and these metals
literally make alkalis when they meet water. The name **alkali metals** is
a behaviour report, not an accident.

The isolation is real and physical. **Lithium, sodium and potassium are
stored under oil** — you will see them in a school lab as dull lumps
suspended in a jar of colourless liquid. Oil keeps out both the oxygen and
the water vapour of the air, so the metal inside stays as unreacted as the
day it was cut. **Rubidium and caesium are so reactive that oil is not
enough**: they are stored in **sealed glass tubes** to remove any
possibility of oxygen reaching them. The storage regime *is* the reactivity
trend made visible — the harsher the containment, the more violent the
metal.

Handling rules follow the same logic, and they are strict. Great care must
be taken **never to touch these metals with bare fingers**. There could be
enough sweat on your skin — sweat is mostly water, with dissolved salts —
to start a reaction on your hand, producing a lot of heat and a very
corrosive metal hydroxide. The metals are cut, when needed, under oil or
with dry tools, and only lithium, sodium and potassium are ever handled at
all; rubidium and caesium are far too reactive for school demonstration.

One notation convention runs through this whole chapter and the exam
questions built on it, so set it now: **'M' stands for any one of the
alkali metals** (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs), and **'X' stands for any one of the
halogens** (Group 7). Equations written with M are *family equations* —
they are true for every member of the group at once. You will meet them
constantly: MOH for the hydroxides, M₂O for the oxides, MX for the salts
formed with halogens. When an examiner writes "an element M in Group 1…",
they are handing you the whole family and asking you to use its pattern.

:::key-point
The storage hierarchy is an exam answer hiding in a lab observation. Ask
"why are alkali metals stored under oil?" and the marking logic wants: they
react with **oxygen** (and water vapour) in the air; oil keeps air away
from the metal; without it the metal would soon be coated in oxide. For the
top of the group the answer extends: rubidium and caesium are reactive
enough to need **sealed glass tubes**. And the family name itself is a
markable fact: they are called *alkali* metals because their reaction with
water produces **alkaline metal hydroxide solutions**.
:::

:::did-you-know
The family was recognised as a family **before anyone knew electrons
existed**. Chemists grouped these elements together purely on the
similarities in their properties — the same softness, the same low
densities, the same water reactions. When electronic configuration was
finally worked out, the reason for the similarity appeared: all of them
carry exactly one electron in their outer shell. The pattern was visible
decades before its cause was.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two classic one-liners live in this section. "Explain why the metals are
stored under oil" wants: react with **oxygen/water vapour in the air**; oil
**keeps air away**; otherwise an oxide coating forms. "Explain why Group 1
is called the *alkali* metals" wants: reaction with water produces a
**metal hydroxide** which dissolves giving a **strongly alkaline
solution**. And if a question shows rubidium or caesium in sealed tubes,
say the containment is harsher **because reactivity increases down the
group** — the storage hierarchy is trend evidence in disguise.
:::

:::misconception
"The oil is there to lubricate the metal, or to keep it cool." Neither.
The oil is a **chemical barrier**: its only job is to keep **oxygen and
water vapour** away from a metal that reacts with both within moments of
exposure. Leave a lump of sodium in the open air and a dull oxide crust
forms in minutes; under oil the metal stays bright and unreacted for
years. A related error — "the oil reacts with the metal slowly" — is
wrong too: the oil is chemically inert to the metal, which is exactly why
it works.
:::
