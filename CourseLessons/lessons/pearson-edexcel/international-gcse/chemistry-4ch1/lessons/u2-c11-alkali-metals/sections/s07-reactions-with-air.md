Water is the family's loud reaction; air is its quiet one — and it is the
quiet one you can watch happening on a bench in seconds. **Lithium, sodium
and potassium are all stored in oil because they react with the air**; take
a piece of sodium out of its oil and you can catch the reaction mid-run.

A piece of sodium fresh from the oil usually has a **crust on the outside**
— the residue of its last conversation with the air. It is **not shiny**
unless it has been **freshly cut**. So cut it, and watch:

- the **fresh surface is shiny** — that is what unreacted sodium actually
  looks like, silvery like any metal;
- within seconds the surface **tarnishes rapidly** as the freshly exposed
  sodium reacts with **oxygen in the air**, growing a dull coating of
  **metal [[oxide]]**;
- do the same with **lithium** and the fresh surface **tarnishes more
  slowly**, because lithium reacts more slowly than sodium;
- do the same with **potassium** and the fresh surface **tarnishes
  extremely rapidly** — faster than sodium's.

Read the comparison carefully, because it is a second, independent
measurement of the reactivity trend. **Potassium is more reactive than
sodium, which is more reactive than lithium** — the same ordering the water
reactions gave, now delivered by a *different* reaction on a *different*
timescale. That convergence is what makes the trend evidence rather than
anecdote: specification 2.2 asks you to see the trend in the *differences
between* the reactions with **both** air and water, and this section is the
air half of that evidence.

In each case the chemistry is the same simple story: **metal + oxygen in
the air → metal oxide coating**. No flame is needed for the tarnishing
reaction — oxygen attacks the fresh surface on contact. The oxide layer
is why lab samples look dull and crusted: a piece of "sodium" under oil is
really a shiny metal wearing an oxide overcoat it grew the last time it
met the air.

The tarnishing test is also the fastest qualitative demonstration of the
trend you can perform with no water, no indicator and no flame — just a
knife, three small samples and a stopwatch. Fresh cut, fresh surface, and
the family orders itself in front of you: lithium sluggish, sodium swift,
potassium nearly instant.

:::key-point
Air evidence, compressed: **freshly cut = shiny; a few seconds later =
tarnished** (the oxide coating). Rate of tarnishing ranks the family —
**Li slowest, Na rapid, K fastest** — and it ranks them in *exactly* the
same order as the water reactions. Two different reactions, one trend:
that is the evidence structure the exam wants for the Group 1 reactivity
ordering.
:::

:::exam-hint
The air-reaction marks attach to the *comparison*. "Describe how the
tarnishing of freshly cut lithium, sodium and potassium provides evidence
for the trend" wants: **all freshly cut surfaces are shiny at first**; all
**tarnish** (oxide forms with air/oxygen); and the **rate order** —
lithium slowest, sodium rapid, potassium fastest — showing **reactivity
increases down the group**. Watch your vocabulary: the coating is a
**metal oxide** formed with **oxygen**, and "tarnish" or "goes dull" is
the observable; "rusts" is iron vocabulary and scores nothing here.
:::

:::misconception
"The dull crust on stored sodium means the metal is dirty — wipe it off
and it would be shiny again." Wiping will not help, because the dullness
is **chemically changed metal**: the surface sodium has **reacted with
oxygen** and become a layer of **oxide** — a different substance bonded
onto the metal beneath. Only **cutting** reveals shiny sodium, because
cutting exposes **fresh, unreacted metal** — which then begins tarnishing
again immediately. Dirt sits *on* a surface; this crust *is* a converted
surface.
:::
