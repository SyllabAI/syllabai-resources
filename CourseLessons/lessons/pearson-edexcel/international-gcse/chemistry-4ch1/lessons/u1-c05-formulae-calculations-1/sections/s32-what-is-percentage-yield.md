If you calculate how much product a reaction *might* produce, in real
life you rarely obtain as much as you expected. Expect 100 g, collect
80 g — your percentage yield is **80%**. The rest is lost somehow, and
the book gives the standard loss mechanisms: **spillages**; losses when
transferring a liquid from one container to another; and — a big one —
**side reactions**, which divert some of your starting materials into
unwanted products. That, the book notes, "happens a lot during reactions
in organic chemistry" (its Figure 5.19 shows student experiments making
and purifying organic compounds, which rarely give high yields).

Percentage yield is also where the previous problem's arithmetic gets its
second life. Revisiting the lead calculation with the *ratios* route:
trace the lead through both equations — 2 mol PbS produces 2 mol PbO,
then (doubling the second equation) 2 mol PbO produces 2 mol Pb — so
every 2 mol of PbS produces 2 mol of Pb. Substitute masses: 2 × 239 g PbS
produces 2 × 207 g Pb, that is **478 g PbS produces 414 g Pb**. Tonnes
asked? "It's much easier to think a bit, and realise that the ratio is
always going to be the same, whatever the units": 478 tonnes PbS produces
414 tonnes Pb. Proportion: 1 tonne gives 414/478 = **0.866 tonne** — the
same answer as section 31, with no gram-conversion at all.

:::key-point
A proportion's ratio is unit-independent: 478 g : 414 g and
478 tonnes : 414 tonnes are the same 414/478. This is why the ratio
method can work directly in tonnes — the units cancel inside the
proportion.
:::

:::exam-hint
The "ratio is the same whatever the units" insight is a genuine exam
shortcut for any yield or reacting-mass question set in tonnes (or kg).
Use it *deliberately*: state the proportion in the question's own units,
then divide. It replaces the convert-in-convert-out arithmetic of the
three-step method — at the cost of the generality that method's HINT in
section 31 warned about.
:::

:::misconception
"A low percentage yield means I did the calculation wrong." No — the
calculated amount is the *theoretical* yield (what the equation promises);
the low percentage yield measures the *experiment's* losses against it.
Spills, transfer losses and side reactions are real chemistry, not
arithmetic errors. The next section defines the two yields formally and
does the division.
:::
