The equation below is for a reaction that occurs in the extraction of
iron:

Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO

**Calculate the mass of iron which can be formed from 1000 g of iron
oxide.**

**Step 1.** Mᵣ(Fe₂O₃) = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = **160** (the book's KEY
POINT builds it exactly so). So:

number of moles = 1000 / 160 = **6.25 mol Fe₂O₃**

**Step 2 — now the ratio matters.** From the equation, **1 mol Fe₂O₃
produces 2 mol Fe** — "we know this because of the big numbers in front
of the formulae". Starting with a certain number of moles of Fe₂O₃, you
obtain **twice as many** moles of Fe:

6.25 mol Fe₂O₃ → 2 × 6.25 = **12.5 mol Fe**

**Step 3.** Mass of 12.5 mol Fe = 12.5 × 56 = **700 g**.

**The alternative route (ratios):** 1 mol Fe₂O₃ produces 2 mol Fe, so
160 g (1 mol) of Fe₂O₃ produces 2 × 56 = 112 g (2 mol) of Fe; 1 g of
Fe₂O₃ produces 112/160 g; therefore 1000 g produces
1000 × 112/160 = **700 g**.

:::exam-hint
The book's HINTs arm you for industrial-scale variants: **you should be
told how many grams there are in a tonne** if the question needs it
(the conversion, Figure 5.18: 1 tonne = 1 000 000 g), and note that
1 000 000 might be written as 10⁶ or 1 × 10⁶ — "these are both the
same". The next section uses exactly this conversion.
:::

:::misconception
"1 mol Fe₂O₃ produces 1 mol Fe — the iron comes from the iron oxide, one
formula unit each." Read the equation: **2Fe**. Each Fe₂O₃ unit carries
*two* iron atoms, so each mole of it yields **two** moles of iron. The
balancing numbers encode the atom bookkeeping — 1 : 2 here — and
ignoring the 2 halves your answer (350 g instead of 700 g).
:::
