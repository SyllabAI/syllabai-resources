The book now fixes the methane equation. With the proportions adjusted —
and *only* the proportions — the balanced equation for the combustion of
methane is:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Count each symbol as one atom of that element and check both sides:
**1 carbon** each side; **4 hydrogens** each side (4 on the left inside
CH₄, 2 × 2 on the right inside 2H₂O); **4 oxygens** each side (2 × 2 on
the left, 2 + 2 on the right). Every atom type balances — this is what
the book calls *balancing the equation*.

Balancing is a skill with a method, and the book gives you its systematic
approach directly. Follow it exactly as printed:

- **Work across the equation from left to right**, checking one element
  after another. If an element seems impossible to fix that way, leave it
  until the end — you will often find it has sorted itself out.
- **Count unchanged groups as a whole.** If a group of atoms such as the
  sulfate group (SO₄) appears unchanged on both sides, count the group
  once instead of individual sulfurs and oxygens. It saves time.
- **Check everything at the end**, to be sure you have not disturbed
  something you already counted.

:::exam-hint
The book's three-step method is worth adopting verbatim: left-to-right,
one element at a time; unchanged atom-groups counted whole; full recount
at the end. The "leave the awkward element until last" tip is the one
that rescues equations involving O₂ — you will see exactly why in
Worked example 2 (the ethane combustion).
:::

:::misconception
"I balanced it left to right, so it must still be balanced when I finish."
Not necessarily — fixing a later element can *unbalance* an earlier one
(for instance, changing 2H₂O on the right alters both the H and the O
counts). That is precisely why the book's method ends with **check
everything at the end**: the last pass is not optional tidying, it is
where the marks are secured.
:::
