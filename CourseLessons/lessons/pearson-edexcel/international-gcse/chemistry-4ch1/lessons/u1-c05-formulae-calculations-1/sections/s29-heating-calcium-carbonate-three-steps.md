When calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, is heated, calcium oxide is formed. The
task: **calculate the mass of calcium oxide produced by heating 25 g of
calcium carbonate** (Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40):

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

**Step 1 — moles from the given mass.** Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) =
100, so:

number of moles = mass ÷ mass of 1 mole = 25 / 100 = **0.25 mol**

**Step 2 — the equation gives the target moles.** From the equation,
**1 mol of CaCO₃ decomposes to produce 1 mol of CaO** — so 0.25 mol
CaCO₃ produces **0.25 mol CaO**.

**Step 3 — moles back to mass.** Mᵣ(CaO) = 40 + 16 = 56:

mass = number of moles × mass of 1 mole = 0.25 × 56 = **14 g**

The reaction will produce **14 g of calcium oxide**.

**The alternative route (ratios).** The same answer without the explicit
moles: 1 mol CaCO₃ produces 1 mol CaO, so
100 g produces 56 g; therefore 1 g produces 56/100 = 0.56 g; therefore
25 g produces 25 × 0.56 = **14 g**. "You do not have to learn both
methods, just learn whichever method you are happier with."

Two more hints complete the section. On precision: the
impression of a full-mark answer is "a lot of words with a few numbers
between them" — and even if your mental arithmetic is fast enough to
skip steps, **you must still show all your calculations**. On a
half-factor trap (which returns in the iron problem): to find the mass
of Fe you multiply moles of Fe by 56 — a common mistake is to multiply
by the mass of **2Fe** (112 g), but the '2' has *already been used* when
the moles of Fe were worked out from the equation.

:::key-point
The Aᵣ of Fe is 56; the Mᵣ of Fe₂O₃ = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = 160. These
are the two numbers the next problem runs on — assembled exactly as
here, from the Periodic Table values.
:::

:::exam-hint
The ratio method shines when the equation's coefficients are awkward or
the arithmetic is round-numbered (100 → 56 here). The three-step method
scales to *every* problem in the rest of the chapter, including the
two-equation lead problem where the ratio method gets messier. Learn one
primary method, keep the other as a checking tool — and remember to
show the working either way.
:::

:::misconception
"CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ shows 3 substances, so 1 mol CaCO₃ gives 3 mol of
products." The big (or unwritten '1') numbers give **1 mol CaO and 1 mol
CO₂** separately — the mole row is read per substance, not summed into a
single "3". Step 2 needs the specific pair (CaCO₃ → CaO), which the
balancing numbers state directly.
:::
