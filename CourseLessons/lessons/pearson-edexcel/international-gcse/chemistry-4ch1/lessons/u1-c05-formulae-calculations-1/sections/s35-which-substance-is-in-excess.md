When you make something in the lab, you rarely mix things in exactly the
right proportions. Usually **something is in excess** — there is more
than enough of it to react with the other substance — and you remove the
excess afterwards (by filtering, for example). A **[[reagent]]** is just
something that reacts; the substance left over is the
**[[excess-reagent]]**.

The trap the book sets up: until now, every problem gave you enough
information to calculate moles of **one** thing. What if you are given
moles of **both**?

> Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid:
> Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
> **0.2 mol Mg is reacted with 0.2 mol HCl. Calculate the mass of
> hydrogen gas produced.**

The tempting wrong answer: 0.2 mol Mg produces 0.2 mol H₂, so the mass of
H₂ = 0.2 × 2 = **0.4 g**. "Unfortunately this is not the correct answer!"
Check against the equation: 1 mol Mg reacts with **2 mol HCl**, so 0.2 mol
Mg would need **0.4 mol HCl** — but only 0.2 mol was used. The magnesium
cannot all react, so its moles cannot be used to work out the hydrogen.

Start from the HCl instead: 2 mol HCl reacts with 1 mol Mg, so 0.2 mol
HCl reacts with 0.1 mol Mg — and there *is* enough Mg (0.2 mol) to react
with all of it. **The Mg is in excess**, which means **all the HCl
reacts**, and HCl controls the product:

2 mol HCl produce 1 mol H₂ → 0.2 mol HCl produce **0.1 mol H₂**

mass of H₂ = 0.1 × 2 = **0.2 g** — the correct answer.

:::key-point
You **always have to use the chemical equation** to determine which
substance is in excess — "more of it" is not the test. The book's own
variant: 0.2 mol Mg with 0.3 mol HCl *looks* like HCl is in excess
(there is more of it), but 0.3 mol HCl only reacts with 0.15 mol Mg —
still not enough HCl to use up all the Mg, so the Mg is **still** in
excess.
:::

:::exam-hint
The book closes the method in one line: **to deduce which substance is
in excess, try both numbers of moles in the chemical equation and
compare**. Run each reagent's moles through the ratio and see which one
runs out first — the one that runs out (HCl here) is the limiting
reagent, and *it* — never the excess one — feeds the product
calculation.
:::

:::misconception
"Whichever reagent I have more moles of must be the one in excess."
Excess is a *ratio* property, not a size property: 0.3 mol of HCl against
0.2 mol of Mg still leaves HCl short, because the equation demands
2 mol HCl per 1 mol Mg. Always convert both amounts through the
equation's ratio before naming the excess.
:::
