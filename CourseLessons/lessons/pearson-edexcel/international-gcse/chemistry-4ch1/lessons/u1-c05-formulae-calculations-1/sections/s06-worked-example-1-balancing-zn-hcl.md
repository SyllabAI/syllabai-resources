Balance the equation for the reaction between zinc and hydrochloric acid:

Zn + HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

The book works the systematic method from the previous section, left to
right. **Zinc**: 1 atom on each side — no problem. **Hydrogen**: 1 atom on
the left, 2 on the right; if there are 2 at the end, there must have been
2 at the start, and the only way to achieve that is **2HCl**. (You must
*not* change the formula to H₂Cl, because that substance does not exist.)
**Chlorine**: with 2HCl there are now 2 on each side — good. Finally
check everything again, and the equation is finished:

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

The book's atom-count table for the finished equation:

|                | Zn | H | Cl | Zn | H | Cl |
|----------------|----|---|----|----|---|----|
| numbers of atoms | 1 | 2 | 2  | 1 | 2 | 2  |

:::key-point
Halves *are* acceptable in balanced equations, and you can always remove
one later by doubling everything — but you will rarely meet them at
International GCSE. Worked example 2 shows the halving trick in its
natural habitat: an odd number of oxygens.
:::

:::exam-hint
The book's second HINT in this section settles two state-symbol worries
before they start: don't worry if you don't yet know what the state
symbols should be — that is not important at this stage. But do register
the correction it adds: **water is a liquid (l), not an aqueous solution
(aq)** — an aqueous solution is formed when something is *dissolved in*
water.
:::

:::misconception
"If I need 2 hydrogens on the left, I can write H₂Cl." You cannot —
**H₂Cl is not a substance**. Hydrochloric acid is HCl, period. Balancing
adjusts *how many* formula units take part (the big number 2HCl); it
never rewrites *what* the substance is. This is the never-change-a-formula
law from the last section, caught red-handed in exam conditions.
:::
