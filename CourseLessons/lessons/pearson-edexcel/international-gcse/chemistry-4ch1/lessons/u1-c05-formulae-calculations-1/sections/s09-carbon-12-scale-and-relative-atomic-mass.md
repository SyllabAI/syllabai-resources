A practical question sets up the whole second half of the chapter: make iron(II) sulfide by heating iron with sulfur —

Fe + S → FeS

— and ask: **in what proportions should you mix them?** Certainly not
equal masses, because iron and sulfur atoms do not weigh the same. An
iron atom contains more protons and neutrons than a sulfur atom, so it is
about **one and three-quarter times heavier**. Getting the proportions
right therefore requires knowing the masses of the individual atoms
taking part — which is exactly what the **[[relative-atomic-mass]]**
delivers.

**The carbon-12 scale.** A single atom weighs about
1.67 × 10⁻²⁴ g — an absurd number to calculate with. Instead, chemists
compare all atomic masses against a standard: the mass of an atom of the
**carbon-12 isotope** ([[carbon-12-scale]]). On this scale one ¹²C atom
weighs **exactly 12 units**, and the basic unit of the scale is
**one-twelfth** of that — approximately the mass of the most common
hydrogen atom. So fluorine-19 has a relative mass of **19** (19 times
one-twelfth of a ¹²C atom), and magnesium's most common isotope has a
relative mass of **24**.

**From isotopes to Aᵣ.** Real samples contain a *mixture* of isotopes, so
we need the average mass of an atom taking them all into account — the
relative atomic mass. The definition, verbatim:

> The relative atomic mass of an element is the **weighted average mass**
> of the isotopes of the element. It is measured on **the carbon-12
> scale**, on which a carbon-12 (¹²C) atom has a mass of **exactly 12**.

Because these are *relative* masses, they have **no units**. Chlorine's
Aᵣ is 35.45 (quoted 35.5), lithium's is 6.94 (quoted 7), sodium's is
22.99 (quoted 23) — averages over their isotope mixtures. The
International GCSE convention: use the Periodic Table values, with
decimals where given (Cl 35.5, Cu 63.5).

:::key-point
To work out a weighted average you have to know **how much of each isotope
is present** in the sample — the weighted-average method from Chapter 3
(page 26) is the same mathematics again here.
:::

:::exam-hint
One vocabulary line the examiner enforces: avoid
**'relative molecular mass'** for ionic substances — it can only properly
describe actual molecules (covalent substances). You should not use it
for magnesium oxide or sodium chloride. **Relative formula mass covers
everything**, which is why the next section installs it as the default
term.
:::

:::misconception
"Aᵣ = 35.5 means a chlorine atom weighs 35.5 units." No single chlorine
atom does — 35.5 is the **weighted average** across the ³⁵Cl/³⁷Cl
mixture. Any one atom weighs 35 or 37; the *sample average* is 35.5.
This is the Chapter 3 isotope story (weighted averages need how much of
each isotope is present) reappearing as the foundation for Mᵣ.
:::
