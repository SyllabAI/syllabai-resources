Spec 1.36 is a practical: **know how to determine the formula of a metal
oxide by combustion or by reduction**. Both experiments are here, each
ending in the same empirical-formula table.

## By combustion — magnesium oxide

Burn magnesium in oxygen and watch how the mass changes. Procedure:
weigh a crucible **with its lid**; add a ~10 cm piece of magnesium ribbon
and weigh again; heat strongly (a roaring flame), **lifting the lid every
few seconds**; cool and weigh when finished. The magnesium burns with a
bright white flame to give magnesium oxide, a white powder. The lid
stops the powder escaping — but it must be lifted to **let oxygen in**.

A set of results: crucible 32.46 g; + magnesium 32.70 g; + contents at
the end 32.86 g.

- mass of magnesium = 32.70 − 32.46 = **0.24 g**
- the mass **increases** because the magnesium combines with oxygen from
  the air: mass of oxygen gained = 32.86 − 32.70 = **0.16 g**
- the reaction type was **[[combustion]]**

The empirical-formula table (Aᵣ: Mg = 24, O = 16):

|                 | Mg        | O         |
|-----------------|-----------|-----------|
| masses/g        | 0.24      | 0.16      |
| moles of atoms  | 0.24/24   | 0.16/16   |
| number of moles | 0.010     | 0.010     |
| ÷ smaller       | 1         | 1         |
| empirical formula | **MgO** |           |

:::safety
Wear eye protection and take care not to get burned when raising the
crucible lid with tongs. The crucible will stay very hot for some time.
:::

## By reduction — copper oxide

Alternatively, **remove the oxygen** and watch the mass fall. Pass
hydrogen gas over copper oxide in a ceramic dish inside a tube; ignite
the excess hydrogen at the small hole; heat strongly until pink-brown
copper is seen. Results: dish 23.78 g; + copper oxide 26.96 g; + contents
at end 26.32 g.

- mass of copper oxide = 26.96 − 23.78 = **3.18 g**
- mass of copper at the end = 26.32 − 23.78 = **2.54 g**
- mass of oxygen removed = 3.18 − 2.54 = **0.64 g**

The reaction, CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, is a **[[displacement-reaction]]**
(hydrogen is more reactive than copper and displaces it) — and can also
be called a **[[redox-reaction]]**: the copper oxide has been
**reduced** — oxygen removed, the [[reduction]] of the copper(II) oxide. Chapter 14 develops both.

The table (Aᵣ: Cu = 63.5, O = 16): 2.54/63.5 = 0.0400 mol Cu;
0.64/16 = 0.040 mol O; ratio 1 : 1 → empirical formula **CuO**.

:::safety
The teacher demonstrating needs to wear a face shield and use safety
screens; pupils require eye protection and should be no closer than
2 metres. If a drying agent is needed, use anhydrous calcium chloride —
NOT concentrated sulfuric acid.
:::

:::key-point
Before lighting the hydrogen stream, **fill the whole tube with
hydrogen first**: a hydrogen–oxygen mixture is explosive. Let the gas
flow through for a while to flush out all the oxygen, then light the
stream (or the Bunsen burner).
:::

:::key-point
How do you know the reaction has finished? Nothing more happens in the
crucible — and you check by **heating to constant mass**: weigh, heat,
weigh again, repeating until the mass stays constant. (Precision note:
the original masses here are 2 significant figures, so quote the moles
as 0.010 — the extra figure shows the precision honestly.)
:::

:::exam-hint
In practice the ratio "is not very often" exactly 1:1 — the experimental
culprits: some magnesium oxide powder may escape when
the lid is lifted; not all the magnesium may react; and magnesium can
also react with **nitrogen** in the air. Expect a question to hand you a
slightly-off ratio and ask which of these explains it — that question is
asking for this list.
:::

:::misconception
"The mass went down in the reduction experiment, so matter was
destroyed." The oxygen **left the dish as water vapour** — hydrogen
carried it away. Every gram is conserved: 0.64 g of oxygen moved from
copper oxide into the water stream. "Mass decrease" in this experiment
means *oxygen removed*, i.e. reduction — not loss of matter.
:::
