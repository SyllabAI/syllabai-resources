Once you have an Mᵣ, one division tells you what fraction of a compound's
mass any part of it contributes — the **[[percentage-composition]]**.
First worked case, magnesium carbonate:

**To find the Mᵣ of MgCO₃** (Aᵣ values: C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24):

Mᵣ = 24 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 24 + 12 + 48 = **84**

You add the masses of 1 × Mg, 1 × C and 3 × O — the subscripts do the
counting. The second case adds brackets to the arithmetic, calcium
hydroxide:

**To find the Mᵣ of Ca(OH)₂** (Aᵣ values: Ca = 40, O = 16, H = 1):

Mᵣ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = **74**

The bracketed (OH) group is counted **as a unit** (16 + 1 = 17) and then
doubled — exactly the bracket rule from section 3, now earning its keep
in a calculation. With the Mᵣ in hand, the percentage by mass of any
element follows: that element's total contribution divided by the whole,
× 100. The next two sections work that division through two complete
examples.

:::exam-hint
Two habits are worth copying in the exam. First, **write the Aᵣ values
down before starting** — list them ("Relative atomic masses: C = 12,
O = 16, Mg = 24") even though they are given, because it makes the
working checkable. Second,
multiply first, add second: 3 × 16 = 48 as its own step stops the
"C + O₃" addition errors.
:::

:::misconception
"Mᵣ of Ca(OH)₂ is 40 + 16 + 1 + 2 = 59." The 2 after the bracket doubles
**everything inside it** — both the O and the H. Correct working is
40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74. Forgetting to multiply the hydrogen is
the bracket-rule failure wearing a calculation costume.
:::
