Modify the reduction apparatus slightly and the same experiment
determines **the formula of water**: this time
the **water vapour** produced by the reaction between copper(II) oxide
and hydrogen is **condensed** in a U-tube and collected. The dish is
weighed before and after as before, **and** the mass of water collected
is measured.

The results table adds one row to the reduction experiment's:

|                                        | g      |
|----------------------------------------|--------|
| mass of empty dish                     | 23.78  |
| mass of dish + copper oxide            | 26.96  |
| mass of dish + contents at end         | 26.32  |
| mass of water collected                | **0.72** |

The reasoning, step by step: the mass of oxygen lost from the
copper(II) oxide is **0.64 g** (26.96 − 26.32). **All** that oxygen
combines with hydrogen to form water — so the water contains 0.64 g of
oxygen. Since 0.72 g of water was collected, the mass of hydrogen in it
must be 0.72 − 0.64 = **0.08 g**. Now the familiar table:

|                        | H         | O         |
|------------------------|-----------|-----------|
| masses/g               | 0.08      | 0.64      |
| number of moles of atoms | 0.08/1  | 0.64/16   |
| number of moles        | 0.08      | 0.040     |
| ÷ smaller              | 2         | 1         |
| empirical formula      | **H₂O**   |           |

Two hydrogen atoms to every oxygen atom — the experiment lands on the
formula you have written since Chapter 1, but now you have *derived* it
from masses.

:::exam-hint
The whole experiment hinges on one conservation sentence worth quoting
in answers: **all the oxygen lost from the copper(II) oxide ends up in
the water**. That is why oxygen's share of the water is *read off the
dish* (0.64 g) and only hydrogen's must be *inferred* (0.72 − 0.64).
Questions love asking which mass comes from which measurement.
:::

:::misconception
"0.72 g of water means 0.72/18 = 0.04 mol of water, so we're done."
That shortcut uses the answer's formula — circular. The experiment's
point is to *derive* H₂O without assuming it: weigh the oxygen that
left the oxide (0.64 g), weigh the water formed (0.72 g), infer the
hydrogen (0.08 g), and let the mole table say 2 : 1. Only after the
table agrees with H₂O does the 18 g/mol check make sense as a
confirmation.
:::
