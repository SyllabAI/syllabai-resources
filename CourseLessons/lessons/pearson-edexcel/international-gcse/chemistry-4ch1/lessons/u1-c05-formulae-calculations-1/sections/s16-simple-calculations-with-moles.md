You need to be able to **interconvert between a mass in grams and an
amount in moles** for a given substance. There is one formula to learn:

$$\text{number of moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{mass of 1 mole (g)}}$$

You can rearrange it to find whatever you want. If rearranging causes
problems, use a **formula triangle**: mass on top, number of moles and
mass of 1 mole on the bottom — cover the quantity you want and the
triangle shows the operation (top ÷ bottom, or bottom × bottom, or top ÷
bottom). Look at it carefully and make sure you can deduce all three
equations from it.

Two worked cases, both with the Mᵣ found first:

**Finding the mass of 0.2 mol of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃**
(Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40):

- Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = **100**, so 1 mol has a mass of 100 g
- 0.2 mol has a mass of 0.2 × 100 = **20 g**

**Finding the number of moles in 54 g of water, H₂O**
(Aᵣ: H = 1, O = 16):

- Mᵣ(H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16 = **18**, so 1 mol has a mass of 18 g
- 54 g of water is 54 / 18 = **3 mol**

:::key-point
Number of moles = mass in grams ÷ mass of 1 mole in grams. Every
remaining calculation in this chapter — reacting masses, yields, excess —
is this line plus a chemical equation.
:::

:::exam-hint
Always find (and *show*) the Mᵣ first. The two
workings above each carry three visible stages — Mᵣ, then the mole-mass
link, then the answer. That structure earns method marks even when an
arithmetic slip lands late, and it makes your answer checkable by the
"how many times does it go in?" instinct from the last section.
:::

:::misconception
"moles = mass ÷ Mᵣ, so to get the mass I also divide by Mᵣ." Rearrange,
don't repeat: **mass = moles × mass of 1 mole**. The two directions are
the triangle's two different reads — 0.2 mol × 100 g/mol = 20 g versus
54 g ÷ 18 g/mol = 3 mol. Writing the division both ways is the single
most common mole-arithmetic slip.
:::
