Formulae come *from* mole ratios. Start with sulfur dioxide, SO₂: there are 2 O atoms for each S atom. Scale it up — if you had 1 mol
of SO₂ you would still have twice as many O atoms as S atoms: **1 mol of
S atoms and 2 mol of O atoms**. And back down: if an experiment found a
sample of sulfur dioxide containing 0.1 mol of S atoms, it must also
contain **0.2 mol of O atoms** (0.2 mol contains twice as many atoms as
0.1 mol — the same point holds with 0.02 vs 0.01 mol).

Run the logic forward and you can *read formulae out of mole data*:

- A compound contains **0.2 mol Ca and 0.2 mol O** → equal numbers of
  atoms → the formula is **CaO**.
- A compound contains **0.4 mol Mn and 0.8 mol O** → twice as many O
  atoms as Mn atoms → the formula is **MnO₂**.

Formulae found this way have names, and both are defined:

> The **[[empirical-formula]]** shows the **simplest whole number ratio**
> of the atoms present in a compound.
>
> The **[[molecular-formula]]** shows the **actual number of atoms of each
> element** present in a molecule (covalent compound) or formula unit
> (ionic compound) of a compound.

The two can coincide or differ: calcium oxide is CaO *both* ways; hydrogen
peroxide has empirical formula **HO** but molecular formula **H₂O₂**. To
get from one to the other you need one extra datum — the Mᵣ of the
compound — which is exactly the machinery the next four sections build.

:::key-point
Mole ratios *are* atom ratios. The empirical formula is that ratio in
simplest whole numbers; the molecular formula is the actual atom count,
and it is always the empirical formula (or a whole-number multiple of it).
:::

:::exam-hint
A significant-figures confession: more significant figures *should*
have been used for the numbers of moles in these examples, but were
omitted for simplicity. In the exam, carry more
figures through the intermediate mole values (the practicals later give
0.0400 vs 0.040 for exactly this reason) and only round at the ratio.
:::

:::misconception
"The empirical formula and molecular formula are always different." They
are often the same — CaO is both. They differ only when the molecular
formula is a *multiple* of the simplest ratio (HO vs H₂O₂). Treating
"empirical" as automatically "not the real formula" is wrong; treating it
as automatically "the real formula" is wrong too — you need the Mᵣ to
decide (section 23).
:::
