Chemical reactions **rearrange atoms into new combinations** — they never
create or destroy them. It follows that a finished equation must end up
with exactly the same number of each type of atom that it started with;
that is what makes it a **[[balanced-equation]]**. The book builds the
idea with methane burning in oxygen:

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Count the atoms on each side and the equation *can't* be right: the right
side has gained an oxygen atom and lost two hydrogens. The substances are
all correct — methane really does burn to carbon dioxide and water — so,
as the book puts it, **the proportions must be wrong**. Fixing
proportions, without touching the substances, is the whole skill.

The book's HINT on page 40 is the single most important sentence in this
chapter, and it deserves to be quoted in full: you must **never, never
change a formula** when balancing an equation. All you are allowed to do
is write **big numbers in front of the formulae**. The next section
applies the fix to the methane equation, and Worked example 1 shows the
systematic method in action.

:::exam-hint
This HINT is really important (the book's own emphasis): **never change a
formula when balancing**. If the equation needs another oxygen, you write
a bigger *big number* — you never turn O₂ into O₃ or H₂O into H₂O₂.
Changing a formula changes the substances, which changes the chemistry;
changing a big number changes only the proportions, which is what
balancing means.
:::

:::misconception
"CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O is nearly balanced, so it is nearly right." There is
no such thing as *nearly* balanced — an equation either conserves every
atom type or it is wrong. Here the left side has 4 H and 2 O while the
right has 2 H and 3 O. The chemistry (which substances) is fine; the
proportions (how much of each) are not, and only the proportions may be
edited.
:::
