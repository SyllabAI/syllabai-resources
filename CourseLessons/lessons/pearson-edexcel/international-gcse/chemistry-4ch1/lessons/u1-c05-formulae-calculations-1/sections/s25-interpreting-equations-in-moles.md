Take the methane equation you balanced in section 5:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Read at molecular level, it says 1 molecule of CH₄ combines with 2
molecules of O₂ to form 1 molecule of CO₂ and 2 molecules of H₂O. But
molecules are inconveniently small for calculation — and the mole fixes
that, because the same ratios hold for moles:

**1 mol CH₄ reacts with 2 mol O₂ to form 1 mol CO₂ and 2 mol H₂O**

Nothing about the equation's meaning has changed — the ratios are still
the same. The equation's big numbers do double
duty: they balance the atoms *and* they announce the proportions in
moles.

The arithmetic, pinned: the big numbers in front of the formulae tell
us **the number of moles** of each substance that
react. And although there does not appear to be a big number in front of
the CH₄ and CO₂, there is — a '1' that we simply do not write.

:::key-point
A chemical equation can always be read as a mole recipe: the balancing
numbers are the moles. An invisible '1' counts as a big number
(CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ means 1 mol : 1 mol : 1 mol).
:::

:::exam-hint
This reading is the hinge between the first half of this chapter
(equations) and the second half (reacting masses): every mass calculation
from here on starts by *interpreting the equation in terms of moles*.
When a question says "using the equation", that instruction means: write
the mole row underneath the equation before touching any masses.
:::

:::misconception
"The equation means 1 *gram* of CH₄ reacts with 2 *grams* of O₂." No —
the balancing numbers are mole ratios, never gram ratios. In fact
1 mol CH₄ (16 g) reacts with 2 mol O₂ (64 g): the mass ratio is 16 : 64,
not 1 : 2. Grams and moles are connected through Mᵣ, which is exactly
why the next sections interpose a moles step between the equation and
the masses.
:::
