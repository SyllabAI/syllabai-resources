**State symbols** are often — but not always — written after each formula
in an equation to show the **physical state** everything is in. The four
you need to know:

| Symbol | State |
|--------|-------|
| (s) | solid |
| (l) | liquid |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | in aqueous solution (dissolved in water) |

A worked illustration:

2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Read aloud, this says: solid potassium reacts with liquid water to make a
solution of potassium hydroxide in water, and hydrogen gas. Notice how
much extra information the state symbols carry — the same balanced
equation *without* them would not tell you that the potassium hydroxide
ends up dissolved, or that the hydrogen leaves as a gas.

:::exam-hint
The two symbols candidates mix up are **(l)** and **(aq)**. Water in a
beaker is H₂O(**l**); salt *dissolved in* that water is NaCl(**aq**).
The previous section said it plainly: water is a liquid, not an aqueous
solution — (aq) is reserved for something dissolved in water. State symbols are part of the 1.25 objective
("including state symbols"), so a perfectly balanced equation can still
drop marks without them.
:::

:::misconception
"(aq) means 'in water', so pure water is H₂O(aq)." No — (aq) means
*dissolved in water*, which requires something doing the dissolving.
Pure water is the solvent itself: H₂O(**l**). Only species dissolved in
water — ions like KOH(aq) above — earn the (aq) label.
:::
