The C₂H₅ from the last section cannot be a real compound's formula — as
you will recognise once you meet organic chemistry in Unit 4. The
**[[molecular-formula]]** (the actual number of each atom present in a
molecule) has to be a **multiple** of the empirical formula, like C₄H₁₀.

The conversion needs exactly one extra piece of information: **the
relative formula mass of the compound** — or the mass of 1 mole, which is
just the Mᵣ in grams. The book continues the previous example: suppose
you are told the relative formula mass is **58**.

- Mᵣ of C₂H₅ = (2 × 12) + (5 × 1) = **29** (Aᵣ: H = 1, C = 12)
- How many times does 29 go into 58? **58 / 29 = 2**
- So the molecule contains **2 lots of C₂H₅**: the molecular formula is
  **C₄H₁₀**

That is the whole method: divide the given Mᵣ by the empirical-formula
Mᵣ, multiply the empirical formula by the answer. The ratio of elements
is untouched (both C and H scale by the same factor), which is why the
molecular formula always "cancels down" to the empirical formula.

:::key-point
Molecular formula = empirical formula × n, where
**n = Mᵣ(compound) ÷ Mᵣ(empirical formula)**. If n comes out as a
whole number, the data is consistent; if it does not, something upstream
(the percentage table or the Mᵣ) needs re-checking.
:::

:::exam-hint
Show the division as its own line (58 / 29 = 2) and *then* the scaled
formula — method marks live in the n step, not just the final C₄H₁₀.
And note what the book flags: knowing "a bit more organic chemistry"
(Unit 4) is how you'd *spot* that C₂H₅ is impossible — but you do not
need that knowledge in the exam; the Mᵣ does the deciding for you.
:::

:::misconception
"The molecular formula is C₂H₅ doubled in mass, so 2 × 29 = 58 —
therefore the molecular formula is 58." The Mᵣ is a number; the formula
is atoms. The factor n multiplies the **formula** (C₂H₅ → C₄H₁₀), not
the mass figure. Writing "the molecular formula is 2" or "is 58" confuses
the scaling factor with the formula itself.
:::
