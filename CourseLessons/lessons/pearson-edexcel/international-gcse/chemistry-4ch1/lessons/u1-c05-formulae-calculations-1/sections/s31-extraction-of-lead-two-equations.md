Lead is extracted from galena, PbS. The ore is roasted in air to produce
lead(II) oxide, PbO:

2PbS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2PbO(s) + 2SO₂(g)

The lead(II) oxide is then converted to lead by heating it with carbon in
a blast furnace:

PbO(s) + C(s) → Pb(l) + CO(g)

The molten lead is tapped from the bottom of the furnace.
**Calculate the mass of lead that would be produced from 1 tonne of
galena.**

**Step 1 — mind the units.** Moles need grams, so convert: 1 tonne =
1 000 000 g (multiply by 1 000 000 — the number has to increase). Then,
with Mᵣ(PbS) = 207 + 32 = 239:

number of moles of PbS = 1 000 000 / 239 = **4184 mol**

**Step 2 — trace the lead through BOTH equations.** From the first:
2 mol PbS produce 2 mol PbO, so 4184 mol PbS → **4184 mol PbO**. From the
second: 1 mol PbO produces 1 mol Pb, so 4184 mol PbO → **4184 mol Pb**.

**Step 3 — back to mass, then to tonnes.** Mass of Pb = 4184 × 207 =
866 000 g. Divide by 1 000 000: **0.866 tonne** of lead.

The doubling trick explains a second route: double the second equation (2PbO + 2C → 2Pb + 2CO) so you can trace what
happens to **all** the 2PbO from the first — and then notes the
simplification: this could be reduced to *1 mol PbS produces 1 mol Pb*,
saving arithmetic, though it makes no difference to the answer. Its HINT
weighs the two methods: the three-step route uses the same steps in every
calculation but needs the tonne conversions; the ratio route has fewer
steps but will not extend to the solution calculations of the next
chapter.

:::cross-curricular
This flow sheet — roast the ore, reduce the oxide, tap the metal from
the furnace's base — is the extractive-metallurgy pattern that powered
the industrial revolution, and the blast furnace is still the reason
iron and lead (Chapter 14 revisits both) have been cheap enough to build
a metal-based civilisation on. The chemistry is old; the mole
arithmetic you just did is what makes the yields predictable.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two-equation problems are bookkeeping, not new chemistry: follow the
substance (here PbS → PbO → Pb) through each equation's own mole ratio,
in order. Write the mole row for each equation separately before
combining — and keep the tonne conversion at the very edges of the
problem (once in, once out), never in the middle of the mole arithmetic.
:::

:::misconception
"The two equations both start with 2, so 2 mol PbS gives 1 mol Pb."
Trace it: 2 mol PbS → 2 mol PbO (first equation), then 2 mol PbO → 2 mol
Pb (second). The lead count never halves — 2 mol PbS yields **2 mol Pb**.
The doubling of the second equation exists precisely to make this chain
visible; simplifying to 1 : 1 is a convenience, not a change.
:::
