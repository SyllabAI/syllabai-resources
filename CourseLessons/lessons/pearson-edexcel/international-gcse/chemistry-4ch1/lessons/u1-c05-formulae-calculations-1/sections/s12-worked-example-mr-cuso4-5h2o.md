This formula looks far more complicated than the previous ones — and the
book's advice is to break it into its two natural pieces. First, why the
water is there at all: when some substances crystallise from solution,
water becomes **chemically bound up with the salt**. This is
[[water-of-crystallisation]], and salts containing it are **hydrated**
([[hydrated-salt]]). There are **always 5 water molecules per 1 CuSO₄
unit** — they are part of the formula, not impurities. (The book's other
examples: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O and MgCl₂·6H₂O.)

**To find the Mᵣ of CuSO₄·5H₂O** (Aᵣ values: H = 1, O = 16, S = 32,
Cu = 63.5).

The book's method: work out the relative formula mass of water first, then
add the correct number of waters on to the rest of the formula:

- **Water**: Mᵣ(H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16 = **18**
- **The salt part**: Mᵣ(CuSO₄) = 63.5 + 32 + (4 × 16) = 63.5 + 32 + 64 = **159.5**
- **Assemble**: 159.5 + (5 × 18) = 159.5 + 90 = **249.5**

The same section then points the tool at a single element — **to find the
percentage by mass of copper in copper(II) oxide, CuO** (Aᵣ: O = 16,
Cu = 63.5): Mᵣ(CuO) = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5, of which **63.5 is copper**, so
the copper percentage is (63.5 / 79.5) × 100 ≈ **79.9%**. "Having found
the relative formula mass of a compound, we can work out the percentage
by mass of any part of it" — the book's own bridge to the next section.

:::key-point
For a hydrated salt, compute the water's Mᵣ once (18 for H₂O) and multiply
by the number of waters of crystallisation. The dot · in the formula
means "plus this many whole waters per formula unit" — nothing more
mysterious than that.
:::

:::exam-hint
Structure your Mᵣ working for hydrated salts exactly as the book does:
salt part, water part, assemble. Examiners award method marks for the
stages — a bare "249.5" with no visible 159.5 + 90 can lose the working
mark even with the right answer. And quote Mᵣ(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = 249.5 —
this exact value returns in Worked example 7 (percentage yield of these
very crystals).
:::

:::misconception
"CuSO₄·5H₂O means CuSO₄ joined to 5 separate H₂O molecules floating
around the crystal." The five waters are **chemically bound** into the
crystal structure — the book's Figure 5.8 shows them bonded to each
CuSO₄ unit. They are not loose water you could shake off; heating is
needed to drive them out (that experiment is Worked example 6's
neighbour, section 26).
:::
