Balance the equation for the combustion of ethane:

C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

The book's systematic method, left to right. **Carbons**: 2 on the left,
so 2CO₂ on the right. **Hydrogens**: 6 on the left, so 3H₂O on the right.
**Oxygens**: now there are 7 on the right ((2 × 2) + 3) but only 2 on the
left — and here comes the problem the book wants you to recognise: oxygen
atoms have to travel **in pairs** (O₂ molecules), so you cannot put an
odd number like 7 of them on the left directly.

The trick: **allow yourself halves**. 7 oxygen atoms is the same as
3½ oxygen molecules:

C₂H₆ + 3½O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Then **double everything** to remove the fraction:

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

:::key-point
Halving (then doubling the whole equation) is the standard escape from an
odd number of oxygens. You might reasonably object that half an O₂
molecule is not a physical thing — the doubling step answers that
objection; the final equation has only whole numbers.
:::

:::exam-hint
The book's KEY POINT in this section adds the full context: halves are
*acceptable* in balanced equations, but you will rarely meet them at
International GCSE — and if you do produce one, **double everything**
rather than leaving it. An examiner will accept either the halved or the
doubled form; the doubled form is the one you should hand in.
:::

:::misconception
"I can't balance this — 7 oxygens can't come from O₂ molecules, so the
reaction must be wrong." The reaction is fine; your *temporary* arithmetic
is what needs the half. Oxygen atoms still conserve: 14 on the left
(7 O₂ × 2), 14 on the right (4 CO₂ gives 8 + 6 H₂O gives 6). The half
molecule is scaffolding, removed by the doubling step before you write
your final answer.
:::
