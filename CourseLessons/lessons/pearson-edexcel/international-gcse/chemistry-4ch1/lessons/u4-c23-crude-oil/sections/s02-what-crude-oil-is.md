Crude oil did not appear on Earth ready-made — it is the product of
a slow underground cook that has been running for millions of years.
The story starts in ancient seas. Plankton, plants and animals died
and sank to the seabed, where layers of sediment gradually buried
them. Their shells and skeletons, rich in calcium carbonate, were
pressed into limestone; the soft tissue took a different route.
Sealed away from oxygen under layer after layer of sediment, that
organic material was slowly transformed by heat and the enormous
pressure of the rocks above into the sticky black liquid we pump
today. Two consequences of this origin story matter to you. First,
crude oil is **finite and [[non-renewable]]**: supplies are being
burned vastly faster than the planet can make them, and once the
existing reserves are gone they will not be replaced — or at least
not for many millions of years. Second, because it came from living
things, crude oil belongs to the [[fossil-fuel]] family, alongside
coal and natural gas.

Now the definition that carries the whole chapter. **Crude oil is a
mixture of [[hydrocarbon]]s** — and a [[hydrocarbon]] is a compound
containing carbon and hydrogen **only**. The mixture part means the
liquid is not one substance but a blend of many different molecules
jostling together, with no chemical bonds between them. The
hydrocarbon part means every one of those molecules is built from
exactly two elements: carbon atoms forming the backbone, hydrogen
atoms filling the remaining bonds. Nothing else is invited — no
oxygen, no nitrogen, no sulfur in the hydrocarbons themselves (the
sulfur that causes acid rain arrives later in this story, as an
impurity mixed into some crude oils rather than part of the
hydrocarbon molecules). The molecules in the mixture range
enormously in size, from small ones carrying just a few carbon and
hydrogen atoms to giants containing over a hundred atoms in total.

That size spread is not a boring detail — it is the lever the entire
oil industry pushes. As the number of carbon atoms in a hydrocarbon
molecule increases, the physical properties of the compound change
in smooth, predictable ways. The cause is easy to state: bigger
molecules attract their neighbours more strongly. These attractions
between neighbouring molecules are the [[intermolecular-forces]] you
met in u1:c08, and they grow steadily as molecules get longer,
because there is more molecule for its neighbours to grip. More
grip means more energy needed to pull molecules apart — and that
single sentence generates every trend the next section catalogues:
boiling point, volatility, viscosity, colour and even how easily the
liquid burns.

Keep the two ideas in this section separate in your mind, because
examiners test them from opposite directions. The *origin* story
(ancient biomass, heat, pressure, millions of years) answers
questions about why oil is finite and non-renewable. The
*composition* statement (a mixture of hydrocarbons) answers
questions about how oil can be separated and why its fractions
behave differently. A question asking "why is crude oil described
as finite?" wants the first story; a question asking "why can crude
oil be separated by distillation?" wants the second. Mix them up
and you write true sentences that earn no marks.

:::exam-hint
Two near-miss phrases lose marks here, so train them out early. Do
not write that crude oil "contains hydrocarbons" when asked what it
*is* — the specification sentence is *a mixture of hydrocarbons*,
and the word **mixture** is the mark: it is what licenses the
physical separation by fractional distillation that the next
sections explain. And when defining a hydrocarbon, the word
**only** carries the mark: "a compound of carbon and hydrogen only".
A description like "a compound containing carbon and hydrogen"
would also admit ethanol (which contains oxygen) — so the mark
scheme treats it as wrong. Exact wording is not decoration in these
definitions; it is the answer.
:::

:::misconception
"Bigger hydrocarbon molecules have stronger *covalent* bonds, which
is why they boil higher." Every mark in that sentence's ending is
lost. The covalent bonds *inside* a hydrocarbon molecule (C–C and
C–H) are about the same strength whatever the molecule's size, and
boiling never breaks covalent bonds anyway — the molecules leave as
whole, intact units. What grows with size is the strength of the
attraction *between* neighbouring molecules — the
[[intermolecular-forces]] idea — and overcoming attractions between
molecules takes far
less energy than breaking bonds inside them. Intermolecular =
between molecules (what boiling overcomes); covalent = inside
molecules (what chemical reactions attack). Keep the two labels on
separate shelves.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you retell the formation story in three steps (ancient marine
life buried by sediment, heat and pressure over millions of years,
crude oil formed) and explain in one sentence why that makes oil
non-renewable? Can you define *hydrocarbon* with the word *only* in
place? And can you explain why the size of a hydrocarbon molecule
affects how strongly its molecules attract their neighbours —
naming the right kind of force, intermolecular rather than covalent?
Those three answers are the raw material for everything that follows.
:::
