The oil industry sits at the very heart of modern life, and this
chapter explains why. Fuels for cars, aircraft and ships; plastics;
the organic chemicals that go into solvents, drugs, dyes and even
explosives — every one of these traces back to a single unappealing
starting material: [[crude-oil]], a sticky, black liquid that is
converted into useful things on an industrial scale. Nothing about
the liquid itself looks valuable. Its value comes entirely from what
it contains and what can be done to it, and both of those ideas are
the business of this chapter.

Here is the route you will travel. First, what crude oil actually
is: a [[fossil-fuel]] brewed underground over millions of years, and
— the statement everything else stands on — a **mixture of
[[hydrocarbon]]s**, compounds containing carbon and hydrogen only.
Second, how the oil industry exploits a simple property trend
(bigger molecules boil at higher temperatures) to split crude oil
into useful **fractions** by fractional distillation, and which
fraction gets used for which job, from refinery gases at the top of
the column to bitumen on the roads. Third, what happens when these
hydrocarbons burn: the energy release that makes them fuels, the
products of complete and incomplete combustion, and the pollutants —
carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, oxides of nitrogen — that turn
burning fuel into an environmental problem. Finally, **cracking**:
the industrial trick that converts surplus long-chain hydrocarbons
into the shorter, more profitable ones the market actually wants.

Every statement in that route is examinable in **both** Paper 1C and
Paper 2C, with no chemistry-only band anywhere in this chapter —
like the introduction to organic chemistry before it (u4:c22), this
is shared territory for every candidate. Twelve specification
statements carry the chapter, numbered 4.7 to 4.18, and they chain
together: the mixture idea (4.7) makes the distillation story
(4.8–4.10) possible; the fractions feed the combustion story
(4.11–4.13) and the pollution story (4.14–4.16); and the supply
mismatch between what distillation delivers and what the market
wants motivates cracking (4.17–4.18). Keep that chain in view as
you work through the sections — each one picks up exactly where the
last left off.

One habit is worth building from the first page: always ask *why*
the oil industry does things this way. Why distill instead of using
crude oil raw (because raw crude oil has no uses)? Why crack (because
distillation delivers the wrong balance of chain lengths)? Why
scrub factory gases (because sulfur impurities become acid rain)?
Examiners reward answers that connect a process to its reason, and
this chapter is full of reason-and-process pairs to practise on.

:::cross-curricular
Crude oil is as much an economics and geography story as a chemistry
one. Oil fields are not spread evenly across the planet, which is
why tanker routes, refineries and politics all follow the
distribution of underground reservoirs. The supply-and-demand logic
behind cracking (Section 8 of this lesson) is straight economics:
produce more of what sells at a better price. And the acid-rain and
climate threads connect to environmental science — the same
molecules can be traced from engine exhaust to lake chemistry.
:::

:::exam-hint
The phrase *mixture of hydrocarbons* is the most valuable six words
in this chapter. Every definition-level question about crude oil
wants exactly that phrase — a mixture (not a compound, not a pure
substance) of hydrocarbons (not "of carbon" — hydrogen must be in
the sentence too). When a one-mark question asks what crude oil is,
write the full phrase and move on; when a longer question asks you
to *explain* how crude oil is separated, that same mixture idea is
the first step of the explanation, because only mixtures separate
by distillation.
:::

:::misconception
"Crude oil is a single chemical." It is not — and the difference
matters for every process in this chapter. Crude oil is a **mixture**
of hundreds of different hydrocarbon molecules, from tiny gases to
wax-like giants. Because it is a mixture, its components keep their
own boiling points and can be separated **physically** (by
distillation) without any chemical reaction. If crude oil were a
single compound, no fractionating column on Earth could split it —
you cannot distill a pure substance into different substances.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state what crude oil is in one examinable sentence, using
the word *mixture* and naming the two elements its compounds
contain? Can you name three families of useful products the oil
industry delivers? And can you say — before reading further — why
crude oil must be separated before any of those products can be
made? If the last answer is "because it is a mixture of substances
with different boiling points", you are already ahead of the game.
:::
