Fractional distillation delivers whatever the crude oil happens to
contain — but the market does not want what crude oil happens to
contain. Here is the mismatch that makes cracking necessary. The
distillation fractions come out dominated by **long-chain
hydrocarbons**: fractions that are not in high demand and are not
particularly profitable to sell. Meanwhile there are **not enough
short-chain hydrocarbons** — yet demand for them is enormous,
because they are what fuel cars (petrol) and what the chemical
industry needs as reactive starting molecules. Supply and demand
point in opposite directions, and every barrel of crude oil
compounds the problem: the long chains keep coming whether the
market wants them or not.

**[[cracking]]** is the industry's answer: a process in which
**long-chain [[alkane]]s are converted into [[alkene]]s and
shorter-chain alkanes**. The big hydrocarbon molecules in the
diesel and fuel-oil fractions, for example, are broken down into
the smaller molecules that petrol needs. The conditions are a
specification statement in themselves: the [[fraction]] is **heated to
give a vapour**, then passed over a **[[catalyst]] of silicon dioxide
(silica) and aluminium oxide (alumina) at about 600–700 °C**.
Cracking can also be done at higher temperatures without a
catalyst (thermal cracking), but the silica–alumina route at
600–700 °C is the one to quote. Chemically, cracking is an example
of **[[thermal-decomposition]]**: a big molecule splitting into
smaller ones on heating. No oxygen is involved — this is not
burning. The bonds that break are the molecule's own C–C single
bonds, and where a chain snaps, a new C=C double bond forms — which
is why alkenes always appear among the products.

One honest warning before the equation: molecules crack in a
**fairly random way**. A long-chain molecule does not split at one
obligatory point — all sorts of bond-breaking events happen at once
inside a catalytic cracker, so a single reaction type produces a
whole shopping list of products. One possibility for a
thirteen-carbon molecule:

C₁₃H₂₈(l) → C₂H₄(g) + C₃H₆(g) + C₈H₁₈(l)

Check it by atom count — carbon: 2 + 3 + 8 = 13 ✓; hydrogen:
4 + 6 + 18 = 28 ✓. The mixture this one reaction produces tells the
whole story: **ethene and propene** (two alkenes — the double-bond
molecules the polymer industry lives on), plus **octane**, an
alkane and a petrol component. The product slate is always a
mixture of alkanes and alkenes, and it needs further separation
(including more fractional distillation) before anything is pure.

Why do oil companies go to the expense? Two reasons, and both are
examinable: **to produce more petrol** from the unloved long
chains, and **to produce more alkenes** for making polymers
(plastics) and other organic chemicals — alkenes are far more
reactive than alkanes, which makes them the chemical industry's
favourite building blocks (their chemistry fills u4:c25, and the
polymers story concludes in u4:c29).

A final word on drawing conventions, so a diagram never throws
you. Cracking schemes draw the long molecules "straightened out"
with right-angle bonds — real molecules are bent and coiled, but
the right-angle style makes every C–C bond easy to count and every
break-point easy to see. Read such drawings as counting diagrams,
not as shapes.

:::key-point
Cracking conditions: heat the fraction to vaporise it, pass the
vapour over a silica (SiO₂) and alumina (Al₂O₃) catalyst at about
600–700 °C. Long-chain alkane in; shorter-chain alkanes **and
alkenes** out. C–C bonds break; C=C bonds form.
:::

:::worked-example
Cracking turns one large molecule into several small ones. A
ten-carbon alkane, C₁₀H₂₂, cracks to give ethene (C₂H₄) and one
other hydrocarbon. Find the other hydrocarbon.

Write the skeleton: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₂H₄ + ? The unknown must soak up the
remaining atoms: carbon 10 − 2 = 8; hydrogen 22 − 4 = 18. The other
product is C₈H₁₈ — octane, an alkane, exactly the petrol component
the process exists to make. Now run the atom-count check
backwards: C 2 + 8 = 10 ✓, H 4 + 18 = 22 ✓. The same subtract-the-
known-products logic solves any "suggest the other product"
cracking question the exam can set.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two habits earn their marks here. First, conditions: "600–700 °C"
plus "silica or alumina catalyst" — the temperature range and the
catalyst names are each worth writing, and "high temperature alone"
is not the full answer. Second, products: if a cracking question
asks what is produced, the answer pattern is "alkenes **and**
shorter alkanes" — naming only alkenes, or only alkanes, drops a
mark. And never describe cracking as burning: it is thermal
decomposition (one molecule splitting on heating), with no oxygen
anywhere in the sentence.
:::

:::misconception
"Cracking needs oxygen because things are being broken up." No —
cracking is **thermal decomposition**, not combustion. The energy
comes from heat; the oxygen would only start a fire, and the whole
point is to split the molecule, not oxidise it. Watch also for the
reverse trap in multiple choice: "cracking produces pure ethene" is
false — the products are always a **mixture** of alkanes and
alkenes, and the industry separates what it needs afterwards.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the supply-and-demand problem in two sentences (too
many long chains, not enough short ones)? Can you give the full
cracking conditions — temperature range and both catalyst names?
Can you explain why alkenes must always appear among the products
(think: where does the C=C come from)? And can you balance a
cracking equation by atom count, the way the C₁₃H₂₈ example did?
:::

An industrial footnote worth having, clearly labelled as beyond the
exam. In industry, catalytic cracking is done by passing
hydrocarbons through a bed of **zeolite** (an aluminosilicate)
catalyst at about 500 °C and moderate pressures — the industrial
variant runs cooler than the school-story silica–alumina bed, and
in real crackers some isomerisation happens too, producing
branched-chain alkanes and ring structures alongside the straight
splits. Those branched molecules matter commercially: they raise a
fuel's **octane number** — a measure of how strongly a fuel resists
auto-ignition (burning spontaneously and uncontrollably inside the
engine, which wastes petrol and wears the engine). The higher the
octane number, the smoother the burn. Quote the specification
conditions in the exam (silica/alumina, 600–700 °C); keep zeolite
and octane numbers as your private proof that the story continues
past the syllabus edge.
