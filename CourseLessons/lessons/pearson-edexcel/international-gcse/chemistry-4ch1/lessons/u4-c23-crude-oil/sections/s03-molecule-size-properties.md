As hydrocarbon molecules get bigger, five properties change in step,
and every one of them has the same root cause: the
[[intermolecular-forces]] between neighbouring molecules grow
stronger, so more energy is needed to pull the molecules apart. Walk
the five trends in order, because together they *are* specification
statement 4.10 — the trend in colour, boiling point and viscosity of
the main fractions.

**Boiling point increases.** The larger the molecule, the higher
its [[boiling-point]]. Bigger molecules attract each other more
strongly, so converting the liquid into widely separated gas
molecules demands more energy — and the temperature at which that
energy is available rises with every extra carbon atom in the chain.

**Volatility falls.** A liquid is [[volatile]] if it turns to a
vapour easily at room temperature — in everyday terms, it evaporates
quickly. The bigger the hydrocarbon, the more slowly it evaporates,
because its molecules grip their neighbours more tightly and are
less eager to escape into the air. Small hydrocarbons are so
volatile that at room temperature they are gases already; large ones
barely evaporate at all.

**Viscosity rises.** [[viscosity]] measures how reluctantly a liquid
flows. Liquids made of small hydrocarbon molecules are runny — pour
petrol and it splashes instantly. Liquids containing large molecules
flow less easily, because the stronger attractions between their
molecules resist the molecules sliding past one another. The
thickest fractions pour like syrup, or barely pour at all.

**Colour darkens.** The liquids become darker in colour as molecule
size increases: light, almost colourless fractions at the small end;
the amber of kerosene and diesel in the middle; and the near-black
of the heaviest residues at the large end.

**Ease of burning falls.** Bigger hydrocarbons do not burn as
easily as smaller ones, and this limits how useful the big
fractions are as fuels. It is a trend to hold onto: the fractions
that burn most readily are the small ones, which is one reason the
smallest fraction — refinery gas — is prized as a clean instant
fuel, while the giant molecules of bitumen never get burned at all.

One vocabulary bridge locks the first two trends together. The
[[boiling-point]] is the same temperature as the point at which a
gas condenses to form a liquid — it could equally be called the
condensation point. Boiling (liquid to gas) and condensing (gas to
liquid) are the two directions of the same change of state, meeting
at one temperature. This single idea does a surprising amount of
work in the next section: the fractionating column separates
hydrocarbons purely by letting each one condense at the height
where the column's temperature matches its boiling point.

:::key-point
The boiling point is the same as the temperature at which a gas
condenses to form a liquid; it could also be called the
condensation point. Boiling and condensing are opposite directions
of the same change of state, and they meet at one temperature.
:::

:::exam-hint
Trend questions follow one template, so answer with the template's
shape: state the **direction** (increases/decreases), then give the
**reason** (bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces of
attraction → more energy needed to separate them). A bare "boiling
point increases" usually scores one of two marks; the reason earns
the other. And note what the reason must *not* say: nothing about
bonds breaking — intermolecular attractions are overcome, not
chemical bonds. The phrase "intermolecular forces of attraction"
itself is worth writing in full; it is the mark scheme's own
vocabulary.
:::

:::misconception
"Volatile means explosive or flammable." It does not — volatility
is purely about how easily a liquid **evaporates**, with no fire
required. A volatile liquid turns to a vapour easily at room
temperature; whether that vapour then burns depends on entirely
different chemistry. The two words travel together in the oil
industry (small hydrocarbons are both volatile *and* easily
burned), which is how the confusion starts, but in the exam they
answer different questions: volatility = evaporation rate; ease of
burning = how readily the substance reacts with oxygen. Keep them
separate.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you list all five trends (boiling point, volatility, viscosity,
colour, ease of burning) with their directions as molecule size
increases? Can you give the single root cause behind all five? Can
you explain why bigger molecules evaporate more slowly, using the
word *intermolecular*? And can you state the link between boiling
point and condensation point? Four confident answers here mean
specification point 4.10 is yours.
:::
