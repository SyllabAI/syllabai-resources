You now hold the complete crude-oil story: what the liquid is (a
mixture of [[hydrocarbon]]s, formed from ancient marine life, finite
and non-renewable); how the size trend in boiling point, volatility,
viscosity, colour and ease of burning lets a fractionating column
sort it into [[fraction]]s; the six fractions and their uses, from [[refinery-gases]]
LPG at the top to [[bitumen]] roads at the bottom; what [[fuel]] combustion
delivers (complete: carbon dioxide and water; incomplete: the
[[carbon-monoxide]] hazard); the two [[acid-rain]] chains (sulfur
impurities and engine-formed oxides of nitrogen); and the [[cracking]]
process that rebalances the fractions towards petrol and alkenes.
Every specification point from 4.7 to 4.18 has been touched, in
order, and each one now joins the platform question bank through
the links below — practice items arrive keyed to exactly the
statements these sections built.

Before opening the bank, audit yourself against the chapter's core
moves. Can you define [[crude-oil]] and a [[hydrocarbon]] with the words
*mixture* and *only* in place? Can you walk a molecule through the
fractionating column and explain why it condenses where it does?
Can you match all six fractions to their uses and read those uses
off the size trend when memory fails? Can you build — not recall —
a complete-combustion equation from any formula you are handed, and
name the products when the air supply runs short? Can you run both
acid-rain chains, equations included, from impurity to damaged
forest? Can you give cracking's conditions and explain the supply
and demand logic that justifies the whole process? Any wobble is a
section to revisit, not a reason to start over.

The question bank for this chapter trains a predictable set of
shapes, so rehearse the moves rather than the answers. Definition
items open the set: the two-element audit for hydrocarbons, and the
mixture statement for crude oil. Explanation items then ask *how*
the fractions separate in the column — the four-step skeleton from
Section 4 of this lesson earns those marks in full. Matching items
pair fractions with uses, and comparison items ask you to place two
fractions side by side by average molecule size, colour and
viscosity — pure 4.10 trend-reading. There is a size-deduction
item: given a large hydrocarbon from the diesel fraction, suggest a
plausible formula from the gasoline fraction, which is the size
trend turned into numbers. Equation items ask for the
complete-combustion equation of a named fuel (butane is the
favourite) and for why incomplete combustion's product is
dangerous. The acid-rain block asks *why* questions: why sulfur
impurities make acid rain, why petrol engines make oxides of
nitrogen, and why the acids attack marble buildings — each a chain
answer with an equation available. Finally the cracking block
tests reasons (two of them), conditions, and one satisfying
puzzle: a twelve-carbon molecule cracked to give two molecules of
ethene plus one other product, with a balanced equation wanted and
an alternative split suggested for extension. The bank closes with
an open essay-style item — imagine a world where fossil fuels never
formed — that rewards the connective thinking this chapter has
been quietly training all along.

:::exam-hint
Balance the practice time against the mark structure: the
definition and matching items are fast marks — bank them first and
refuse to overthink them. The chain explanations (distillation,
acid rain) are where the marks cluster, and each chain has a fixed
link order that examiners expect; write the links in order and the
marks follow. For the cracking puzzle items, the subtract-the-
known-products method from Section 8 works on every variant — set
the skeleton equation, count what is missing, and check by atom
count before moving on.
:::

:::misconception
"The question bank rewards knowing the book's answers." It rewards
knowing the *chemistry* — the same reaction can be asked about
through a different fuel, a different fraction or a different
pollutant, and a memorised answer to a different question will not
fit. That is why this lesson trained methods (the balance order,
the column walk-through, the chain skeletons, the atom-count
check) rather than specific answers. If you can run the method,
the question's costume does not matter.
:::

:::summary
- **Crude oil (4.7)**: a mixture of hydrocarbons — compounds of
  carbon and hydrogen only — formed from ancient marine organisms
  by heat and pressure over millions of years; finite and
  non-renewable.
- **Fractional distillation (4.8)**: crude oil is heated, vapours
  rise up a column that is hot at the bottom and cool at the top;
  each fraction condenses where the temperature matches its
  boiling-point range; small molecules high, large molecules low;
  top gases and bottom residue.
- **The fractions (4.9)**: refinery gases (LPG — heating and
  cooking), gasoline (cars), kerosene (jet fuel, heating oil,
  paraffin), diesel (buses, lorries, some trains), fuel oil
  (ships, industrial heating), bitumen (roads).
- **The trend (4.10)**: as molecule size increases — boiling point
  up, volatility down, viscosity up, colour darker, ease of burning
  down; root cause: stronger intermolecular forces of attraction.
- **Fuels and combustion (4.11, 4.12)**: a fuel releases heat
  energy when burned; complete combustion (plenty of O₂) gives
  CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete combustion (limited O₂) gives carbon
  monoxide and/or soot, plus water.
- **Carbon monoxide (4.13)**: colourless, odourless, toxic — it
  reduces the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
- **Pollutants (4.14, 4.15)**: engine temperatures force
  N₂ + O₂ → 2NO (oxides of nitrogen, NOₓ); sulfur impurities burn
  to SO₂: S + O₂ → SO₂.
- **Acid rain (4.16)**: SO₂ and NOₓ → sulfuric and nitric acids in
  the atmosphere; pH below the natural ≈5.6, often about 4; kills
  trees and lake life, attacks limestone, marble and iron; fixes:
  low-sulfur fuels, gas scrubbing, catalytic converters.
- **Cracking (4.17, 4.18)**: long-chain alkanes → alkenes +
  shorter alkanes, over silica/alumina at 600–700 °C; needed
  because supply of long chains outruns demand while short chains
  (petrol, alkene feedstocks) run short; C–C bonds break, C=C bonds
  form.
:::
