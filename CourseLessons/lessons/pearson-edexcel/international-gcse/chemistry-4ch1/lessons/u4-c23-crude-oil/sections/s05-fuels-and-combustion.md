Every hydrocarbon in every fraction does the same fundamental trick:
it burns in air, reacting with oxygen to release a large amount of
heat. That single shared property is what makes the fractions worth
having — and it makes **fuel** the first specification definition of
this section: a **[[fuel]] is a substance that, when burned,
releases heat energy**. Notice what the definition does *not* say.
It does not mention hydrocarbons (coal and hydrogen are fuels too),
and it does not mention carbon dioxide. The definition is exactly
two claims: burning happens, and heat comes out. State it that way
and the mark is yours.

When a hydrocarbon burns in **plenty** of oxygen, the reaction is
**[[complete-combustion]]** and the products are always the same
pair: carbon dioxide and water. Two worked equations show the
pattern. Burning methane, the major constituent of natural gas:

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

and burning octane, one of the hydrocarbons present in petrol:

2C₈H₁₈(l) + 25O₂(g) → 16CO₂(g) + 18H₂O(l)

Do not memorise equations like these — there are far too many
possible hydrocarbons to learn them all, and the exam can hand you
any formula. Instead, carry the products (CO₂ + H₂O), know the
fuel's formula, and balance on the spot. The octane equation looks
fearsome, but it is just atom bookkeeping: 16 carbons on the left
(2 × C₈H₁₈) demand 16 CO₂; 36 hydrogens (2 × 18) demand 18 H₂O;
and those products need 16 × 2 + 18 × 1 = 50 oxygen atoms, which is
25 O₂. Balance the carbon first, then the hydrogen, then count the
oxygen — every hydrocarbon equation falls to that order.

When oxygen is **in short supply** — a badly maintained gas
appliance, a blocked air vent, a smouldering fire — the same fuel
burns incompletely, and the products turn dangerous.
**[[incomplete-combustion]]** produces carbon (soot) and/or toxic
**[[carbon-monoxide]]** instead of (or alongside) carbon dioxide.
Methane burning in a badly maintained appliance:

2CH₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO(g) + 4H₂O(l)

Compare the oxygen count with complete combustion of the same two
methane molecules, which needs four O₂ (double the one-molecule
equation above): incomplete combustion manages with just three —
and the carbon pays the price, leaving as carbon monoxide instead
of carbon dioxide. The chemistry is honest about
the shortage — with insufficient oxygen, each carbon cannot be fully
oxidised to CO₂, so some of it stops one step short at CO. The
examinable product list for incomplete combustion is therefore
**carbon monoxide and carbon (soot), plus water**; for complete
combustion it is **carbon dioxide and water only**.

Carbon monoxide earns its own specification statement because of
what it does to you. The gas is colourless and odourless — no
sight, no smell, no warning — and it is intensely poisonous because
it **reduces the ability of the blood to carry oxygen** around the
body. Oxygen transport fails, body cells run short of oxygen for
respiration, and the result is illness or death. This is why a
gas appliance that might burn fuel incompletely must be serviced
and ventilated, and why carbon-monoxide detectors exist: the gas
gives your senses nothing to work with.

One extension thought for the bonding-curious. Try drawing a
dot-and-cross diagram for carbon monoxide and you will meet a
puzzle: carbon appears not to make its usual four bonds. Carbon
monoxide is genuinely a strange little molecule — its bonding does
not fit the simple counting rules that work so neatly for CO₂ —
and that oddity is part of why the gas is so chemically eager to
latch onto the oxygen-carrying sites in your blood. You do not
need the bonding detail for the exam; you need the poisoning
consequence above. The diagram puzzle is a preview of how bonding
rules bend once you leave the most comfortable molecules of
Unit 1.

:::did-you-know
Carbon monoxide combines with **haemoglobin**, the molecule inside
red blood cells that normally carries oxygen — and it binds to
haemoglobin **more strongly than oxygen does**. Molecules of carbon
monoxide essentially lock the oxygen-carrying sites shut, which is
why the blood's capacity to transport oxygen collapses. (At
International GCSE you only need the consequence — carbon monoxide
reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity; the haemoglobin
detail is the story behind it.)
:::

:::exam-hint
In the exam you will never be asked to recall the octane equation —
you will be asked to *construct* one from a formula you are given.
The examinable skill is the balance order: carbon atoms first
(product CO₂), hydrogen atoms second (product H₂O), oxygen last
(count both products, then divide by 2 for O₂). Practise it on
propane (C₃H₈) and butane (C₄H₁₀) until it takes under a minute.
And when a question says "incomplete combustion", the two product
words it is fishing for are **carbon monoxide** and **carbon
(soot)** — water alone is not enough of an answer, and writing
carbon dioxide in an incomplete-combustion box loses the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Incomplete combustion is a different fuel burning differently." The
fuel is identical — the *oxygen supply* is what changes. Methane
burning with plenty of air gives CO₂ + H₂O; the same methane in the
same burner starved of air gives CO (and soot) + H₂O. Same bonds,
same fuel, different oxygen availability. The second classic slip is
writing that incomplete combustion "cannot happen with enough
oxygen present" in the wrong direction: complete combustion needs
*plenty* of oxygen; incomplete combustion happens when oxygen is
*limited*. If a question shows you a yellow, sooty flame, it is
telling you the air supply failed — say so explicitly.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define a fuel in one sentence with no mention of
hydrocarbons or carbon dioxide? Can you write the
complete-combustion equation for methane from memory of the
*method* (products first, then balance C, H, then O)? Can you list
the products of complete and of incomplete combustion, and name the
condition that decides which set you get? And can you explain — in
mark-scheme language — why carbon monoxide is poisonous?
:::
