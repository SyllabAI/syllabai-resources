Crude oil as it comes out of the ground has no uses. Nothing about
an unseparated blend of hundreds of different hydrocarbons will run
an engine or heat a home — the usefulness arrives only after the
mixture has been sorted. The sorting happens in an oil refinery by
**[[fractional-distillation]]**, which splits crude oil into
**[[fraction]]s**: simpler mixtures that each contain a narrow range
of hydrocarbon sizes, and therefore similar boiling points. Because
every fraction is still a mixture (of similar-sized hydrocarbons,
not of one pure compound), this is a physical separation from start
to finish — no chemical bonds are made or broken anywhere in the
column.

Here is the machinery. Crude oil is heated until it boils, and the
vapours pass into a [[fractionating-column]] — a tall tower that is
**hot at the bottom and progressively cooler towards the top**. The
vapours rise up the column, cooling as they climb. How far up a
particular hydrocarbon travels is decided by exactly one property:
its boiling point. Each hydrocarbon rises until it reaches the
height where the column's temperature has fallen to its own boiling
point — and there it **condenses** to liquid and can be drained
away. Small molecules have low boiling points, so they ride high
before condensing; long-chain hydrocarbons have high boiling points
and condense low down. The column's temperature gradient sorts the
mixture for free, floor by floor, all the way up.

Follow one molecule through the column to see the logic run
end-to-end.

:::worked-example
A hydrocarbon boils at 120 °C. Track its journey through the
fractionating column.

Near the **bottom**, the temperature is far above 120 °C. At that
temperature the hydrocarbon is comfortably a gas, so it keeps
rising with the vapour stream. As it climbs, the surrounding
temperature falls. When it reaches the height where the column is
at exactly 120 °C, the hydrocarbon can no longer stay as a gas —
it **condenses** to liquid at that level and is removed as part of
the fraction collected there. A hydrocarbon boiling at 40 °C would
keep rising far past this point (the column is still too hot for
it to condense), while one boiling at 300 °C would condense much
lower down. One boiling point, one condensation height — that is
the entire sorting principle.
:::

The extremes of the column are worth a sentence each, because both
are specification-favourite details. At the very top, the
[[refinery-gases]] fraction contains the smallest hydrocarbons,
whose boiling points are so low that the column never gets cold
enough for them to condense — they leave the top as gases. At the
very bottom, some hydrocarbons are so large that the column is
never hot enough to boil them: they never vaporise at all, and they
drain from the base as a thick liquid [[residue]] — the source
material for bitumen, melted with rock fragments to surface roads.

:::key-point
Fractional distillation works because each hydrocarbon condenses at
the height where the column's temperature equals its boiling
point: low boiling points condense high up, high boiling points
condense low down. The smallest molecules leave the top as gases
and the largest leave the bottom as residue.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Describe how fractional distillation separates crude oil into
fractions" is a four-mark classic, and it has a fixed skeleton:
(1) crude oil is heated so most of it boils/vaporises; (2) the
vapours enter a fractionating column that is hot at the bottom and
cooler at the top; (3) fractions **condense** at different heights,
each where the temperature falls to that fraction's boiling-point
range; (4) short-chain hydrocarbons have low boiling points and
condense near the top, long-chain ones condense near the bottom.
Notice that all four steps are physical-process language — boil,
rise, cool, condense. Writing about reactions or burning loses
marks instantly, because nothing in the column reacts.
:::

:::misconception
"The column separates crude oil into pure compounds." It does not —
each collected **fraction is itself still a mixture**, just a much
simpler one: hydrocarbons within a narrow band of chain lengths and
boiling points. Petrol is not one substance; kerosene is not one
substance. They are blends of similar-sized hydrocarbons that
condense over a similar temperature range. That is why fractions
are described by *ranges* (of boiling point, of chain length) and
why two refineries can sell "petrol" whose exact molecular recipes
differ. The column sorts by size band, not by identity.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you explain why crude oil must be separated before it has any

end is cool — and state what determines how far up a hydrocarbon
rises before condensing? Can you walk a 120 °C hydrocarbon through
the column from vaporiser to drain? And can you name what leaves
the very top (uncondensed gases) and the very bottom (residue)?
:::
