Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide — and carbon dioxide
is a **[[greenhouse-gas]]**: a gas that traps heat radiated from
the Earth's surface (heat that originally arrived from the Sun).
The more greenhouse gas in the atmosphere, the more trapped heat,
which is why rising carbon-dioxide levels are linked to climate
change. That global thread is developed in u2:c13; this section
follows the *local* pollution threads, because combustion exhaust
carries more than carbon dioxide — and the two pollutants that
matter for this specification both rain back down as acid.

The first thread starts with an impurity. [[fossil-fuel]]s contain
a small amount of **sulfur** — not in the hydrocarbon molecules,
but mixed in with them. When the fuel burns, the sulfur burns too,
producing **[[sulfur-dioxide]]**:

S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g)

Once in the atmosphere, sulfur dioxide meets water vapour and
oxygen, and the mixture slowly converts it into **sulfuric acid** —
a strong acid and a major component of **[[acid-rain]]**:

2SO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) → 2H₂SO₄(aq)

(A milder pathway also exists: sulfur dioxide reacting with water
alone forms **sulfurous acid**, H₂SO₃, a weaker acid —
SO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₃(aq) — but the sulfuric-acid route is the
one to quote for acid rain.) Notice the chain: sulfur impurity →
sulfur dioxide → acid. No step needs a car engine; power stations
and factories burning fossil fuels do most of the damage.

The second thread is an engine's own doing. In a petrol engine,
electric sparks ignite the petrol–air mixture, and the spark's
temperature is so high that it does something ordinary burning
cannot: it makes **nitrogen and oxygen from the air react with each
other**. Those two gases normally ignore one another completely,
but inside the cylinder the temperature is high enough to force
them together into **[[oxides-of-nitrogen]]**:

N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g)

Several oxides of nitrogen are involved, including NO and NO₂ —
they are often grouped under the general formula **NOₓ**. Like
sulfur dioxide, the oxides of nitrogen are converted in the
atmosphere (with water and oxygen) into an acid — **nitric acid**,
HNO₃ — which also contributes to acid rain. So acid rain has two
chemical sources with one shared outcome: sulfur dioxide from
burning sulfur impurities, and oxides of nitrogen from the
temperatures inside car engines.

What does acid rain actually do? Its pH tells the numbers first.
Rain is naturally slightly acidic (about pH 5.6) because dissolved
carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid; **acid rain is rain with a pH
lower than that — often around pH 4** — because of the extra
pollutant acids. The damage is broad. It kills trees and damages
forests; it acidifies lakes until fish die and, in the worst cases,
nothing lives at all; it attacks limestone buildings and marble
statues (both calcium carbonate) and corrodes metals such as iron.
The limestone attack is a chemistry you already own — carbonate
plus acid:

CaCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CaSO₄(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

The solutions follow directly from the causes. Remove the sulfur:
most petrol sold for cars is now low-sulfur or desulfurised, which
strips the acid-rain chain at its first link. Scrub the exhaust
gases: power stations and factories treat their waste gases to
remove SO₂ and NOₓ before they reach the sky. And treat the engine
side: catalytic converters in cars cut the oxides of nitrogen
leaving the exhaust. Three fixes, one per link in the chain —
which is exactly how an "explain how acid rain can be reduced"
answer should be organised.

:::key-point
Several oxides of nitrogen are involved in engine pollution,
including NO and NO₂; they are often given the general formula NOₓ.
Rain is naturally slightly acidic (pH ≈ 5.6, from dissolved CO₂);
acid rain is rain with a lower pH than this — often about pH 4 —
because of dissolved pollutant acids.
:::

:::key-point
N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g): in car engines the temperature is high
enough for nitrogen and oxygen from the air to react, forming
oxides of nitrogen that are converted to nitric acid in the
atmosphere and contribute to acid rain. The fixes: low-sulfur
fuels, scrubbing power-station and factory gases, catalytic
converters.
:::

:::exam-hint
Acid-rain questions are chain questions, and the mark scheme pays
each link separately. For "explain how burning fossil fuels causes
acid rain": sulfur **impurity** in the fuel → burns to **sulfur
dioxide** → reacts with **water and oxygen** in the atmosphere →
**sulfuric acid** → falls as acid rain. For "explain why petrol
engines produce oxides of nitrogen": the spark ignition makes the
temperature **high enough** for nitrogen and oxygen **from the
air** to react. The word **impurity** is a mark in itself — sulfur
is not part of the hydrocarbon molecules — and "from the air" is
the other easy mark: the nitrogen comes from the atmosphere, not
from the fuel.
:::

:::misconception
"The sulfur in acid rain comes from burning the hydrocarbon
itself." It does not — pure hydrocarbons contain no sulfur at all
(carbon and hydrogen only, remember). The sulfur arrives as an
**impurity** mixed into some crude oils and coals, and burning
simply oxidises it to sulfur dioxide. Keep the distinction: CO₂
comes from the fuel's own carbon; SO₂ comes from the fuel's
contamination. A parallel slip is blaming acid rain entirely on
cars — power stations and factories burning fossil fuels are the
main sulfur-dioxide sources, while cars contribute the oxides of
nitrogen. Match each pollutant to its proper source.
:::
