Specification point 4.9 is a memory job with a logic behind it: know
the names and uses of the main fractions. There are six to carry,
and the beautiful part is that the uses are not arbitrary — they
follow the size trend from the previous sections, so you can
*rederive* any use you forget. Small molecules: volatile, low
boiling point, burn easily, runny — perfect for fueling things that
need instant, clean flames. Large molecules: thick, dark, hard to
burn — good for waterproofing and road-building, useless in an
engine. Every fraction sits somewhere on that slide from gas to
tar.

**[[refinery-gases]]** are the smallest fraction: a mixture of
methane, ethane, propane and butane (which can be separated into
individual gases if a use demands it). Their boiling points are so
low that they never condense in the column — they emerge at the top
as gases. Bottled and sold as **liquefied petroleum gas (LPG)**,
they fuel domestic heating and cooking: instant ignition, clean
flame, no residue.

**[[gasoline]]** — the petrol fraction — is a mixture of
hydrocarbons with similar boiling points in the middle-small range,
used as the fuel for cars. Like every fraction it is a blend, not a
pure compound; octane (from the previous section's equation) is one
of its many hydrocarbons.

**[[kerosene]]** carries three uses to memorise: fuel for jet
aircraft, domestic heating oil, and — under its old name
*paraffin* — small heaters and lamps. **[[diesel]]** fuels buses,
lorries, some cars and some railway engines; and diesel has a
second life that points forward: some of it is converted into more
useful products, including petrol, by **cracking** — the process
the next section explains in full.

**[[fuel-oil]]** is the thick fraction burned in ships' boilers and
for industrial heating — bigness is no disadvantage when your
"engine" is a furnace with room to burn slowly. And at the bottom
of the column, **[[bitumen]]**: a thick, black material that is
melted and mixed with small pieces of rock to make the top surface
of roads. Notice that bitumen is the fraction that is never burned
at all — its molecules are so large and so reluctant to vaporise
that using it as a surface material is worth more than using it as
a fuel.

Thread the fossil-fuel idea through the list and the section
closes on itself. [[fossil-fuel]]s include coal, natural gas and
all the crude-oil fractions burned above — fuels that come from
things that were once alive, which is why the energy in your car's
tank (and the carbon it releases) is, ultimately, ancient sunlight
stored by organisms hundreds of millions of years ago.

:::key-point
Fossil fuels include coal, gas and fuels derived from crude oil.
These all come from things that were once alive — which is exactly
why they are finite: the stock cannot be remade on any human
timescale.
:::

:::exam-hint
The fraction-to-use match is a one-mark-per-line table waiting to
happen, and the six pairings are: refinery gases → LPG for heating
and cooking; gasoline → car fuel; kerosene → jet fuel, heating oil,
paraffin lamps; diesel → buses, lorries, some trains; fuel oil →
ships and industrial furnaces; bitumen → road surfaces. If a use
slips your mind, reconstruct it from the size trend: small = clean
fast fuel for engines, large = thick material for surfaces and
furnaces. And read question stems carefully — "state one use of
kerosene" wants exactly one, so the jet-fuel answer alone is a
clean mark; listing three uses cannot score more than the one mark
on offer.
:::

:::misconception
"Petrol (gasoline) is octane." It is not — petrol is a **mixture**
of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points, of which octane is
just one member (the one that happened to appear in the previous
section's equation). The same applies to every fraction: kerosene,
diesel and fuel oil are all blends of many different hydrocarbons
within a size band. This is worth holding onto precisely because
equations tempt you to think in single molecules — the equations
model *one representative* hydrocarbon from the blend, while the
fraction in the tank is always the whole band.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you name all six main fractions in column order (top to
bottom) and attach one use to each? Can you explain why the
smallest fraction never condenses in the column? Can you say what
LPG stands for and which gases it contains? And can you explain why
bitumen's use (roads) fits its position at the bottom of the column
— large molecules, high boiling point, thick, and not worth
burning?
:::
