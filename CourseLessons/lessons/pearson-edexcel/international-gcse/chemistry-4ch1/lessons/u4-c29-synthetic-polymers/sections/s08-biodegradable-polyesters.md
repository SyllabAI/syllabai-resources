Condensation polymers carry a different environmental account
from the addition polymers of the disposal section, and the
difference traces back to their seams. As with addition
polymers there are environmental issues with the disposal of
condensation polymers, but these are more reactive because of
the ester linkage in the chain, and they will
all break down eventually — although "eventually" can still
mean hundreds of years. The ester groups give the bacteria's
chemistry somewhere to attack. Building on that crack in the
armour, scientists have developed some biodegradable
[[polyester]]s which break down much more quickly. These are
called [[biopolyester]]s, and they are the closest thing this
chapter offers to a solution of the disposal problem that
addition polymers created.

The flagship [[biopolyester]] is made from lactic acid. Lactic
acid can be obtained from corn starch, and when it undergoes
polymerisation it forms a biodegradable polyester —
[[polylactic-acid]], or PLA — which can be used for making
biodegradable plastic bags or in surgery for internal
stitches. Both uses lean on the same property: the material
does its job, then quietly goes away. A compostable bag
decomposes with the compost; a suture holds the wound while it
heals and then degrades inside the body without a second
operation to remove it.

:::did-you-know
Lactic acid, more properly known as 2-hydroxypropanoic acid
(CH₃CHOHCOOH), has both an alcohol group and a carboxylic acid
group in the same molecule — so it can polymerise with itself.
One molecule supplies both ends of every condensation join,
which is why a single monomer type is enough to build PLA.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The amino-acid extension of condensation polymerisation is the
chemistry behind biology's most important polymers. Amino
acids carry an amine group (–NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group
(–COOH) in the same molecule; the amine group reacts very
similarly to the –OH group of alcohols, connecting with a
carboxylic acid to form an amide bond. When amino acids join
together this way they form polypeptides — the primary
structure of every protein, from enzymes to keratin. The
condensation logic you learned in the previous section is,
literally, the logic of you.
:::

:::exam-hint
Statement 4.50C is a *know*: some polyesters, known as
[[biopolyester]]s, are biodegradable. The examinable chain is
short — condensation polymers are more reactive than addition
polymers because of the ester linkage; biopolyesters break
down much more quickly; PLA is made from lactic acid obtained
from corn starch. A one-mark question usually wants the
linkage; a two-mark question wants the linkage *and* the
consequence. Do not overclaim: ordinary condensation polymers
also break down eventually — hundreds of years — so
"biodegradable" means faster, not instant.
:::

:::misconception
"Biodegradable means the plastic disappears in days in any
bin or sea." The word is comparative, not magical. Ordinary
condensation polymers already break down eventually — their
ester links are attackable — but that can take hundreds of
years, and [[polylactic-acid]] still needs the right
conditions to decompose usefully. What the [[biopolyester]]
label promises is *much more quickly*, not overnight, and
never a licence for littering.
:::
