This section crosses the flag: from here to the end of the
chapter the content is chemistry-only, examined on Paper 2C
(4.48C onwards). The chemistry itself is a contrast study.
When ethene molecules combine to make a chain, the double
bonds break and the monomers just add onto each other —
nothing is lost, which is why the first half of this lesson is
called addition. Making a [[polyester]] is different. Instead
of one type of monomer, you often have two, joining
together alternately, and each time two monomers combine a
small molecule such as water or hydrogen chloride is lost. The
elimination of water gives the name [[condensation-polymerisation]]
to this type of polymerisation: a dicarboxylic acid reacts
with a diol, and the products are a polyester and water
(4.48C). It is the same condensation idea you met when an
alcohol and a carboxylic acid built an ester and expelled
water (u4:c28) — now run along a whole chain instead of once.

Draw the general reaction as a block diagram and the pattern
becomes visible. One monomer is a dicarboxylic acid — think of
it as a blue box with reactive –COOH groups at both ends. The
other is a diol — a red box with –OH groups at both ends. Line
them up and join them: at every junction an –OH and a
–COOH meet, water is removed, and an ester group forms in its
place. The chain can continue at both ends, so the boxes
alternate — acid, alcohol, acid, alcohol — with an ester group
at every seam. That repeating seam is the [[ester-link]], the
same –COO– arrangement that defined the esters of the previous
lesson (u4:c28), now repeated all the way down the polymer.

:::key-point
One of the monomers is a [[diol]]: an alcohol molecule with
one –OH at each end. Ethane-1,2-diol has the structural
formula CH₂OHCH₂OH — the prefix 'eth' says two carbon atoms,
the code 'di' says two –OH groups, and the number '1,2' says
they sit on carbons 1 and 2, one on each carbon.
Propane-1,3-diol, CH₂OHCH₂CH₂OH, has one more carbon in the
chain. And it is not a mistake that an extra 'e' appears in
these names: we talk about propan-1-ol but propane-1,3-diol.
:::

The second monomer is a [[dicarboxylic-acid]]: a carboxylic
molecule with one –COOH at each end. Ethanedioic acid has the
structural formula HOOCCOOH — two carboxylic acid groups
back to back. A more complicated example is hexane-1,6-dioic
acid, HOOCCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂COOH, where 'hex' indicates six carbon
atoms in the main chain and '1,6-dioic acid' means two
carboxylic acid groups at either end of the molecule, on
carbons 1 and 6.

Under the right conditions, diol and dicarboxylic molecules
join together with the loss of a molecule of water each time a
new bond is formed. Both monomers react at each end, so the
joining continues at both ends of the growing chain, producing
a long-chain [[polyester]] held together by a large number of
ester groups. It does not matter whether you take the OH from
the dicarboxylic acid and the H from the alcohol or the other
way around — the polymer formed is still the same. A useful
way to practise: think of one monomer as a red rectangle with
an –OH at each end and the other as a blue rectangle with a
–COOH at each end, draw those end groups as displayed
formulae, line the rectangles up, remove the water between
them, and slot the remainder together. It looks difficult
because it is unfamiliar; a few runs and it becomes routine.

For the ethanedioic acid and ethane-1,2-diol pair, the repeat
unit and the balanced equation are:

```
n HOOC–COOH + n HO–CH₂CH₂–OH  →  –[ OC–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O ]n–  +  2n H₂O
    (ethanedioic      (ethane-1,2-     (the polyester        (water)
      acid)             diol)          repeat unit)
```

Balance check per repeat: the monomers supply C₂H₂O₄ +
C₂H₆O₂ = C₄H₈O₆; the repeat unit carries C₄H₄O₄ and two water
molecules carry H₄O₂ — carbon, hydrogen and oxygen all
accounted for. The block-diagram version of the same reaction
works for any polyester: put real carbons and hydrogens into
the boxes — hexane-1,6-dioic acid and ethane-1,2-diol, for
example — and the identical remove-water logic builds the
identical alternating chain. One refinement for the curious:
the equation balances with 2n water molecules, but a real
chain of finite length forms only (2n – 1) of them, because
one –OH and one –COOH are left dangling at the chain's ends.

:::exam-hint
4.49C asks for the structural and displayed formula of a
polyester repeat unit, given the monomers. Draw the ester
groups *inside* the brackets and let the continuation bonds
pass through the oxygen ends: –[OC–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O]n–. The two
classic errors are closing the chain into a ring (forgetting
the continuation bonds) and drawing an –COOH group inside the
unit — the water has already been expelled, so the unit
contains ester links, not acid groups.
:::

:::misconception
"Condensation polymerisation is just addition polymerisation
with extra steps — the products are the same kind of polymer."
The two are distinguishable at a glance, and the discriminator
is the small molecule. Addition: one monomer type, double bond
opened, nothing expelled. Condensation: two monomer types
alternating, and a small molecule — water here, hydrogen
chloride elsewhere — expelled at every junction. Add the
functional groups and the two routes cannot be confused:
addition monomers carry C=C; condensation monomers carry two
reactive end groups (–OH and –COOH here) and no double bond at
all. If the question shows you a –COOH at both ends, the
answer is condensation, whatever else it shows.
:::

:::quick-check

other, water removed at every seam? Can you write the
structural formulae of ethane-1,2-diol and ethanedioic acid
and explain each code in their names? Can you reproduce the

:::
