A [[polymer]] is a giant molecule built by joining huge numbers of
small units together, and once you know what to look for you will
find them everywhere: polymers are large molecules consisting
of many repeating units, and this chapter looks at two
different ways that polymers can be made. Natural polymers run
your body and your
kitchen: proteins are polypeptides, and sugar is built from
polysaccharides — chains you have already met in biology.
Synthetic polymers run everything else: plastic bags, clothing,
water pipes, non-stick pans. This lesson is about the synthetic
kind, and it turns on one idea — a [[polymer]] grows from small
starting molecules called [[monomer]]s, and there are exactly two
ways the joining can happen. **[[addition-polymerisation]]** builds
polymers such as poly(ethene) and PVC: the monomers simply add on,
one after another, and nothing is thrown away. **[[condensation-polymerisation]]**
builds a [[polyester]]: two different monomers
alternate down the chain, and each time a pair joins, a small
molecule is expelled. Everything in this chapter hangs off that
one distinction.

The specification splits this lesson down the same line, and the
split decides which exam paper each half belongs to. Four
statements are for everyone: knowing that an addition polymer is
formed by joining up many small molecules called [[monomer]]s
(4.44); drawing repeat units for the four named polymers
(4.45); deducing a monomer from a repeat unit and back again
(4.46); and explaining the disposal problems (4.47). Three more
statements carry the C suffix — condensation polymerisation
(4.48C), writing a polyester's structural and displayed formula
(4.49C), and biodegradable polyesters (4.50C) — and C-suffixed
content is chemistry-only: it appears on Paper 2C, not on the
double-award papers. If you sit Paper 1C only, the addition half
of this lesson is your whole territory; the condensation half is
flagged as you enter it.

The route through the chapter follows the chemistry. First the
addition story: how a C=C double bond opens out and chains grow,
what a repeat unit is, and how to write the polymerisation
reaction starting from *any* alkene — ethene, propene,
chloroethene and tetrafluoroethene in turn. Then the reverse
skill: given a polymer's structure, work backwards to the monomer
that built it. Then the bill that polymers present to the
environment, because their great strength — inertness — is
exactly what makes their disposal a problem. Finally the
condensation story: a diol and a dicarboxylic acid joining
with water expelled, the [[polyester]] family, and the
biodegradable members that might solve the disposal problem the
addition polymers created.

:::prior-knowledge
Three ideas load this lesson. First, the alkenes and their C=C
double bond — the reactive site every addition polymer grows
from — including the bromine-water test that spotlights that
bond (u4:c25). Second, cracking, the source of the small alkene
molecules that feed polymer production (u4:c23). Third, the
ester group –COO– and the condensation reaction that builds it
(u4:c28): the condensation half of this lesson reuses that exact
group, just at every junction of a chain instead of once in a
small molecule. If the alkenes feel shaky, repair them first —
every polymer in the addition half is an alkene in disguise.
:::

:::exam-hint
Statement 4.44 says *know* — a recall item — while 4.45 and 4.46
say *understand*, which means drawn structures, not sentences.
Notice also how the C suffix sorts the paper route: 4.48C,
4.49C and 4.50C are the only statements in this chapter that
name condensation chemistry, so if the question number carries
a C, the answer lives in the second half of this lesson. Asking
yourself "which paper is this on?" before revising is a cheap
way to prioritise.
:::

:::misconception
"Polymers are a synthetic invention — chemistry made them and
chemistry owns them." Chemistry certainly perfected them, but
nature got there first and at enormous scale: the protein
chains in your hair and muscles are polypeptides, and starch
is a polysaccharide. Natural and synthetic polymers are built
on the same idea — many repeating units joined into one giant
molecule — and the only thing this lesson adds is the two
industrial routes for doing the joining.
:::
