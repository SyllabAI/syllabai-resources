Tetrafluoroethene completes the set: it is another molecule
derived from ethene, this time with all four hydrogen atoms
replaced by fluorine, giving CF₂=CF₂. Polymerising it gives
[[ptfe]] — poly(tetrafluoroethene) — more commonly known as
Teflon. The reaction is exactly the same shape as the
poly(ethene) equation, and if you look at the two side by side
you can see it is the same reaction except that each H has been
replaced by an F:

```
   F    F               F    F
   |    |               |    |
n  C == C     →       [  C - C  ]n
   |    |               |    |
   F    F               F    F
(tetrafluoroethene)  (poly(tetrafluoroethene) repeat unit)
```

The fluorine atoms are not decoration — they are the reason
PTFE is worth its place on the specification. Carbon–fluorine
bonds are very strong, and a chain sheathed in them is
extremely unreactive. That unreactivity is the property behind
every use: PTFE is the non-stick coating on pots and pans,
where nothing bonds to the surface and nothing burns onto it,
and it lines containers for corrosive chemicals precisely
because the chemicals cannot attack it. The same inertness
returns in the disposal section (4.47) — remember it when the
question asks why polymers are a problem in landfill.

:::exam-hint
4.45 asks for the repeat unit, so learn the four named
polymers as one family of drawings: H/H for poly(ethene), CH₃/H
for poly(propene), Cl/H for poly(chloroethene), F/F for PTFE.
The F/F case is the easiest to draw and the easiest to forget
— two fluorines on each carbon, nothing else. If asked for the
[[monomer]] of PTFE, reverse the routine: put the C=C double
bond back between the two carbons of the repeat unit and you
have tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂.
:::

:::misconception
"Fluorine is ferociously reactive, so a fluorine-containing
polymer must be too." The reactivity of the element and the
reactivity of the bond are different questions. Fluorine gas
attacks almost everything, but once a C–F bond has formed it
is one of the strongest single bonds carbon makes, and a chain
of them is what makes PTFE slippery and chemically lazy. The
polymer's inertness is a property of its strong bonds, not an
echo of its elements.
:::
