Recycling plastics matters for two reasons at once: to save raw
materials, and because it takes a very long time for addition
polymers like poly(ethene) and poly(chloroethene) to break down
in the environment. The reason is the chemistry you have just
learned. Their chains are built from strong covalent bonds,
making them essentially inert at ordinary temperatures, and
they are [[non-biodegradable]] — they cannot be broken down by
bacteria in the environment. The very stability that makes a
water pipe last decades makes a plastic bag last
geological-looking timescales, and the same [[polymer]] chains
that refused to react with corrosive chemicals (s04) refuse
equally to react with the enzymes of decay.

One solution is to bury the problem: [[landfill]] sites are
basically just big holes in the ground where plastic waste
will remain unchanged for thousands of years. The other
widely used solution is to burn it: some countries, including
Denmark and Japan, [[incineration]] plastics to
tackle the disposal problem at scale. Burning releases a lot
of heat energy, which can be used to generate electricity —
waste as a fuel. However, there are problems associated with
this: carbon dioxide is produced, which contributes to global
warming, and toxic gases are also released, including carbon
monoxide and hydrogen chloride. The hydrogen chloride comes
from chlorine-bearing polymers — PVC is the everyday example
you met in the previous section — so the cleaner the flame,
the cleaner the chemistry, and chlorine-containing plastics
are the worst offenders in an incinerator.

:::key-point
There are advantages and disadvantages to using either method
to dispose of plastics, and the exam wants both sides of both
columns. [[landfill]]: no greenhouse gases or toxic gases
produced from the plastics, and it is cheap — but the sites
are ugly, smelly and noisy, nobody wants to live next to one,
they use large areas of land, and the waste will be there for
thousands of years. [[incineration]]: it requires little
space and can produce heat for local homes and offices or
electricity — but the plant is expensive to build and
maintain, burning produces greenhouse gases, it releases
toxic gases, and the ash produced must still be disposed of
in landfill sites.
:::

Read that table as the chemistry argument it is. Incineration
converts one waste problem (space) into an emissions problem
(CO₂ plus toxic gases), and even its ash — the incombustible
residue — ends up back in landfill, only concentrated. Neither
route destroys the polymer problem, because neither route
touches the property that causes it: the strong covalent
bonds that make addition polymers inert. That is the honest
conclusion of 4.47, and it is why the condensation polymers
later in this lesson — and their biodegradable cousins in
particular — are worth the chemistry they are getting.

:::exam-hint
Statement 4.47 asks you to *explain* the disposal problems,
"including" two named items: the inertness and inability to
biodegrade, and the production of toxic gases when they are
burned. Both words in the first item carry marks — strong
covalent bonds explain the inertness, and the inertness is why
bacteria cannot break them down. For the second item, name the
gases (carbon monoxide and hydrogen chloride) rather than just
saying "toxic gases". "Include" means the list is not
exhaustive — landfill space and ash disposal are fair extra
marks.
:::

:::misconception
"Plastic eventually rots away like paper or food — it just
takes a bit longer." Biodegrading is not slow rotting; it is
bacteria digesting a material, and bacteria can only digest
what their enzymes can attack. Paper is cellulose, a natural
polymer that evolved alongside the organisms that eat it.
Addition polymers are new, inert, and built from strong
covalent bonds nothing in a bacterium's toolkit can cut — so
they do not rot slowly, they simply persist unchanged.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you explain, in terms of bonds, why addition polymers are
[[non-biodegradable]]? Can you give one advantage and one
disadvantage each for [[landfill]] and [[incineration]]? Can
you name the two toxic gases released when plastics burn and
say which everyday polymer supplies the chlorine? If yes, the
disposal bill is understood — and the condensation half of
this lesson, where a partial answer to the problem is being

:::
