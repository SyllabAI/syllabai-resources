Ethene is the smallest alkene and the smallest hydrocarbon that
carries a carbon–carbon double bond, and it is produced by
cracking longer fractions (u4:c23). That double bond is the whole
story of this section. You can represent an ethene molecule as
the molecular formula C₂H₄, as the displayed formula with all
four bonds drawn, or as a ball-and-stick model — three views of
the same pair of carbon atoms joined by two pairs of shared
electrons.

Under the right conditions, molecules carrying C=C bonds join
together into very long chains. Part of each double bond breaks
to become a single bond, and the electrons set free are used to
grip the next molecule in line. Heat, high pressure and an
[[initiator]] start the process, and the molecules add on and on:
lots of little molecules called [[monomer]]s join into one big
molecule called a [[polymer]]. Nothing else is formed and
nothing is lost — the monomers simply add onto each other — and
that is why the process is called [[addition-polymerisation]].
With ethene as the monomer the product is [[polyethene]], more
usually called polythene. The chains are not all the same
length: a typical chain carries somewhere between about 4000 and
40 000 carbon atoms, all stitched together by the same reaction.

:::key-point
An [[initiator]] is used to start the process. You mustn't call
it a catalyst because it is consumed in the reaction — bits of
it end up bonded onto the ends of every chain. Catalysts, like
the concentrated sulfuric acid in esterification (u4:c28), come
out unchanged; an initiator does not.
:::

For exam purposes the reaction is written with displayed
formulae. The monomer's double bond opens out into a single
bond, the two freed bonds become continuation bonds, and the
whole arrangement sits inside square brackets with a subscript
n:

```
   H  H                H  H
   |  |                |  |
n  C=C      →        [ C-C ]          (continued on both sides)
   |  |                |  |
   H  H                H  H
```

The structure inside the brackets is called the [[repeat-unit]],
and n is simply a large but variable number — the repeat unit
repeats many times down the chain. Draw the bonds on the two
sides of the bracket extending *outside* it: those are the
continuation bonds that connect each unit to the next ones in
the chain. The equation reads "n ethene molecules give one
poly(ethene) molecule", and it is balanced in the most
satisfying way — n C₂H₄ on the left, (C₂H₄)ₙ on the right, every
atom accounted for because nothing was lost.

:::key-point
Although the polymer is called poly(ethene), it is actually an alkane-like structure: the polymer is [[saturated]] and will
not decolourise bromine water. The double bond each monomer
brought to the reaction is spent in the joining — the polymer
chain carries only single bonds, so the alkene test that works
on ethene (u4:c25) is silent on poly(ethene).
:::

Poly(ethene) comes in two grades. Low-density poly(ethene),
LDPE, is mainly used as a thin film for polythene bags: very
flexible, not very strong. High-density poly(ethene), HDPE, is
used where greater strength and rigidity are needed — plastic
bottles such as milk bottles. The recycling symbols on the
bottom of packaging tell you which you are holding: the letters
HDPE next to the recycling mark mean high-density
poly(ethene), and any other letters mean some other polymer
entirely. A curiosity worth knowing from the extension of the
chain-ends story: polymer chains do not end tidily — bits of
the initiator stay bonded on at either end, but for
International GCSE you never need to draw them.

:::exam-hint
When you draw an addition polymerisation reaction, never change
anything except opening out the double bond and drawing the
continuation bonds on each side. The two classic lost marks:
forgetting the subscript n after the bracket, and hiding the
continuation bonds inside the brackets instead of letting them
cross the bracket walls.
:::

:::misconception
"Poly(ethene) has C=C bonds because it is made from ethene."
The joining consumes every double bond — that is the addition.
The polymer is [[saturated]], closer in character to an alkane
than to the alkene it came from, which is exactly why bromine
water stays orange in it. If a question shows you a polymer
fragment that still contains C=C, you are looking at the
monomer or at an unfinished drawing, not at a correct repeat
unit.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state what an [[initiator]] does and why it is not a
catalyst? Can you draw the [[polyethene]] equation with the
[[repeat-unit]] in brackets, continuation bonds crossing the
bracket walls and the subscript n in place? Can you explain why
the polymer will not decolourise bromine water? If yes, you are
ready to run the same reaction on any alkene.
:::
