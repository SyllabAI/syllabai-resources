In an exam you may be handed the structure of a polymer and
asked to deduce what the [[repeat-unit]] is, or what
[[monomer]] it was made from — the skill of 4.46 running the
drawing routine of the last three sections backwards. The
method has two moves. First, find the repeat unit: take any
two adjacent carbon atoms in the main polymer chain, and the
fragment between them (plus whatever hangs off those carbons)
is the repeat unit. Second, derive the monomer: put a double
bond back between those two middle carbon atoms and get rid of
the continuation bonds. Work from the structure you are given,
and do not be distracted by how complicated the attached
groups look — they simply ride along.

:::worked-example
A short length of poly(styrene) is drawn as a carbon chain
carrying alternating hydrogens and C₆H₅ groups. Find the
repeat unit and the monomer.

Step 1 — pick two adjacent carbons in the main chain. Take a
carbon bearing H and its neighbour bearing C₆H₅: the fragment
–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)– is the repeat unit. (Any adjacent pair gives
the same answer — the chain is the same unit repeated, so
where you start does not matter.)

Step 2 — put the double bond back between those two carbons
and remove the continuation bonds: C₆H₅CH=CH₂, which is
styrene. The C₆H₅ group is complicated to draw, but you never
need to unpack it — it came through the reaction unchanged,
and it goes back into the monomer unchanged.

Check by running the forward routine: styrene with the C=C
opened out, continuation bonds added, bracketed with n — the
poly(styrene) structure returns.
:::

The same walk answers the reverse question with equal speed:
given a monomer, open its double bond and bracket the result,
and you have the polymer. Monomer to polymer and polymer to
monomer are one routine read in two directions, and 4.46 is
satisfied by both. The benzene-ring case is the examiners'
favourite because it looks harder than it is — the ring is
just a passenger, exactly as the CH₃, Cl and F were on the
polymers of the previous sections.

:::exam-hint
The most common error in this question type is anchoring the
repeat unit on the wrong pair of carbons — picking two carbons
that are *not* adjacent, or spanning across the unit
boundary. If your derived monomer does not have its double
bond between two carbons that each carried chain bonds, your
pair was wrong: slide along by one carbon and try again. A
correct answer always re-polymerises cleanly by the forward
routine.
:::

:::misconception
"There is exactly one correct place to cut the chain, and if
you start somewhere else you get a different monomer." The
repeat unit repeats — that is the definition — so every
adjacent pair of chain carbons yields the same fragment. What
*is* fixed is the double bond's final position: it must sit
between the two carbons that were joined through the chain.
Cut anywhere, but always rebuild the C=C across the cut.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the two moves of the backwards routine in one
sentence each? Can you take a drawn repeat unit and restore
its [[monomer]] by re-forming the double bond? Can you explain
why any two adjacent chain carbons give the same
[[repeat-unit]]? If yes, both directions of 4.46 are yours,
and the next section turns from how polymers are made to what
happens when we are finished with them.
:::
