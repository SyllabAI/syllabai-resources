The book opens the chapter with a claim worth pausing on: the
**[[periodic-table]]** shows *all the elements in the universe* and is one
of the most important tools a chemist has. Not one of the most important
tools in chemistry — one of the most important tools, full stop. The
reason is the word the book underlines twice: **arrangement**. Because the
elements are laid out in a fixed, meaningful order, the table lets you
understand **trends in properties** and **make predictions** — including
predictions about elements nobody had ever seen.

The prediction power is not hypothetical. The modern Periodic Table was
first presented in **1869** by the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev**,
and his table is famous precisely because it left gaps for elements that
had not been discovered yet and correctly predicted their properties. This
chapter explores some of the features of that table: how the elements are
ordered, what the **groups** and **periods** mean, how the table lets you
count protons, neutrons and electrons, and how it answers the question
"metal or non-metal?".

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- say how the elements are arranged in the Periodic Table — **in order of
  atomic number**, in **groups** (columns) and **periods** (rows);
- deduce the **electronic configurations of the first 20 elements** from
  their positions in the table;
- classify an element as a **metal or a non-metal**, both from its
  position and from **electrical conductivity** and the **acid-base
  character of its oxides**;
- explain why elements in the **same group** have **similar chemical
  properties**, and why the **noble gases** barely react at all.

:::prior-knowledge
From Lesson u1:c03 you know the counting recipe — protons from the atomic
number, electrons = protons, neutrons = mass number − atomic number — and
you know electrons sit in **shells** (energy levels) around the nucleus.
From Lesson u1:c02 you know that the 118 elements are the building blocks
of everything. This chapter finally explains how that list of elements is
*organised* — and why the organisation is not arbitrary.
:::

:::key-point
The Periodic Table shows all the elements in the universe, arranged so
that trends in properties can be understood and predictions made. The
modern form was first presented in 1869 by Dmitri Mendeleev. This
chapter: arrangement (groups and periods), electronic configurations,
and the metal / non-metal split.
:::

:::misconception
"The Periodic Table is just a list of elements in alphabetical order, or
in the order they were discovered." No — it is neither. The table is
arranged **in order of atomic number** (the number of protons), which is
exactly why its patterns are meaningful: neighbouring elements differ by
one proton, and the columns line up elements whose atoms share the same
number of outer-shell electrons.
:::

:::exam-hint
"State how elements are arranged in the Periodic Table" wants the
arrangement, not the history: **in order of (increasing) atomic number,
in groups (columns) and periods (rows)**. Naming Mendeleev or 1869 earns
nothing on that question — save the history for "describe the
development of the Periodic Table" style questions if they ever appear.
:::
