Section 15 said molecular substances melt and boil easily. This section
refines the rule into a trend you can predict — the trend spec 1.48 is built
on — using the halogens as its cleanest evidence.

The halogens (Group 7) all have simple molecular structures: [[diatomic]]
molecules with intermolecular forces between them. Line up the group and read
their numbers:

| Halogen | Formula | Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fluorine | F₂ | 38 | −220 | −188 |
| chlorine | Cl₂ | 71 | −101 | −34 |
| bromine | Br₂ | 160 | −7 | 59 |
| iodine | I₂ | 254 | 114 | 184 |

The trend is unmissable: **as the relative molecular mass increases, the
melting points and boiling points increase.** Fluorine is a gas at room
temperature, chlorine a gas just above it, bromine a liquid, iodine a grey
solid — the same room, four states, ordered by molecular mass alone. (Note
the iodine data point in particular: a molecular substance that is a *solid*
at room temperature — "molecular" does not have to mean "gas".)

The *why* is an energy argument you already own. To melt or boil one of these
substances you must break [[intermolecular-forces]] — no covalent bonds. Higher
boiling points mean more energy must be put in, which means the
**intermolecular forces must be getting stronger** as the relative molecular
mass rises. Bigger molecules attract their neighbours more strongly; bigger
Mᵣ, stickier neighbours, higher temperatures needed to pull them apart. The
same trend walks up the alkane series — methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane
(C₃H₈) boil at steadily higher temperatures as Mᵣ grows — and you will meet
that family properly in Chapter 23 (u4:c23).

Two pieces of fine print make your answer examiner-proof. First, the
vocabulary: a covalent compound with a simple molecular structure is called a
**covalent molecular compound** — the pairing of terms that papers use when
they want precision. Second, the honesty: the trend is stated "in general"
because it strictly holds only within families of similar substances — the
halogens, the alkanes. Across the whole of chemistry it leaks: water (Mᵣ 18)
boils at 100 °C while ethane (Mᵣ 30) boils at −89 °C, and ammonia (Mᵣ 17)
beats phosphine (Mᵣ 34) by over 120 degrees. The anomalies come from
especially strong intermolecular attractions in water and ammonia — a hint of
hydrogen bonding from section 14. Trend first, exceptions second: that is
the order the marks are written in.

:::key-point
A tetrahedron is a triangular-based pyramid, and a **tetrahedral arrangement**
puts one atom at the centre with four atoms at the corners. You will meet the
shape again built in three dimensions when the chapter reaches diamond — and
when a structure diagram shows a carbon atom apparently bonded to only two
others, read the caption's caution: diagrams of giant structures show a small
slice of something that continues in all directions, and every atom drawn is
bonded to four neighbours in the full structure. Diagram edges are not
chemistry.
:::

:::maths-skills
Reading the halogen table is a data-handling skill papers test directly.
Describe the trend with **both variables named and both directions fixed**:
"as relative molecular mass increases from 38 (F₂) to 254 (I₂), the melting
point increases from −220 °C to 114 °C". Quote *both* ends of the range —
an answer with numbers from the table outscores a bare "it increases". And
watch the negative values: iodine's melting point (114 °C) is *higher* than
fluorine's (−220 °C) even though 114 < 220 in absolute terms; on a
temperature scale, less negative means higher. Papers deliberately include
negative melting points to test exactly this reading.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why the boiling point increases down Group 7" — the full-mark chain
has three links and they must all appear: (1) the molecules are held together
by intermolecular forces; (2) as relative molecular mass increases the
intermolecular forces become **stronger**; (3) so **more energy** is needed
to break them, giving higher boiling points. The classic wrong answer names
covalent bonds — "bigger molecules have stronger covalent bonds, so they are
harder to separate". Zero marks: covalent bonds are not broken on boiling,
and stronger covalent bonds would change nothing about the boiling point at
all.
:::

:::misconception
"Higher melting and boiling points mean the covalent bonds inside the
molecules are getting stronger." The property being measured tests the forces
*between* molecules, not the bonds *within* them — I₂'s covalent I–I bond is
no stronger than F₂'s F–F bond to any relevant degree, yet iodine melts 334
degrees higher. What grows down the group is the intermolecular attraction,
because heavier molecules with more electrons attract their neighbours more
strongly. If your explanation of a melting-point trend names covalent bonds,
you have answered about the wrong force — switch to intermolecular forces and
the marks reappear.
:::
