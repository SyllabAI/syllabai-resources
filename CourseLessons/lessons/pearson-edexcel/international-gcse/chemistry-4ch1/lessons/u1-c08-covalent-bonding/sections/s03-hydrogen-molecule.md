The simplest covalent substance on the course is hydrogen gas, and it is the
best place to see the shared pair in action. Covalent bonds are shown using a
**[[dot-and-cross-diagram]]** — and for hydrogen, the diagram is almost
suspiciously simple. Each hydrogen atom has one electron. Two hydrogen atoms
each put their single electron into a shared pair, and that pair sits between
the two nuclei. Both nuclei pull on the pair; the pair holds on to both atoms.
Hydrogen atoms therefore form **[[molecule]]s with the formula H₂** — two atoms
joined by one covalent bond — and the bond between them is very strong.

When you draw the diagram yourself, show each atom's single electron — one as
a dot, one as a cross — overlapping in the middle so the shared pair is
unmistakable. Around each atom, the pair completes a two-electron shell: the
same electron count as a helium atom. That is the covalent version of reaching
a noble gas arrangement, and it is the reason H₂ forms at all.

Two atoms per molecule is common enough to have its own name: molecules that
contain two atoms are called **[[diatomic]]**. Hydrogen is diatomic, and so are
the oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine and hydrogen-chloride molecules you will draw
in the next few sections. Molecules in general contain a fixed number of atoms
joined by covalent bonds — two for diatomic gases, three for water, five for
methane, and at the extreme, thousands of atoms for giant biological molecules
such as proteins and DNA. The word "fixed" matters: a real molecule always
contains exactly its own count of atoms, which is what lets you write a
formula like H₂ or CH₄ at all.

:::key-point
A molecule is a **fixed** number of atoms joined by covalent bonds. H₂ is
always two atoms; H₂O is always three. That fixed count is why molecular
substances have clean chemical formulae, and it is also the feature that
separates molecules from the giant structures you meet at the end of this
lesson, where no such fixed count exists.
:::

:::exam-hint
If a question asks you to "draw a dot-and-cross diagram for hydrogen", draw
the two overlapping circles with **one dot and one cross in the overlap** and
label the nuclei. Do not draw extra shells — hydrogen has only one shell, and
padding it out with invented electrons loses the mark. For "outer shell
electrons only" questions, hydrogen's entire configuration *is* its outer
shell, so a single circle per atom is the complete answer.
:::

:::misconception
"When two hydrogen atoms bond, they merge into one helium atom." They do not —
sharing electrons changes nothing about the nuclei. Each atom in the molecule
still carries exactly one proton and is still hydrogen; only the *electron
arrangement* is shared. The test of what an atom is, is the number of protons
in its nucleus, and that never changes in covalent bonding. You will see this
misconception dismantled properly in the next section — keep it in mind every
time a diagram makes a bonded atom look like a noble gas.
:::
