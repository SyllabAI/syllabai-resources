Three molecules every candidate must be able to draw: methane, ammonia and
water. They are a matched set — a central non-metal atom surrounded by
hydrogen atoms — and drawn one after another they reveal the pattern that
connects the Periodic Table to molecular formulae.

**Methane, CH₄.** A carbon atom has 4 electrons in its outer shell, so it
shares one electron with each of 4 hydrogen atoms: four shared pairs, four
covalent bonds. Count the audit after bonding — the carbon's outer shell
holds 8 (4 of its own plus its share of each pair), and each hydrogen holds
2. The formula CH₄ records the outcome: one carbon bonded to four hydrogens.

**Ammonia, NH₃.** A nitrogen atom has 5 electrons in its outer shell — three
short of 8 — so it shares three of its electrons, one with each of 3 hydrogen
atoms. Three covalent bonds form, and the nitrogen keeps its remaining two
outer electrons as an unshared pair of its own (you will give those a name in
section 10). The audit: N on 8, each H on 2. The formula is NH₃ — three
hydrogens, not four, and the count explains why.

**Water, H₂O.** An oxygen atom has 6 electrons in its outer shell — two short
— so it shares two of its electrons, one with each of 2 hydrogen atoms. Two
covalent bonds, two remaining unshared pairs on the oxygen. The audit: O on
8, each H on 2. The formula H₂O falls straight out of the counting.

Now stand back and read the pattern across the three: carbon (Group 4) formed
**4** bonds, nitrogen (Group 5) formed **3**, oxygen (Group 6) formed **2** —
and chlorine in the last section (Group 7) formed **1**. The number of bonds
a non-metal atom forms is **8 minus its group number** (for Groups 4 to 7),
because each atom bonds until its outer shell reaches 8. That one sentence
generates every formula in this section, and the dot-and-cross diagrams are
just the proof on paper.

:::worked-example
**Draw the dot-and-cross diagram for water, showing outer shells only.**
(1) Oxygen brings 6 outer electrons; hydrogen brings 1 each. (2) Place the
oxygen's electrons around it in pairs; share one oxygen electron with each
hydrogen's single electron — two shared pairs, each shown as a dot+cross
overlap. (3) Draw the oxygen's remaining 4 outer electrons as two unshared
pairs. (4) Audit: O counts 8; each H counts 2. Four marks on the page in
under a minute — and the same four steps draw methane and ammonia with only
the counts changed.
:::

:::exam-hint
The "8 − group number = bonds formed" shortcut earns formulae fast, but in
explain questions the marks live in the *reasoning*: the atom shares until its
outer shell holds 8 (2 for hydrogen). Write the counting sentence, not just
the number. Typical full-mark answer for ammonia: "nitrogen has 5 outer-shell
electrons and needs 3 more to reach 8, so it forms 3 covalent bonds, one with
each of three hydrogen atoms." The same sentence skeleton scores on any
central atom the course can throw at you.
:::

:::misconception
"An atom with 8 outer electrons after bonding must have formed 8 bonds."
Confusing the *target count* with the *number of bonds* is the classic error
on this set. Each covalent bond contributes just one new electron to an
atom's count (its share of the pair), so an atom starting with 5 outer
electrons forms 8 − 5 = 3 bonds — not 8. Audit every diagram by counting, not
by vibes: carbon 4 + 4 bonds, nitrogen 5 + 3, oxygen 6 + 2, chlorine 7 + 1.
The start count is what varies; the target is always 2 or 8.
:::
