Why does hydrogen stop at H₂ and never form H₃? The answer is the counting
rule that organises almost everything in this chapter. In H₂, each hydrogen
atom has only one electron to share, so it can form **only one covalent bond**.
Once that single shared pair is in place, each atom's outer shell holds two
electrons — the same count as a helium atom, so each atom is
[[isoelectronic]] with a noble gas. There is nothing left to share, so no
third atom can join: the molecule is complete.

For every element except hydrogen, the target count is **eight** outer-shell
electrons. In virtually all the molecules you will meet at this level,
electrons are shared so that each hydrogen atom ends up with 2 electrons in
its outer shell and every other atom ends up with 8. Some books of mnemonics
call this the "octet rule" — the rule of eight. The counting convention that
makes it work is crucial: **shared electrons count as belonging to the outer
shells of both atoms at once.** One shared pair delivers one new electron to
each atom's count. Draw the overlap, count each atom's electrons (its own plus
its share of every pair), and check the totals: 2 for hydrogen, 8 for the rest.

Does reaching a noble-gas electron count mean the atom *becomes* a noble gas?
It does not — and this check matters. Sharing electrons changes nothing in the
nucleus. The number of protons is untouched, and it is the proton count that
defines what an atom is. A hydrogen atom in H₂ is still hydrogen even though
it is isoelectronic with helium; a chlorine atom sharing a pair in HCl is
still chlorine even though its outer-shell count matches argon. Isoelectronic
is about electron *counts*, never about element *identity*.

The rule has a structure to it as well as a number. When one atom sits in the
middle with others attached to it — as in methane, ammonia or water — the
**outer** atoms virtually always end up with 8 outer electrons (or 2 if they
are hydrogen). It is genuinely hard to find an exception. The **central** atom
is a different story: a few molecules exist where it ends up with more or
fewer than 8, and you will meet the famous examples in section 14.

:::key-point
Stability is an energy statement. When chemists say something is "more
stable", they usually mean it has **less energy** — the lower the energy, the
more stable. Hold a book in the air and let go: it drops to the floor, a
lower-energy position, and stays there. Chemical changes happen for the same
reason. **When bonds form, energy is given out**, so the bonded substance has
less energy than the separate atoms and is more stable. That is why bonds
form at all — and why you must supply energy to break them again.
:::

:::exam-hint
The 2-or-8 count is the fastest self-check in the whole topic. After drawing
any dot-and-cross diagram, count every atom's outer shell (own electrons +
shared electrons). If an outer hydrogen is not on 2, or an outer non-hydrogen
is not on 8, the diagram is wrong — fix it before moving on. Examiners award
the mark for correct sharing, and this ten-second audit catches nearly every
slip: missed pairs, doubled pairs, electrons dropped while copying from your
own working.
:::

:::misconception
"An atom bonded covalently has turned into a noble gas — hydrogen in H₂ *is*
helium." Electron counts can match while the atoms stay utterly different.
Identity is set by protons, and sharing electrons moves no protons. The
correct phrasing for the exam is that the atoms are **isoelectronic with** a
noble gas — the same number of electrons — never that they become it. Watch
also for the mirror-image error: claiming a bonded atom "has a full outer
shell so it is unreactive like a noble gas". Reactivity belongs to the
molecule as a whole, not to a noble-gas costume worn by one atom.
:::
