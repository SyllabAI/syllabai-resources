Two identical atoms can share with each other, and chlorine shows how. A
chlorine atom has 7 electrons in its outer shell — one short of a full octet.
Put two chlorine atoms together and each shares **one** of its electrons with
the other: the shared pair contributes one electron to each atom's count, and
both atoms reach 8 in their outer shells. The result is the Cl₂ molecule — two
chlorine atoms joined by one covalent bond, each atom's outer shell complete.

The diagram has a pleasing symmetry: each atom contributes one electron (one
dot from the left atom, one cross from the right), the pair sits in the
overlap, and each atom also shows its other six outer electrons as its own.
Because the two atoms are identical, there is no question of which atom
"owns" the pair — the sharing is exactly equal, which is why chlorine gas is
made of Cl₂ molecules rather than anything charged. Chlorine is [[diatomic]]
for the same reason hydrogen is: one shared pair completes each atom's outer
shell, and nothing remains to bond with a third atom.

The same diagram can be drawn in more than one way — with the two atoms as
overlapping circles, or with the shared pair written between the chemical
symbols. They are the same chemistry; pick whichever the question asks for.
What never changes is the bookkeeping: 7 + 1 shared = 8, on both sides,
every time.

Chlorine's bonding pattern here generalises to the whole of Group 7: fluorine,
bromine and iodine all have 7 outer electrons and all form the same
single-bonded diatomic molecules — F₂, Br₂, I₂. The group number fixed the
bond count, exactly as the periodic-table work in Chapter 4 (u1:c04) predicts.
Hold on to that prediction skill: group number → outer electrons → bonds
formed → formula of the molecule. The next section applies it to three
molecules at once.

:::exam-hint
"Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for chlorine" is the standard halogen-diagram
question, and the mark scheme checks three things: (1) 7 outer electrons
shown on each atom *before* sharing; (2) exactly one shared pair in the
overlap, one dot + one cross; (3) each atom counting 8 after sharing. Draw
all seven outer electrons per atom — forgetting the six non-bonding ones is
the single most common slip. And if the question says "outer shell electrons
only", do not draw the [2, 8] inner shells at all.
:::

:::misconception
"Two chlorine atoms each share one electron, so the bond contains one
electron." The sharing is of a **pair**: one electron from each atom makes a
two-electron shared pair, and that pair is the bond. Counting the bond as a
single electron wrecks the audit — each atom would land on 7½, which is
nonsense. The safe habit from section 4 applies: after drawing, count each
atom's outer shell in whole electrons (7 own + 1 from the shared pair's other
member = 8) and the error cannot survive.
:::
