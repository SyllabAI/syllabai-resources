Atoms are not limited to sharing one pair between them. When sharing one
pair is not enough to complete both outer shells, they share **two** or even
**three** pairs — and these multiple bonds are among the most examined
diagrams on the course.

**The double bond: oxygen, O₂.** An oxygen atom has 6 electrons in its outer
shell — two short of 8. If two oxygen atoms each shared just one electron,
both would still be one short. So they share **two electrons each**, giving
**two shared pairs** between the atoms: a **[[double-bond]]** (or double
covalent bond). Count the audit: each oxygen counts 6 own + 2 from its share
of the two pairs = 8. Two shared pairs, four electrons, one very strong bond
— and O₂ is the molecule that runs your respiration on exactly this
arrangement.

**The double bond in carbon dioxide, CO₂.** A carbon atom has 4 outer
electrons; an oxygen atom has 6. Each oxygen shares two electrons with the
carbon, and the carbon shares two with each oxygen: **two double bonds**, one
from the carbon to each oxygen. Every atom lands on 8 — carbon on 8 via its
two double bonds, each oxygen on 8 via its one. The line structure is O=C=O,
two double bonds in a row; the dot-and-cross shows the four shared pairs
explicitly, two per bond.

**The triple bond: nitrogen, N₂.** A nitrogen atom has 5 outer electrons —
three short. Two nitrogen atoms each share **three** electrons, giving three
shared pairs: a **[[triple-bond]]**. Each atom counts 5 + 3 = 8. The triple
bond in N₂ is **very strong and needs a lot of energy to break**, and that
single sentence of structure explains a headline chemical fact: **nitrogen gas
is relatively unreactive**. The air is four-fifths nitrogen, and it stays
chemically sleepy precisely because its molecules are held together by three
shared pairs apiece. Structure → strength → unreactivity: one line of
reasoning, three marking points.

**The double bond in ethene, C₂H₄.** Ethene is rather like ethane except
that each carbon has only **two** hydrogen atoms attached, and the two
carbons make up the difference with a **double bond between them**. Each
carbon: two C–H single bonds plus one C=C double bond — 2 + 2 shared pairs
= 8. You will meet ethene again as the starting material for polymers
(u4:c25); the bonding you would draw for it here is the same diagram.

:::worked-example
**Draw the dot-and-cross diagram for CO₂, outer electrons only.** (1) Carbon
brings 4 outer electrons; each oxygen brings 6. (2) Between C and each O,
place **two** shared pairs (two dot–cross overlaps per bond) — that is
carbons' 4 electrons all shared, two to each oxygen. (3) Draw each oxygen's
remaining 4 electrons as two lone pairs. (4) Audit: C on 8 (its share of four
pairs), each O on 8 (its share of two pairs plus its own four). If any atom
misses 8, the bond order — not the atom — is what you mis-set.
:::

:::exam-hint
Ethane and ethene are **completely different compounds** — a single letter
apart in their names, worlds apart in their bonding (seven single bonds
versus six bonds including a C=C double). In every organic-naming question,
read the name letter by letter before drawing anything: -ane means single
bonds throughout; -ene means a C=C double bond is present. Papers love this
pair precisely because fast readers draw the wrong molecule. The same care
applies to the formulae: C₂H₆ and C₂H₄ differ by two hydrogens, and those two
hydrogens are the double bond's other face.
:::

:::misconception
"A double bond is two separate single bonds drawn side by side, so the atoms
share with each other twice as many *times*." A double bond is **two shared
pairs between the same two atoms** — one bond made of four shared electrons,
not two independent bonds to two different atoms. The audit keeps it honest:
O₂'s oxygens each count 8 total, not 8 twice. The mirror error appears in
counts: "a double bond counts as two bonds for the atom's 8" is right, but
"a double bond gives each atom four extra electrons" is wrong — each atom
gains only its share, two electrons, from either a double bond's two pairs
(two electrons each) or two single bonds. Count electrons, not bond labels.
:::
