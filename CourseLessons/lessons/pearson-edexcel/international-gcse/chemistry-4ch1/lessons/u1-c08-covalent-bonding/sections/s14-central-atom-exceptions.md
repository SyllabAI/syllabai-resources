The 2-or-8 rule is strong but not universal, and the exceptions are
examinable. The reliable part first: **outer atoms** in a molecule will
virtually always finish with 8 outer electrons (or 2 for hydrogen). The
flexible part: a few **central atoms** finish with more or fewer than 8. Two
molecules cover both directions.

**Fewer than 8: boron trifluoride, BF₃.** Boron is the central atom with the
three fluorines around it. Each fluorine (Group 7) shares 1 electron to reach
8 — that part is untouched. But boron only has **3 electrons in its outer
shell**, and 3 is the maximum it can share. After three B–F bonds, boron's
outer shell holds just **6 electrons**. It cannot do better: an atom cannot
share electrons it does not have. BF₃ is therefore a stable, real molecule
with an incomplete central octet — the count *predicts* it, and no amount of
insisting on "8" will draw it away.

**More than 8: sulfur dioxide, SO₂** (also called sulfur(IV) oxide). Sulfur
sits central with two oxygens around it. Each oxygen (Group 6) needs to share
2 electrons to reach 8, so the sulfur shares 2 electrons with each oxygen —
**two double bonds**. But a sulfur atom starts with 6 outer electrons, and
now it is sharing 4 of them: after bonding it has **10 electrons in its outer
shell**. Ten — past the octet, and perfectly stable. The pattern across both
examples: the *outer* atoms always get their 8; the *central* atom ends up
with whatever its own electron supply and bond demand produce. When you draw,
count the central atom honestly rather than forcing it to 8.

The same page of the chapter also plants a term that the rest of this lesson
depends on. **[[intermolecular-forces]]** — the name is literal:
*inter*-molecular, **between molecules**. Water evaporating and boiling gives
the picture: only the forces *between* the water molecules are broken when
liquid water becomes steam — every molecule's internal covalent bonds ride
along intact. Keep the two scales apart in your head: covalent bonds *inside*
molecules (strong, not broken on boiling), intermolecular forces *between*
molecules (weak, broken on boiling). Sections 15 and 16 turn that separation
into the melting-point story, and spec 1.47 names it directly.

If you continue chemistry beyond International GCSE you will find that
"intermolecular forces" is an umbrella for several distinct mechanisms — you
will meet names such as van der Waals' forces, London dispersion forces and
hydrogen bonds. One of them is already visible in your own kitchen: water has
a special intermolecular force called **hydrogen bonding**, and it gives
water some of its very unusual properties — including the fact that ice, the
solid form, is *less* dense than liquid water, so ice floats. At this level
"intermolecular forces" as a single term is all you need; the plumbing behind
it comes later.

:::exam-hint
Do not force the octet onto central atoms. If your BF₃ diagram shows boron on
6 — three bonds, no lone pairs to add — leave it there: that is the correct
diagram, and "boron has only 3 outer electrons to share" is the explaining
sentence the mark scheme wants. Likewise SO₂'s sulfur sits on 10, and
"sulfur shares 4 electrons so it has 10 in its outer shell" is the matching
point. Auditing an *outer* atom short of 8 means your diagram is wrong;
finding a *central* atom off 8 may simply mean you drew one of this section's
molecules correctly.
:::

:::misconception
"Every atom in every molecule must end with exactly 8 outer electrons, so BF₃
cannot exist and SO₂ must be wrong." The 8 rule describes the overwhelmingly
common case, not a law of chemistry. Boron ends on 6 because it has only 3
electrons to give; sulfur ends on 10 because its bonds demand more sharing
than 8 allows. The invariant that *never* breaks: outer atoms reach 8 (2 for
hydrogen). Draw from the electron counts and let the central number land
where it lands.
:::
