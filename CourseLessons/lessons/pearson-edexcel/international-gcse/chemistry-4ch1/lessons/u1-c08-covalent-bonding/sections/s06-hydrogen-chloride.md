Hydrogen chloride, HCl, is the first [[dot-and-cross-diagram]] with two different
atoms, and it introduces three drawing conventions you will use for the rest
of the course.

The chemistry first. A chlorine atom has 7 electrons in its outer shell. By
sharing one electron with a hydrogen atom, both atoms reach the electron
count of the noble gas nearest to them: the chlorine's outer shell fills to 8
— its configuration becomes [2, 8, 8], the same as an argon atom — and the
hydrogen reaches 2 in its outer shell, the same as helium. One shared pair,
one covalent bond, both atoms satisfied. Note that only the **outer-shell**
electrons of chlorine take part: the inner shells ([2, 8]) are spectators,
buried too deep to do any bonding.

That observation sets up the first convention: in the examples you meet at
International GCSE, **inner electrons never get used in bonding**, so they are
usually left out of bonding diagrams entirely. A dot-and-cross diagram for
HCl drawn the standard way shows just the outer shell of chlorine and the
single shell of hydrogen. But — care — this is a convention, not a licence:
**in an exam, only leave out the inner electrons if the question tells you
to** (or shows you it expects outer shells only). If a question asks for all
electrons, draw all of them. Read the question, then choose the drawing.

The second convention: the same bond can be drawn as a **line between the two
atoms** — H–Cl. The line represents one covalent bond (one shared pair).
These line structures are quick and tidy, and you will use them constantly
for organic molecules later in the course. The third convention follows
directly: **a line structure is not a dot-and-cross diagram**. If the
question says "draw a dot-and-cross diagram", a line drawing scores zero even
though the chemistry in it is correct. The examiner is checking the electron
bookkeeping, so the electrons must be on the page.

:::key-point
The HCl diagram teaches the general recipe for every dot-and-cross you will
draw: (1) find each atom's outer-shell electrons from its group number;
(2) pair up the electrons being shared, one origin per atom in the standard
cases; (3) count each atom's outer shell after sharing — 2 for hydrogen,
8 for the others; (4) show only the shells the question asks for. The recipe
is identical for molecules with two atoms or twenty; only the bookkeeping
grows.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Only outer electrons are involved in bonding" is a statement about the
*chemistry*; "only draw outer electrons" is a statement about the *question*.
Keep the two apart. The safe reading habit: look for the phrase "outer shell
electrons only" before you draw anything. If it is there, draw shells-in;
if it is not, draw the full configurations. Half-marks are lost to this
reading slip every session — the diagram is chemically right but answers a
question that was not asked.
:::

:::misconception
"The H–Cl line drawing and the dot-and-cross diagram are the same thing drawn
two ways, so either answers a dot-and-cross question." They carry different
information, and examiners treat them as different answer types. The line
diagram records *that* a bond exists; the dot-and-cross records *where every
outer electron sits and where each came from*. When the question names
dot-and-cross, the electron bookkeeping is the assessed skill — a line
drawing hides exactly what is being marked. Draw lines only when the question
allows or asks for a line structure.
:::
