Molecules contain fixed numbers of atoms joined by **strong covalent bonds**
— but that is only the inside story. Look closely at a glass of liquid water:
there are individual water molecules, each with its H and O atoms locked
together covalently. Yet the water sits in the glass rather than flying apart
as gas, so something must be holding the molecules *to each other*. That
something is the [[intermolecular-forces]] from section 14 — weak attractions
between neighbouring molecules.

The scale comparison is the whole of this section in one sentence:
**intermolecular forces are much, much weaker than covalent bonds.** Strong
inside each molecule; weak between neighbouring molecules. A substance made
of molecules with intermolecular forces between them has a **[[simple-molecular-structure]]** — and virtually every covalent compound you have met
in this lesson has one: H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, C₂H₄ — exactly the molecules you
drew dot-and-cross diagrams for.

Now the consequence that examiners ask about every year. When you boil
water, **only the weak intermolecular forces break — the covalent bonds do
not.** Steam is still H₂O: the same bent molecules, same two O–H covalent
bonds per molecule, same electron-pair sharing. All that has changed is the
separation — the molecules have torn free of one another, because it takes
only a little energy to snap the weak attractions between them. Nobody has
split a single O–H bond, which is why boiling is a physical change and why
the steam condenses straight back to water when it cools: covalent bonds were
never touched, so nothing chemical ever happened.

Flip the same reasoning to the melting and boiling points themselves.
Because so little energy is needed to break the weak intermolecular forces,
**substances with simple molecular structures tend to be gases, liquids, or
solids with low melting points and low boiling points.** Methane boils at a
frigid −162 °C; carbon dioxide sublimes at −78 °C; even room-temperature
liquids like ethanol boil well under 200 °C. The covalent bonds inside the
molecules are as strong as ever — they are simply *not the forces being
tested* when the substance melts or boils. Keep the two force types labelled
in your answer and the explanation writes itself.

:::key-point
The single most examined distinction in this chapter: when a simple molecular
substance **melts or boils, only the weak intermolecular forces of attraction
break. The covalent bonds inside the molecules never break.** Steam is still
H₂O; gaseous methane is still CH₄. Every "explain the low boiling point"
mark scheme awards its points for exactly this separation — weak forces
broken (little energy) versus strong covalent bonds intact.
:::

:::exam-hint
The question "what is broken when water boils?" has one right answer and a
famous wrong one. Right: **intermolecular forces of attraction between the
molecules**. Wrong: the covalent O–H bonds. The wrong answer is also the
 chemically absurd one — if boiling split water's covalent bonds, the steam
would be hydrogen and oxygen gases, not steam. Structure-and-bonding papers
use this exact test on both water and hydrocarbons; write "intermolecular
forces between molecules are broken, covalent bonds within the molecules are
not" and bank all the marks.
:::

:::misconception
"When water boils, the molecules split into hydrogen and oxygen." If that
were true, boiling would be a chemical reaction and steam would be two new
substances. It is not: the molecules survive boiling completely intact, and
the gas is still H₂O — just molecules that have escaped from each other. The
energy went into overcoming the weak attractions *between* molecules, which
is a physical change, reversible with no chemistry at all. Molecules move
apart when boiled; they do not come apart.
:::
