Everything molecular so far shared one feature: the covalent bonds stopped at
the edge of each molecule. Now meet the substances where the covalent bonds
**never stop** — and discover why the same shared pairs that build methane
also build the hardest natural material on Earth.

**Diamond is a form of pure carbon.** Each carbon atom has four electrons in
its outer shell, so — by the rule you have used all lesson — it forms **four
covalent bonds**. In diamond, each carbon atom bonds strongly to **four other
carbon atoms** arranged tetrahedrally around it (section 9's shape, made
physical). Each of those neighbours bonds to four more, and so on outward
without end: the covalent bonds continue **on and on in three dimensions**
through the entire crystal. This is a **[[giant-covalent-structure]]** — a
structure, not a molecule.

That last point is worth its own paragraph because it is a favourite
examiner trap. Diamond is **not a molecule**, because molecules always
contain a *fixed* number of atoms, and the number of atoms joined together in
a real diamond **is completely variable — it depends on the size of the
crystal**. A tiny diamond and a huge one are chemically identical but contain
different numbers of atoms, so no formula like "C₂₅₀" could ever describe
one. The carbon-to-carbon ratio is always 1:1 (the substance is pure carbon),
but there is no molecule to count. Compare: methane is always CH₄, exactly;
diamond is just... carbon, extended.

**Melting and boiling: very high.** To melt diamond you must break the very
strong carbon–carbon covalent bonds, and those bonds extend **throughout the
whole crystal in three dimensions** — there is no weak seam anywhere. A
tremendous amount of energy has to be supplied, so diamond has very high
melting and boiling points. Feel the contrast with the molecular story from
sections 15–16: to melt methane you only had to nudge molecules apart from
weak intermolecular attractions; to melt diamond you have to *unbuild the
covalent network itself*. Same bonding principle, opposite energy bill — and
that contrast **is** spec 1.49 and spec 1.47 sitting side by side on the
paper.

**Hardness: extreme.** The same structure explains it. A lot of energy is
needed to break the strong covalent bonds in the giant structure, so diamond
is **very hard — the hardest naturally occurring substance** — and crystalline.
That is not trivia: drill bits for stone and rock are **tipped with
diamonds**, because nothing else holds its edge against rock.

:::key-point
"Giant" in chemistry has a precise meaning: the structure **continues in
three dimensions with no fixed limit** — no molecule, no fixed atom count,
no edge formula. Diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (SiO₂) are the course's
three giant covalent structures. Any sentence that calls diamond a "molecule"
or gives it a molecular formula has already lost the marking point; the right
vocabulary is *structure*, *network*, *crystal*, *giant covalent*.
:::

:::exam-hint
The 4-mark giant-covalent explanation has a fixed skeleton worth memorising:
(1) each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds to four other carbon atoms;
(2) the strong covalent bonds extend throughout the giant structure in three
dimensions; (3) a large amount of energy is needed to break them; (4)
therefore diamond has a very high melting point / is very hard. Papers ask
the same chain for melting point, hardness, and "why is diamond different
from methane?" — one skeleton, three question skins. Notice what is *never*
in the answer: intermolecular forces. Diamond has none (no molecules!).
:::

:::misconception
"Diamond is one enormous molecule." The error is understandable — it is one
connected object — but "molecule" means a *fixed* number of atoms, and a
diamond's atom count is whatever the crystal's size makes it. Two diamonds
differ in atom count while being the same substance; no molecule behaves that
way. Say **giant covalent structure** and you keep the marking points; say
"molecule" and you contradict the definition the examiner is testing. The
same warning applies in reverse to methane: it is a genuine molecule because
its atom count is fixed at 5, every time.
:::
