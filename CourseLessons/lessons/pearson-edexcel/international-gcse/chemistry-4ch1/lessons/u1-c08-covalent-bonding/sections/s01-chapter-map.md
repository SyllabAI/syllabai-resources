Chapter 7 (u1:c07) explained compounds built by electron **transfer** — metals
handing electrons to non-metals to make ions. This chapter explains the other,
even bigger family of compounds: the ones built by electron **sharing**. Water,
the sugar in your food, the carbon dioxide you breathe out, the plastics in your
pencil case — almost every substance around you that is not a metal, not a salt
and not a rock mineral is held together by covalent bonds. There are far more
covalent compounds than ionic compounds, and by the end of this lesson the bond
that holds them together will feel routine rather than mysterious.

All eight objectives of record for this lesson are **core** specification
statements — examined in **both Paper 1 (1C) and Paper 2 (2C)**, and shared
with Science Double Award:

- a covalent bond is a **shared pair of electrons** (spec 1.44);
- covalent bonds explained as **electrostatic attractions** (spec 1.45);
- **dot-and-cross diagrams** for diatomic molecules (hydrogen, oxygen,
  nitrogen, the halogens and hydrogen halides), inorganic molecules (water,
  ammonia, carbon dioxide) and organic molecules up to two carbons (methane,
  ethane, ethene and halogen-containing ones) (spec 1.46);
- why **simple molecular structures** are gases, liquids or low-melting solids,
  and the term **intermolecular forces of attraction** (spec 1.47);
- why melting and boiling points of simple molecular substances **increase as
  relative molecular mass increases** (spec 1.48);
- why **giant covalent structures** are solids with high melting and boiling
  points (spec 1.49);
- how the structures of **diamond, graphite and C₆₀ fullerene** decide their
  properties — conductivity and hardness (spec 1.50);
- covalent compounds **do not usually conduct electricity** (spec 1.51).

The lesson runs in four movements: what a covalent bond is and how you draw it
(dot-and-cross, molecule by molecule); the shapes molecules take and the
special cases that break the 8-electron pattern; why molecular substances melt
and boil so easily — the intermolecular-forces story; and the giant covalent
counter-examples, diamond and graphite, where the same shared-pair idea builds
the hardest natural material on Earth. Work through the sections in order —
each one's skill feeds the next.

:::prior-knowledge
Three pieces of earlier work carry this whole lesson. First,
**electronic configurations** from Chapter 3 (u1:c03) — you need [2, 8, 7] for
chlorine or [2, 6] for oxygen without hesitation, because covalent bonding is
outer-shell bookkeeping. Second, **groups of the Periodic Table** from Chapter
4 (u1:c04) — the group number tells you the number of outer-shell electrons,
which decides how many bonds an atom forms. Third, **ionic bonding** from
Chapter 7 (u1:c07) — every idea here is its mirror image: sharing instead of
transfer, molecules instead of lattices, low melting points instead of high.
If any of those feels shaky, revisit it before going further.
:::

:::exam-hint
Every objective in this lesson is core — Paper 1 or Paper 2, every candidate
including Double Award. The single most examined skill is **drawing
dot-and-cross diagrams** (spec 1.46): it appears in some form in nearly every
paper, and the diagram rules are mechanical once learned. The second most
examined idea is the **melting-point argument** (specs 1.47–1.49): "which
forces break, and how much energy does that take?" Learn the two stories —
weak intermolecular forces for molecular substances, strong covalent bonds for
giant structures — and you can answer a whole family of 4–6 mark questions
almost on autopilot.
:::

:::misconception
"Covalent bonding is a completely new topic — nothing from ionic bonding
carries over." The opposite: it is the **same electrostatic idea pointed at a
different arrangement**. In both bond types the thing holding atoms together
is positive–negative attraction; the difference is only what the electrons do
(transfer versus share). Learners who file the two bond types as unrelated
subjects memorise twice. Learners who see sharing as transfer's twin need to
learn half as much — and exam questions frequently ask you to compare the two
in a single answer.
:::
