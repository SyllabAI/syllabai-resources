Dot-and-cross diagrams are flat, but molecules are not. Understanding the
bonding lets you work out the actual three-dimensional **shape** of a
molecule, and the rule is delightfully simple: **pairs of electrons in the
outer shell of the central atom repel each other, and therefore get as far
apart as possible.**

Take methane. Around the central carbon atom there are four pairs of
electrons — the four shared pairs of the four C–H bonds. All four pairs are
negative and all four repel, so they spread out to the maximum separation
that geometry allows: pointing towards the corners of a **[[tetrahedral]]**
arrangement. A tetrahedron (the noun; "tetrahedral" is the adjective) is a
triangular-based pyramid — one position on top and three around the base.
The carbon sits in the middle and the four hydrogen atoms occupy the four
corners, with bond angles of about 109.5°. Every C–H bond in methane is
identical, so the molecule is a perfectly symmetric spinning top.

Water, from the same rule, cannot be flat either — and it cannot be
tetrahedral *in shape* either, which sounds contradictory until you separate
two ideas: the arrangement of **electron pairs** and the shape formed by the
**atoms**. Water's oxygen also has four electron pairs, and those four also
spread out tetrahedrally — but only two of the pairs are bonds to hydrogen
atoms. The molecule's *atomic* shape is what the two hydrogens and the oxygen
trace out, and you will see in the next section how the other two pairs bend
it into a distinctive V. The electron pairs set the geometry; the atoms show
only part of it.

The prediction recipe, then: count the electron pairs around the central
atom, spread them as far apart as possible, and read off where the *bonded*
atoms sit. Four pairs, all bonding — tetrahedral molecule. Four pairs, two
bonding — bent molecule. The pairs you cannot see in a line drawing are doing
as much geometric work as the bonds you can.

:::cross-curricular
The tetrahedron is a solid you already own from mathematics: a
triangular-based pyramid, one of the Platonic solids, with four triangular
faces. The same solid appears as a fair die with four faces, in the corner
brackets of steel frameworks, and in crystallography — the science of crystal
shapes that grew from exactly this kind of geometry. When chemistry says a
methane molecule is tetrahedral, it is borrowing mathematics' most economical
way of putting four things as far apart as possible in three-dimensional
space: the geometry is not an analogy, it is the same theorem.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two drawing habits score here. First, when drawing organic molecules,
remember that **carbon always forms 4 bonds** (sharing 4 electrons) and
**hydrogen always forms 1** (sharing 1 electron) — and put the **hydrogen
atoms on the outside**, never in the middle of the structure. That single
rule prevents most wrong organic diagrams at this level. Second, in "shape of
molecule" questions, name the arrangement of the electron *pairs* and the
shape of the *atoms* separately: methane's pairs are tetrahedral and so is
its atomic shape; water's pairs are tetrahedral but its atomic shape is bent.
Mixing the two levels loses the marking point.
:::

:::misconception
"A molecule drawn flat on paper is flat in reality." The diagram is a
projection, not a photograph. Methane is drawn as a symmetric flat cross in
simple sketches, but the real molecule is tetrahedral in three dimensions —
no two of those C–H bonds are in the same plane as drawn. When a question
asks about shape, answer from the electron-pair repulsion rule, never from
the flatness of your drawing. The paper is 2D; the chemistry is 3D.
:::
