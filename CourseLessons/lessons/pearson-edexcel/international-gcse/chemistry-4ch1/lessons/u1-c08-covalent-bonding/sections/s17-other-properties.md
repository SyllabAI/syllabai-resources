Melting and boiling are not the only properties that follow from structure.
Covalent molecular substances carry a signature across conductivity and
solubility too — and the reasoning is the same structure-first method you
have practised all lesson.

**Conductivity: no.** Covalent molecular compounds **do not conduct
electricity**. Two conditions must hold before anything conducts: there must
be charged particles, and they must be **free to move**. Molecular substances
fail on both counts. The molecules carry **no overall electrical charge**
(there are no ions — sharing is not charging), and all the electrons are held
tightly: locked in atoms or fixed in covalent bonds between specific atoms,
**unable to move from molecule to molecule**. No mobile charges, no current —
pure water, candle wax, sugar and hexane are all insulators, and the reason
is the same in every case.

**Solubility in water: usually no.** Covalent molecular substances **tend to
be insoluble in water**. Water is a polar solvent (section 10) and most
molecular substances do not interact with it strongly enough to be pulled
apart into solution. The exceptions are instructive rather than
embarrassing: **ethanol** (C₂H₅OH) mixes with water in any proportion, and
substances such as **ammonia** and **hydrogen chloride** dissolve readily —
because they **react with water** as they dissolve, forming new species. A
reaction-driven "dissolving" is chemistry, not just mixing; that is why HCl
in water is hydrochloric acid, a new substance entirely.

**Solubility in organic solvents: usually yes.** The mirror image: covalent
molecular substances are **often soluble in organic solvents** — liquids such
as ethanol, propanone (nail-varnish remover) and hexane. Like dissolves
like: molecular solutes sit happily among molecular solvents. This is the
working rule behind everyday chemistry — nail varnish (molecular) dissolves
in propanone (molecular) but not in water; grease dissolves in petrol but not
in rain.

Put the three properties beside the melting-point rule from sections 15–16
and the molecular profile is complete: low melting and boiling points, no
conductivity, water-insoluble but organic-solvent-soluble. Contrast the ionic
profile from Chapter 7 (u1:c07) — high melting points, conducting when molten
or dissolved. Every question in this family is answered by asking one thing:
*what particles are present, and can they move?*

:::exam-hint
Conductivity explanations are marked by the **two-part test**: (1) there are
no charged particles (no ions) / the molecules are neutral, and (2) the
electrons are held tightly in atoms and covalent bonds and **cannot move from
molecule to molecule**. Write both parts — "no free to move charge carriers"
alone is often only half the marks. And when the paper sets a table of
substances and asks which conducts, run the ionic/covalent classification
first (that is the chapter-questions skill too): molecular → no.
:::

:::misconception
"Covalent substances never conduct — full stop, no explanation needed." The
conclusion is right at this level (spec 1.51 says "not usually") but the
unexplained version scores one mark at best. Equally dangerous is the
invented mechanism: "they conduct a little because the molecules move". Fixed
molecules moving around does not make a current — a current is **charge
carriers moving**, and molecules are neutral. Learn the proper two-part
mechanism once (no ions, electrons locked in bonds) and deploy it in every
answer; the property is the conclusion, the particles are the marks.
:::
