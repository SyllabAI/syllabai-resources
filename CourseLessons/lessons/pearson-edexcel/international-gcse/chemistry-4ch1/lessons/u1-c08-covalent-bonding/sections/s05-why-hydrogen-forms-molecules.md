Section 4 gave you the counting rule; this section gives you the *reason* the
rule works, and it is worth a section of its own because the same reasoning
answers every "why do bonds form?" question on the course.

**Whenever a bond is formed — of whatever kind — energy is released**, and the
things involved end up more stable (lower in energy) than they were before.
The more bonds an atom can form, the more energy is released, and the more
stable the resulting system becomes. Two separate hydrogen atoms sit high in
energy. Let them share a pair, and the H₂ molecule that forms is **much more
stable** than the two atoms were apart. The molecule is the lower-energy
state, which is why hydrogen gas exists as H₂ molecules and why the atoms
stay bonded rather than drifting apart again.

This energy story is also the exact reverse of bond breaking. If forming a
bond releases energy, then breaking a bond must **take in** the same amount
of energy — you have to climb back up to the separated-atom state. Keep this
pair of statements together in your mind:

- bond **formation** → energy **released** → products more stable;
- bond **breaking** → energy **supplied** → separates into higher-energy pieces.

You will use both directions constantly. In this chapter they explain why
molecular substances melt and boil at low temperatures (breaking the weak
forces between molecules costs little energy) while giant structures like
diamond demand enormous temperatures (breaking strong covalent bonds costs a
lot). In the energetics chapter (u3:c19) the same ideas become exothermic and
endothermic reactions properly. Learn them once, in the bonding context,
where the picture is simplest.

:::exam-hint
"Explain why hydrogen exists as H₂ molecules" is a two-mark gift if you speak
the energy language: (1) forming the bond **releases energy**, so (2) the
molecule is **more stable** (lower energy) than the separate atoms. The
mark scheme wants the energy movement named explicitly — answers that only
say "because they share electrons" describe *what* happens without the *why*.
The phrase "more stable than the separate atoms" is worth memorising as a
unit.
:::

:::misconception
"Hydrogen atoms *want* to reach a full shell, so they bond." Atoms do not want
anything — this teleology language costs marks in explanation questions. The
mechanical reason is energy: the bonded arrangement has **less energy** than
the separated atoms, and lower-energy arrangements are the stable ones.
Everything else — full shells, noble-gas counts — is a pattern we observe in
*which* arrangements win, not a desire the atoms feel. In your written
answers, replace every "wants", "tries" and "needs to" with an energy
statement and the mark scheme will find your reasoning where it looks for it.
:::
