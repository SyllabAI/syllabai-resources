Real organic molecules mix their atoms, and the exam's favourite mixture is a
carbon–hydrogen skeleton with a halogen (Group 7) atom attached. Two
molecules cover every rule you need.

**Bromomethane, CH₃Br.** Three hydrogen atoms and one bromine atom are joined
to a central carbon atom. You might worry about the bromine: with 35
electrons in total, you have never worked out its full electronic
configuration — and you do not need to. Look at the Periodic Table: bromine
sits in **Group 7**, so it has **7 electrons in its outer shell**, and it will
share 1 electron to reach 8. Therefore bromine forms **exactly 1 covalent
bond**, just as chlorine did in HCl and Cl₂. The diagram for CH₃Br shows
**outer shells only**: carbon on 8 (three C–H pairs plus one C–Br pair), each
hydrogen on 2, bromine on 8 with its three lone pairs drawn in. The rule that
saves you: **the group number gives the outer electrons; you never need the
inner ones** — and in diagrams you are only asked for outer electrons anyway.
(Colours are a drawing convenience, not chemistry: examiners accept any
clearly labelled dot-and-cross, in one colour or many.)

**Chloroethene, CH₂CHCl.** Probably the most complicated molecule for which
you could be asked to draw a dot-and-cross at this level — and it is just two
rules stacked. Each carbon forms 4 bonds; each hydrogen forms 1; the chlorine
(Group 7) forms 1. The two carbons are bonded to each other, each carrying
two hydrogens except where the substitution sits: one carbon holds two H's
and the other holds one H plus the Cl. Count to check the double bond: each
carbon has 4 electrons to share; after its hydrogens (and the Cl on the
second carbon), the leftover electron on each carbon pairs up between the
carbons — giving the C=C **double bond** between them. The audit: both
carbons on 8, hydrogens on 2, chlorine on 8 with three lone pairs.

One trap to disarm: the same molecule can be drawn as a dot-and-cross diagram
*or* as a line structure showing the bonds — and they are **different
answers to different questions**. The line structure is quicker, but if the
question asks for a dot-and-cross diagram, the electrons must appear as dots
and crosses with their shared pairs visible. Choose by the question's words,
not by which drawing you prefer.

:::key-point
Extension — atoms in Period 3 and below can break the octet ceiling. The
maximum number of electrons a central atom can **share** equals the number of
electrons in its outer shell. Sulfur (6 outer electrons) can form up to **6
bonds** — as in SF₆ — and chlorine (7) can form up to **7**, as in HClO₄.
Outer atoms still finish on 8; only generous central atoms grow beyond it.
You will not be asked to draw these heavyweights often, but knowing they
exist stops you from "correcting" a right diagram into a wrong one.
:::

:::exam-hint
For every halogenated organic molecule: identify each atom's bond count
**from its group** before drawing — C (Group 4) 4 bonds; H 1; Cl, Br, I
(Group 7) 1 bond each with three lone pairs left over. Then let the bond
counts *generate* the structure: in CH₂CHCl the counts force the C=C double
bond, because two carbons with only three substituents each have one electron
left over to share with each other. Questions hand you the formula; the group
numbers hand you the diagram.
:::

:::misconception
"I can't draw bromomethane because I don't know bromine's full electronic
configuration." This stalls more candidates than any chemistry in the
chapter. Full configurations are irrelevant to outer-shell diagrams: a
Group 7 atom has 7 outer electrons and forms 1 bond, whether it is chlorine
with 17 electrons or iodine with 53. The Periodic Table position *is* the
information. If you catch yourself counting inner shells to reach a halogen,
stop — the group number already answered the question.
:::
