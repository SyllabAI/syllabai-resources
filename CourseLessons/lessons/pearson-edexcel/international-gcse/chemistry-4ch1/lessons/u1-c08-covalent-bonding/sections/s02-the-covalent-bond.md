Every bond you will meet — ionic, covalent, metallic — is held
together by the same physics: **electrostatic attractions between something
positively charged and something negatively charged**. What makes a
**[[covalent-bond]]** different is the arrangement. In a covalent bond, a
**pair of electrons is shared between two atoms**. The shared pair sits
between the two nuclei, and both nuclei (positive) pull on it (negative) at
the same time. That two-way pull — the [[electrostatic-attraction]] between
the shared pair and *both* nuclei — is what locks the atoms together.

It is worth drawing the picture in your head precisely: two positive nuclei,
and between them one negative pair that belongs to neither atom alone. Each
nucleus is attracted to the shared pair; the pair is attracted to each
nucleus; and those pulls hold the two atoms at a fixed distance apart. Pull
them apart and you remove that attraction — which costs energy. This is the
whole bonding model in one paragraph, and every diagram later in this lesson
is just this picture with real atoms dropped in.

In most of the simple examples you will meet at International GCSE, each atom
supplies **one electron** to the shared pair — one from atom A, one from atom
B. That does not have to be so: both electrons of the shared pair may come
from the same atom. You will meet a few examples of this sharing pattern later
in the course, but the one-each arrangement is the one to picture by default.

:::key-point
When atoms have 8 electrons in their outer shell, they have the same number of
electrons as a noble gas atom — they are **[[isoelectronic]]** with a noble
gas. This is the target state that covalent bonding reaches for: each atom in
a molecule counts the shared electrons as part of its own outer shell, so
bonding arrangements that give every atom 2 (for hydrogen) or 8 outer
electrons are the ones that form.
:::

:::exam-hint
Dots and crosses are pure bookkeeping. The electrons drawn as dots and the
electrons drawn as crosses are **absolutely identical in reality** — there is
no difference between them at all. The only job of dots-versus-crosses is to
show which atom each electron came from. In an exam answer, never write that
"dot electrons" and "cross electrons" behave differently; and never be alarmed
if your own diagram mixes them — the examiner reads the *pairs*, not the
symbols.
:::

:::misconception
"A shared pair of electrons belongs to whichever atom it is drawn closer to."
No — the pair is shared, meaning both atoms count it in their outer shells
simultaneously. A related error is treating the shared pair as a kind of glue
stuck on the outside of one atom. The correct picture is a pair sitting
between the two nuclei, attracting and attracted by both at once. If your
diagram or your sentence assigns the pair exclusively to one atom, you have
drawn an ion, not a covalent bond.
:::
