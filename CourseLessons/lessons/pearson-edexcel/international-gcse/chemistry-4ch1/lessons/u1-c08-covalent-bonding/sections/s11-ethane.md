Everything so far had a single central atom. Ethane, C₂H₆, is the first
molecule on the course with **two** carbon atoms, and it is your bridge from
simple inorganic diagrams to the organic molecules of Unit 4 — built from
nothing but the rules you already have.

Start from methane and extend it. In methane, one carbon shares with four
hydrogens. In ethane, each of the two carbon atoms bonds to **three**
hydrogen atoms — and the two carbons bond **to each other**, each contributing
one electron to a shared pair in the C–C bond. Run the audit atom by atom:
each carbon has 4 outer electrons, spends 3 sharing with hydrogens and 1
sharing with the other carbon, and ends with 8; each hydrogen shares one pair
and ends with 2. Six C–H bonds and one C–C bond — seven covalent bonds in
total, and every atom on 2-or-8. The formula C₂H₆ is the diagram's summary.

The drawing strategy for any two-carbon molecule is now on the table: draw
the carbon–carbon connection first, then hang the hydrogens off each carbon
until every carbon's count reaches 8. Hydrogens go on the outside (section
9's rule), each carrying its single shared pair. No new bonding ideas are
needed — only careful bookkeeping with more atoms on the page.

Ethane is also your first explicitly **organic compound**: a compound built
around carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen bonding. There is a whole unit of
this ahead (Unit 4, from u4:c22 onwards) — fuels, alcohols, polymers — and
every one of those molecules will be drawn with exactly the technique you
just used. Nothing about organic chemistry at this level is a new bonding
theory; it is the same shared pairs, in bigger and more interesting
arrangements.

:::exam-hint
When a question asks for the dot-and-cross diagram of ethane (outer electrons
only), the fastest safe order is: (1) place the two carbon atoms side by side
and share a pair between them (one dot, one cross) for the C–C bond;
(2) attach three hydrogens to *each* carbon, each sharing one pair;
(3) fill each carbon's remaining outer electrons; (4) audit — each carbon on
8, each hydrogen on 2, seven bonds total. Carbon-4-bonds, hydrogen-1-bond,
hydrogens-on-the-outside: the three-part rule from section 9 does all the
work.
:::

:::misconception
"Ethane is two methane molecules joined together." Molecules do not join to
make bigger molecules by sticking — ethane is one molecule with one specific
bonding arrangement: two carbons sharing a pair directly with each other, and
three hydrogens per carbon. The phrase "two methanes" also gets the bonding
wrong at the joint: a C–C shared pair is a genuine covalent bond with its own
two electrons, not a kind of staple between two finished CH₄ units. Draw the
C–C shared pair explicitly and the distinction draws itself.
:::
