Water completes the shape story that section 9 set up. Around the central
oxygen atom there are **four pairs of electrons** — but only two of them are
shared pairs in covalent bonds with the hydrogens. The other two are pairs
that sit on the oxygen's outer shell without bonding to anything: **[[lone-pair]]s** of electrons. All four pairs — bonding and lone alike — repel each
other, so all four adopt the tetrahedral arrangement.

But the *shape of the molecule* is drawn by the atoms, not the pairs. Two of
the four tetrahedral positions are occupied by lone pairs, which show no atom
at all. The oxygen and its two hydrogens therefore trace out only part of the
tetrahedron, and the molecule comes out **bent** — often described as
**V-shaped**. The lone pairs are the hidden scaffolding: invisible in a
formula, decisive in the geometry.

Those lone pairs do something else with a visible consequence. In the O–H
shared pairs, the electrons are attracted **to a different extent** by the
oxygen and by the hydrogen — the oxygen nucleus pulls the shared electrons
towards itself more strongly. Each bond has its electrons shifted slightly
towards the oxygen end, and because the molecule is bent (not symmetric like
methane's spinning top), the little shifts do not cancel. The result: a water
molecule is **[[polar]]** — it has a slightly negative end (towards the
oxygen) and a slightly positive end (towards the hydrogens).

Polarity is not just a label; it is something you can see. A stream of water
from a tap is **bent by an electrically charged object** — run a comb through
dry hair, hold it near a thin trickle of water, and the stream leans towards
the comb. The charged object attracts the slightly charged ends of the water
molecules and pulls the whole stream off course. That demonstration is the
cleanest evidence you will ever see that electron pairs in water are shared
unequally — and it all follows from two unshared pairs and a bent shape.

:::key-point
Lone pairs are electron pairs **in the outer shell of a bonded atom that take
no part in covalent bonding** — but they still repel, still occupy positions
in the arrangement around the central atom, and still count when you work out
a molecule's shape. Water: four pairs total, two bonding, two lone → pairs
tetrahedral, atoms bent. Any shape prediction that ignores lone pairs will
get water wrong.
:::

:::exam-hint
Shape questions about water almost always want the three-step chain: (1) the
oxygen has four electron pairs in its outer shell; (2) the pairs repel and
arrange tetrahedrally; (3) because two pairs are lone pairs, the *molecular*
shape is bent (V-shaped). If the question pushes further — a charged rod
bending a water stream — the marking points are "water is polar" and "the
charged rod attracts the oppositely charged end of the molecule". Write
"lone pairs" by name; papers use the term and so should you.
:::

:::misconception
"Only shared pairs matter, so water should be a straight molecule like
H–O–H on a line." Ignoring the lone pairs deletes half of the oxygen's outer
shell, and the shape prediction collapses. The two lone pairs repel the
bonding pairs exactly as the bonding pairs repel each other — they occupy
their own two corners of the tetrahedron and push the hydrogens together.
The visible result is the bend, and the electrical proof is the comb
experiment. Electron pairs you cannot see still push.
:::
