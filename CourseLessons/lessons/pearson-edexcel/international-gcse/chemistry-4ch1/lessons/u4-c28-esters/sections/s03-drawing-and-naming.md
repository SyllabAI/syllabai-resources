The making section built one ester; this section builds all of
them, in both directions: formula from name, and name from
formula (4.41C), with ethyl ethanoate's own structures as the
model (4.40C).

**Building the ester.** To work out the structure of the ester
formed from any alcohol and carboxylic acid, start by drawing
the two molecules so that their –OH groups sit next to each
other. Now remove H₂O — one H and one OH — and join together
what is left. The leftover fragments bond through the ester
group, C=O joined to C–O, exactly the –COO– arrangement from
the opening section:

```
ethanol part          ethanoic-acid part
   CH₃CH₂—O—H  +  H—O—C(=O)CH₃
              ↓  −H₂O
        CH₃CH₂—O—C(=O)CH₃
           ethyl ethanoate
```

Which H and which OH are removed makes no difference to the
product: take the H from the alcohol and the OH from the acid,
or swap them, and joining the leftovers gives the same structure
either way. At International GCSE the distinction is not
examined — what is examined is the join: break the O–H pairing,
release water, bond the fragments.

An ester can be drawn either way round — CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ and
CH₃CH₂OOCCH₃ are the same molecule drawn from each end — but
the *name* is always written one way: **ethyl ethanoate**,
never "ethanoate ethyl". Keep the two drawing levels straight:
the condensed CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ is the [[structural-formula]], and
the every-bond-shown version — each C–C, C=O, C–O and O–H
drawn out — is the [[displayed-formula]]. Drawing items say
which level they want; producing the wrong level is a lost
mark even when the molecule is right.

**The naming rule.** When naming esters, the part with the C=O
is always the **'oate'** part. The name reads
[[alkyl-group]] [[alkanoate]]: the alkyl word comes from the
alcohol, the alkanoate word from the carboxylic acid. So the
'yl' bit tells you the alcohol and the 'oate' bit tells you the
carboxylic acid — **propyl butanoate** is made from propanol
and butanoic acid. Count carefully: the alkanoate carbon count
includes the carbonyl carbon, exactly as it did for the acids
(u4:c27).

**The family grid.** Three alcohols (methanol, ethanol,
propan-1-ol) crossed with three acids (methanoic, ethanoic,
propanoic) give nine esters in a three-by-three grid:

| | methanoic acid | ethanoic acid | propanoic acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **methanol** | methyl methanoate | methyl ethanoate | methyl propanoate |
| **ethanol** | ethyl methanoate | **ethyl ethanoate** | ethyl propanoate |
| **propan-1-ol** | propyl methanoate | propyl ethanoate | propyl propanoate |

Read any cell as alcohol-row + acid-column: the row names the
alkyl part, the column names the alkanoate part. In every cell
the same trace holds: the fragment bonded through its spare
oxygen is the alcohol part, the fragment carrying the C=O is
the acid part. When each member is drawn in full with its
parents colour-coded — the alcohol parts in red, the acid
parts in blue — every bond in every one of the nine molecules
traces back to exactly one parent, which is the whole point
the grid teaches: an ester is two fragments joined through the
–COO– group, nothing more.

**Reversing the read.** You also need to work backwards: given
an ester, name the alcohol and carboxylic acid it came from.
Break the molecule apart between the single-bonded C–O inside
the ester group, then add OH to one side and H to the other so
that both fragments end with an –OH group:

CH₃COOCH₂CH₃(l) + H₂O(l) → CH₃COOH(l) + CH₃CH₂OH(l)

ethyl ethanoate + water → ethanoic acid + ethanol

The water you removed to build the ester is the water you add
back to split it. It does not actually matter which C–O bond
you break, as long as you add H to one side and OH to the other
to give two –OH groups, you will get the same answer — because
the two C–O bonds of the ester group are interchangeable under
this operation. Split ethyl ethanoate and you recover ethanol
and ethanoic acid; that is the grid cell it was born from.

:::key-point
When naming esters, the part with the C=O is always the 'oate'
part — and the name always runs alkyl-then-alkanoate. Ethyl
ethanoate: ethyl from ethanol (the alcohol), ethanoate from
ethanoic acid (the acid, carbonyl carbon counted). Methyl
propanoate: methyl from methanol, propanoate from propanoic
acid. The formula can be drawn either way round; the name
cannot.
:::

:::key-point
Splitting an ester back into its parents is insensitive to
which C–O bond you break — add H to one fragment and OH to the
other and both routes give the same two –OH molecules. Use the
bond that gives the cleaner drawing; the answer does not
change.
:::

:::worked-example
Name the ester CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ and give its parents.
*Decode:* find the ester group. The C=O carbon sits in the
CH₃CH₂C(=O)O– fragment, so the alkanoate part is propanoate —
three carbons, carbonyl included. The fragment on the other
side of the single-bonded O is CH₃–, so the alkyl part is
methyl. The ester is **methyl propanoate**. *Reverse:* split
between the C and the O of the group, add OH to the
CH₃CH₂C(=O)– side and H to the CH₃– side: **propanoic acid**
(CH₃CH₂COOH) and **methanol** (CH₃OH). Grid check: methanol
row, propanoic acid column — methyl propanoate. Consistent.
:::

:::exam-hint
The 'yl'/'oate' decode is the mark-earner in naming items: read
the name left to right, alcohol then acid. "Propyl butanoate is
made from propanol and butanoic acid" — one mark for each
parent, and the formulae must show the parents with their –OH
groups intact. When the question hands you a drawn structure
instead, locate the C=O first: the carbon bearing it starts the
alkanoate count, and everything beyond the single-bonded
oxygen is the alkyl part.
:::

:::did-you-know
Esters are often described as having a sweet, fruity smell —
and chemists have used that on purpose. Artificial flavourings
and perfumes are built by mixing esters chosen for their
scents, which is why the same family that makes nail polish
remover smell sharp also makes pear drops smell like pear.
:::

:::misconception
"Propyl butanoate comes from butanol and propanoic acid." The
words were swapped: position in the name does not track size.
The 'yl' word — propyl — names the alcohol fragment, and the
'oate' word — butanoate — names the acid fragment, regardless
of which fragment is longer. Decode left to right, alcohol
then acid, every time.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you build any grid ester by drawing the two parents with
their –OH groups adjacent and removing H₂O? Can you write
ethyl ethanoate both ways round and name it correctly? Can you
decode propyl butanoate to its parents, and split butyl
ethanoate back the other way? If yes, both directions of 4.41C
are yours.
:::
