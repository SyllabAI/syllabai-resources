Heat a mixture of ethanoic acid and ethanol with a few drops of
concentrated sulfuric acid and a new colourless liquid appears:
**[[ethyl-ethanoate]]**, the ester of this lesson (4.39C). The
equation of record carries every atom, with the word labels
under each formula:

CH₃COOH(l) + CH₃CH₂OH(l) → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃(l) + H₂O(l)

ethanoic acid + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + water

Three practical details sit under that equation, and the
examiners ask for all of them. The **starting materials** are
ethanol and ethanoic acid. The **catalyst** is a few drops of
concentrated sulfuric acid. The **conditions** are heat. The
sulfuric acid earns its place above the arrow, not in it: a
[[catalyst]] speeds the reaction but is not consumed, so writing
it into the equation would break the atom balance — the same
bookkeeping rule that kept MnO₂ above the arrow in the
peroxide decomposition back in the rates lesson (u3:c20).

The reaction has two names, and both are examinable. It is
**[[esterification]]** — an alcohol plus a carboxylic acid
making an ester. It is also a **[[condensation-reaction]]**,
because water is produced while two molecules join together.
The condensation label is the one that matters next: the
synthetic-polymers lesson (u4:c29) builds on exactly this
join-and-expel pattern.

The third detail decides the yield. Esterification is a
[[reversible-reaction]], so the mixture sits at a position of
equilibrium (u3:c21): the position always moves to counteract
whatever change you impose. Add water to the reactants and the
position shifts left to consume it — more alcohol and acid, less
ester. That is the opposite of what you want, so the synthesis
run in reverse is guarded against: pure ethanol and pure
ethanoic acid (water-free [[glacial-ethanoic-acid]]) are used,
keeping the equilibrium stocked with reactants instead of
product-killers.

:::key-point
The concentrated sulfuric acid never enters the equation. A
catalyst is not consumed, so it appears above the arrow or not
at all — but the *conditions* line still names it, because "heat
with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid" is examinable
wording. Catalyst in the conditions, catalyst out of the
equation.
:::

:::did-you-know
Ethyl ethanoate has quite a strong smell of its own — and a day
job you have probably held in your hand. It is used as a
solvent in nail polish and nail polish remover: the same
volatility that carries fruit scents across a room also makes
it evaporate cleanly from a painted nail.
:::

:::exam-hint
State symbols in the esterification equation are all (l) — both
reactants and both products are pure liquids, not aqueous
solutions, because no water is present to dissolve them in.
That (l) is also the mark-scheme hook for the yield argument:
water is a *product*, so adding any dilutes the reactants and
pulls the equilibrium left. Pure (glacial) acid and pure
alcohol plus a drying catalyst is the recipe the mark scheme
wants.
:::

:::misconception
"Concentrated sulfuric acid is a reactant, so it belongs in the
equation." Weigh it: the acid is recovered unchanged at the end,
and the atom balance of CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH already accounts for
every product atom. A catalyst changes the rate, never the
balance — into the conditions line, not into the equation.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the esterification equation with state symbols and
the word labels under each formula? Can you name the starting
materials, the catalyst and the conditions as three separate
items? Can you explain why water must be kept out of the mixture,
using the equilibrium argument? If yes, you can make an ester on
paper — the drawing section shows you how to make any member of
the family.
:::
