**Alcohols, carboxylic acids and esters** are organic
compounds that contain oxygen as well as carbon and hydrogen —
and they are closely related. The [[alcohol]]s carry –OH, and
oxidising them builds the [[carboxylic-acid]]s with their –COOH
group (u4:c26, u4:c27). This lesson adds the third member of
that oxygen-bearing trio. An
[[ester]] forms when an alcohol reacts with a carboxylic acid —
the –OH-bearing families join, water is expelled, and the
product is neither an alcohol nor an acid but something new.
That new family runs the flavour and perfume industry: esters
are frequently used for perfumes and artificial flavourings,
and you will meet them every time fruit scent appears in a
chemistry question.

Every family member carries the same [[functional-group]]: two
oxygen atoms arranged around one carbon, written in condensed
form as **–COO–**. Read the arrangement bond by bond:

```
      O
      ‖
—C—O—
```

The carbon at the centre carries a double bond to one oxygen
(C=O) and a single bond to the other (C–O), and it sits in the
chain with one bond spare on each side — that is what lets it
bridge the acid fragment and the alcohol fragment. The –COO–
group is the ester's signature the way –OH is the alcohol's and
–COOH is the acid's: spot it in a formula and you have found an
ester.

The family is not a laboratory curiosity. Olive oil,
sunflower oil, butter and cream are all built from natural
esters: their fat and oil molecules carry the same –COO–
arrangement, which is why this family's chemistry reaches from
a test tube to the kitchen. The small esters are the ones with
the famous smells, and their volatility makes those smells
travel — a property the uses section turns into chemistry
(4.42C). First, though, the group itself: everything else in
this lesson is drawn from it.

:::prior-knowledge
Three ideas load this lesson. First, the alcohol family and its
–OH group, including the two oxidation routes that build
carboxylic acids from them (u4:c26). Second, the carboxylic-acid
family with its –COOH group and its naming habit of counting all
carbons including the acid carbon (u4:c27). Third, the position
of equilibrium and how it responds to changes you make (u3:c21)
— esterification is reversible, so that idea decides the yield.
If any of those wobble, repair them before the making section,
because the reaction there uses all three.
:::

:::exam-hint
Statement 4.38C says *know* that esters contain the functional
group — a recall item. Reproduce –COO– from memory: a carbon
with C=O to one oxygen and C–O to the other. The classic lost
mark is writing –COOH (that is the carboxylic-acid group, with
its hydrogen still attached) or –CO– (one oxygen only). Two
oxygens, one carbon, no hydrogen on the group: that is –COO–.
:::

:::misconception
"–COO– and –COOH are the same group written differently." They
are different families. The ester group has no hydrogen on its
second oxygen — the acid's hydrogen is exactly what leaves when
the ester forms. Writing the acid's –COOH where an ester's
–COO– belongs changes the compound: the hydrogen is not
decoration, it is the difference between a family that donates
protons and one that smells of pear.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you draw the ester functional group with the C=O and C–O
bonds both explicit, and write its condensed form? Can you say
which two families react to make an ester? Can you name three
everyday materials built from natural esters? If yes, the
–COO– signature is loaded and the making section is next.
:::
