It is easy to produce esters in the lab on a test-tube scale by
reacting various combinations of carboxylic acids and alcohols
in the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid
(4.43C). The mixture has to be heated — but never by a direct
Bunsen flame, because the contents of the tubes are flammable.
A beaker of hot water at about 80 °C does the heating instead.
The full procedure for ethyl ethanoate, as run on the
test-tube scale:

1. Put 1 cm³ of ethanoic acid and 1 cm³ of ethanol into a
   boiling tube.
2. Mix well and carefully add a few drops of concentrated
   sulfuric acid.
3. Place the boiling tube in a beaker of hot water at about
   80 °C for 5 minutes.
4. Allow the contents of the tube to cool.
5. When cool, pour the mixture into a beaker half-full of
   0.5 mol/dm³ [[sodium-carbonate]] solution. There will be
   some effervescence as the excess acid reacts with the metal
   carbonate.
6. A layer of ester separates and floats on top of the water.
   The acid and alcohol mostly dissolve in the water, but the
   ester does not — the small esters float, which makes them
   easy to smell.
7. Smell the product by gently **wafting** the odour towards
   your nose with your hand. Never put your nose near the top
   of the beaker.

Two steps deserve the "why". The sodium carbonate pour exists
so that you can smell the ester better: not much ester forms in
such a short time, and the smell of the ester in the tube would
be masked by the smell of the unreacted carboxylic acid — the
carbonate mops the excess acid up (the same
carbonate-plus-acid chemistry as u2:c16, seen here as a
purification trick), and the effervescence you watch is that
mop working. The wafting step exists because the beaker holds
acid fumes and ester vapour; wafting brings a diluted sample
of the smell to your nose and keeps your face out of the
beaker.

The apparatus draws itself: a boiling tube standing in a beaker
of hot water, then the reaction mixture poured into a beaker of
cold sodium carbonate solution, where a thin layer of ester
gathers on the surface. The ester is likely to be colourless —
any colour in a drawing is there to make the layer visible, not
a property of the product.

Repeat the procedure for other combinations of acids and
alcohols. Two worth knowing by name: **butyl butanoate**, which
should smell like pineapple, and **propyl ethanoate**, which
smells like pear — run the naming decode on both and the grid
from the drawing section hands you their parents.

:::safety
Wear goggles and dispense the acids in a fume cupboard. Avoid
all skin contact with the acids; wash off immediately with
plenty of cold water if you get some on your skin. Carefully
follow the instructions as to how to smell the esters produced
— waft, never lean in.
:::

:::exam-hint
The practical item marks the *why* of each step, not just the
step. Strong answers name the hazard control (hot-water bath,
not flame — the vapours are flammable), the purification logic
(sodium carbonate removes the excess acid whose smell would
mask the ester's), and the safe-smelling technique (wafting).
A results sentence earns its mark by tying the observation to
the property: the ester floats as a separate layer because it
will not dissolve in water, and you can smell it because it is
volatile.
:::

:::misconception
"The sodium carbonate step is there to speed the reaction up."
The reaction is already over by the time the carbonate goes in
— step 5 happens after cooling. Its job is cleanup: neutralise
the leftover acid so its smell stops masking the ester's. The
effervescence is the carbonate reacting with that excess acid,
not with the ester.
:::

:::
