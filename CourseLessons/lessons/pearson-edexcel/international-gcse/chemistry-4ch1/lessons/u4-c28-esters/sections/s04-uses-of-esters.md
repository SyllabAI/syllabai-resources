The small esters like ethyl ethanoate are commonly used as
[[solvent]]s — nail polish and nail polish remover carry them
(4.42C). But the family's headline job is scent: most esters
are used in **food flavourings and perfumes**. They are
[[volatile]] liquids — they turn to vapour easily — with
distinctive smells, and that volatility is the property doing
the work: a flavouring or perfume only works if its molecules
leave the liquid and travel through the air to your nose.

The smells are not invented; they are borrowed. The typical
smell of bananas, raspberries, pears and other fruit is due in
part to naturally occurring esters ripening inside the fruit.
Chemists create artificial flavourings and perfumes using
mixtures of esters and other organic compounds — rarely one
ester alone, because a natural fruit note is a chord, not a
single note. The lab practical coming next makes three of
these scent esters directly.

One more property separates esters from the two families that
build them, and it explains the practical's most surprising
step. Esters are hardly soluble in water, whereas small
alcohols and carboxylic acids usually are. The reason is the
–OH and –COOH groups themselves: in alcohols and acids those
groups form a special type of intermolecular force called
[[hydrogen-bond]]s with water molecules, and the attractions
between water and alcohol or acid molecules are strong enough
to pull them into solution. An ester has no O–H and no
O–H-bearing group left — its two oxygens are locked into the
–COO– arrangement — so it forms very weak interactions with
water and will not dissolve. Pour an ester into water and it
sits on top: a separate layer.

:::exam-hint
"Volatile" is a defined word, not a vibe: it means turns to
vapour easily, and it is the link between structure and use.
The full-chain answer for a uses item runs: esters are volatile
so their molecules reach the nose; they have distinctive,
often fruity smells; so they are used in food flavourings and
perfumes. Naming the use without the volatility sentence loses
the explanation mark — the property-to-use link is what the
question is testing.
:::

:::misconception
"Esters dissolve in water because they contain oxygen atoms."
Solubility in water is negotiated by O–H groups forming
hydrogen bonds with water molecules — not by oxygen atoms as
such. The ester's oxygens are buried in the –COO– group with
no hydrogen to offer, so the attractions stay weak and the
ester floats as a separate layer. Oxygen content is not the
test; hydrogen-bond capability is.
:::

the family's job description is clear and the practical will

:::
