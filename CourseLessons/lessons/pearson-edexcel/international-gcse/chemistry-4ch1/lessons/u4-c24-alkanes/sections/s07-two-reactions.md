Section 6 promised two reactions; here they both are, with their
equations. All alkanes burn in air or oxygen — that is reaction one.
Alkanes react with halogens in the presence of ultraviolet
radiation — that is reaction two. Everything else you may hear
about alkane chemistry at International GCSE is a variation on
these two themes.

## Combustion

If there is enough oxygen, an alkane burns completely to give
carbon dioxide and water. Two examples, straight from the family:

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

2C₂H₆(g) + 7O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l)

If there is not enough oxygen, the combustion is
[[incomplete-combustion]], and you obtain [[carbon-monoxide]] or
carbon (soot) instead of carbon dioxide — with water still
produced:

2C₂H₆(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO(g) + 6H₂O(l)

or

2C₂H₆(g) + 3O₂(g) → 4C(s) + 6H₂O(l)

Read the four equations as one story with an oxygen dial. Plenty of
oxygen: every carbon reaches CO₂. Moderate shortage: carbon can
only reach CO. Severe shortage: carbon cannot bond to oxygen at all
and drops out as soot. In every version the hydrogens still end as
water — [[complete-combustion]] and incomplete differ *only* in the
fate of the carbon.

The incomplete products are not academic. Carbon monoxide is the
colourless, odourless gas that binds to haemoglobin in place of
oxygen — the poisoning mechanism you met in the crude oil chapter
(u4:c23) — which is why a flame starved of air in a closed room is
a killer and not just an inconvenience. Soot is the black smoke
that coats the inside of a burner starved of oxygen. The chemistry
of the oxygen dial is the safety story behind every "ventilate the
room" warning on a gas appliance.

Never try to memorise combustion equations — there are as many of
them as there are fuels. Practise deriving them instead: write the
skeleton (fuel + O₂), send every hydrogen to H₂O, then send the
carbons to CO₂, CO or C according to the oxygen available, and
balance the O₂ last. The full method is trained in the formulae and
calculations lessons (u1:c05, u1:c06). Balanced in that order, even
the 15-carbon giants of Section 4 surrender in a minute.

## Substitution

The second reaction swaps an atom instead of burning the molecule.
Alkanes react with halogens in the presence of [[ultraviolet-radiation]]
(UV light), for example from sunlight. A hydrogen atom in the
alkane is replaced by a halogen atom — and because one atom has
been replaced (or *substituted*) by a different one, the reaction
type is called a [[substitution-reaction]].

Watch it happen with methane and bromine. A mixture of methane and
bromine gas is orange, because of the bromine. Leave the mixture in
sunlight and the colour fades: the bromine has been consumed, and a
mixture of bromomethane and hydrogen bromide gases has formed.
The displayed-formula picture of the change is worth building in
your head: a methane molecule with its four C–H bonds stands next
to a Br–Br molecule; the products are the same methane frame with
one H replaced by Br, plus a small H–Br molecule carrying the
displaced hydrogen. In symbols:

CH₄(g) + Br₂(g) → CH₃Br(g) + HBr(g)

An exactly similar reaction happens between ethane and chlorine
exposed to UV light, giving a mixture of chloroethane and hydrogen
chloride gases:

CH₃CH₃(g) + Cl₂(g) → CH₃CH₂Cl(g) + HCl(g)

When only one hydrogen atom is replaced — [[mono-substitution]] —
propane reacting with bromine still manages two organic products,
because the bromine can sit on either of the two end carbons or on
the middle one. The two products are structural isomers of each
other: same molecular formula (C₃H₇Br), different structural
formulae. Products of substitution reactions of alkanes with
halogens are called [[halogenoalkane]]s, named in the format
*x*-haloalkane, where *x* gives the position of the halogen atom in
the longest carbon chain — so bromine reacting with propane gives
1-bromopropane and 2-bromopropane.

One wording trap sits inside that second equation. The HCl(g)
formed is called **hydrogen chloride**, not hydrochloric acid. To
form an acid, hydrogen chloride gas must dissolve in water and
dissociate to form H⁺ ions — the acids story from u2:c16. A dry gas
is not an acid, and writing "hydrochloric acid" beside a (g) state
symbol is a mark surrendered.

:::key-point
The alkanes undergo exactly two reactions. **Combustion**: burning
in oxygen — complete gives CO₂ + H₂O, incomplete (limited oxygen)
gives CO and/or soot, always plus water. **Substitution**: reaction
with a halogen in the presence of UV light, where one hydrogen atom
of the alkane is replaced by one halogen atom.
:::

:::exam-hint
The condition word examiners are hunting in substitution questions
is **ultraviolet radiation** (UV light) — sunlight is the everyday
example. Name it or lose the mark: "heat" and "catalyst" belong to
cracking (u4:c23), not here. And when you name the HCl product,
match the state to the name: HCl(g) is *hydrogen chloride*; only
its aqueous solution is *hydrochloric acid*. Both of these are
one-word, one-mark checks that cost nothing to get right forever.
:::

:::did-you-know
If bromine is in excess, the substitution does not stop at one
hydrogen. The reaction can go on to replace more of the hydrogens
in the methane, and what you obtain is a mixture of CH₃Br,
CH₂Br₂, CHBr₃ and CBr₄ — plus HBr of course. This
multi-substitution chemistry is exactly the kind of thing you will
meet again if you take chemistry further; at International GCSE the
mono-substitution products are the examinable ones.
:::

:::did-you-know
Why does the reaction need UV light at all? The UV radiation splits
halogen molecules into atoms — energy is needed to break the covalent
bond between the two halogen atoms. The atoms formed are called
**free radicals**, because of the unpaired electrons they carry;
these very reactive species are what go on to attack the otherwise
unreactive alkane. Without the light, the bromine stays as the
orange Br₂ molecules you started with.
:::

:::misconception
"Alkanes react with bromine water and decolorise it — that's the
substitution test." That is the *alkenes'* test (u4:c25), and it
happens rapidly in the dark without any special condition. Alkanes
need pure halogen *and* UV light, and they substitute slowly —
which is precisely why "no rapid decolourising of bromine water"
behaves as the negative result that distinguishes a saturated
hydrocarbon from an unsaturated one. Mixing up the two conditions
is one of the classic organic-chemistry mark losses.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the complete-combustion equation for methane — and
derive, not recall, the ethane one? Can you state the two products
of incomplete combustion and why one of them is dangerous to
humans? Can you give the exact condition for the substitution
reaction, name the products when methane meets bromine, and explain
why the hydrogen chloride formed is not called an acid? That is the
complete alkane reaction list — two reactions, six equations' worth
of variations, one family.
:::
