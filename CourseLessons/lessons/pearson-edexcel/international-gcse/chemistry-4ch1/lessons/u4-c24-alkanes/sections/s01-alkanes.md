The hydrocarbons that emerge from the fractional distillation of
crude oil (u4:c23) are mainly **[[alkane]]s** — and this chapter is
devoted to them, because they are the simplest family of
[[hydrocarbon]]s in chemistry. Every molecule in the family is built
from just two kinds of bond: carbon–carbon single bonds and
carbon–hydrogen single bonds. Nothing else. That one structural fact
explains almost everything you are about to read: why the family has
a neat formula, why its members behave in similar ways, why the
family is described as unreactive, and why — despite that — every
alkane will burn. The structures and reactions of these compounds
build directly on the bonding and formula work from the
introduction to organic chemistry (u4:c22).

Start with the family portrait. The small alkanes — methane, ethane,
propane, butane — are gases at room temperature, and their most
important job is the one happening around you right now: they burn
as fuels. Methane is the major component of natural gas, the
fuel that likely heats your home; ethane and propane are present in
small quantities in natural gas too, and both are important
constituents of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) from crude oil
distillation. The first five members of the family are laid out in
the table below — learn to read it, because it is the map of
everything else in this chapter.

| Name | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|
| methane | CH₄ | CH₄ |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃CH₃ |
| propane | C₃H₈ | CH₃CH₂CH₃ |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| pentane | C₅H₁₂ | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ |

Two columns of that table deserve a second look. The **molecular
formula** counts atoms: C₃H₈ tells you a propane molecule contains
3 carbon atoms and 8 hydrogen atoms, and nothing about how they are
joined. The **structural formula** shows the order of the chain:
CH₃CH₂CH₃ says carbon–carbon–carbon, with the hydrogen atoms
attached along it. A third representation exists too — the
**displayed formula** — in which every single bond is drawn as an
explicit line and every atom is shown. For methane it is a central C
with four H atoms each joined to it by one line (H–C–H on top and
bottom, H on each side); for ethane it is H–C–H above and below each
of the two carbon atoms joined by a central C–C line; and each
further member lengthens the carbon backbone by one carbon, with
hydrogen atoms filling every remaining bond. Drawing displayed
formulae for these first five alkanes, and naming the straight-chain
members, is a skill this chapter trains — specification statement
4.21 asks for exactly that, up to five carbon atoms.

Now the word that will follow the family for the rest of the
chapter: **saturated**. The alkanes are [[saturated-hydrocarbon]]s
because they contain only C–C single bonds and have no double (C=C)
or triple (C≡C) bonds anywhere in the molecule. Every carbon atom is
using all four of its bonds on four neighbours, and there is no
double bond anywhere waiting to react. Keep the definition in
exactly that shape — single bonds only — because it is the version
the mark scheme rewards.

:::key-point
The alkanes are **saturated hydrocarbons**: they contain only C–C
single bonds and no double (C=C) or triple (C≡C) bonds. A helpful
way of thinking about it is that the alkanes are *saturated with
hydrogen* — they carry the maximum number of hydrogen atoms possible
for their number of carbon atoms. The thinking aid is for your
understanding; the exam definition is the single-bonds sentence.
:::

:::prior-knowledge
This chapter assumes you have already studied the introduction to
organic chemistry (u4:c22): hydrocarbons, the C–C and C–H bonding
skeleton, and how organic formulae are written and read. The
covalent bonding model itself comes from u1:c07 and u1:c08, and the
way a molecule's size controls the strength of its
intermolecular forces — which returns in Section 5 of this
lesson to explain a beautiful property trend — was first met there.
The crude oil story (u4:c23) supplies the starting material: it is
the alkanes' industrial source.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The fuel role of the small alkanes is an energy story as much as a
chemistry one. Burning methane in a boiler, propane in a camp stove
and the LPG mixture in a rural kitchen are all the same chemical
event — C–C and C–H bonds being replaced by stronger C=O and O–H
bonds — releasing the difference as heat. Physics calls it
combustion enthalpy; geography maps where the gas pipelines run; and
Section 7 of this lesson puts the equations on the table.
:::

:::exam-hint
When a question asks *why are alkanes classified as saturated
hydrocarbons*, the mark scheme wants the bonding statement: **they
contain only single bonds** (C–C and C–H), with no C=C or C≡C
double or triple bonds. Do not write "they are full of hydrogen" or
"saturated with hydrogen" — that is a thinking aid, not a
definition, and it earns nothing on its own. If you want to use the
hydrogen-counting idea at all, bolt it on *after* the bonding
sentence, never instead of it.
:::

:::misconception
"Saturated means the molecule holds as much hydrogen as it can, so
it cannot react." The second half of that sentence is wrong twice
over. Saturated describes the *bonding* (single bonds only), not a
reaction limit — and every alkane burns readily, as Section 7 will
show. The word describes structure, not laziness: alkanes are
*saturated* yet still undergo two important reactions.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define a hydrocarbon in one sentence with the word *only*
doing its job? Can you state why alkanes are saturated in bonding
language — single C–C and C–H bonds, no C=C, no C≡C? Can you write
the molecular and structural formulae of the first five alkanes from
memory, and sketch the displayed formula of methane and ethane with
every bond drawn? If all four are yes, the rest of the chapter is
downhill.
:::
