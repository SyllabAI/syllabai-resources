The alkanes are not a random pile of similar molecules — they form
a [[homologous-series]], and that phrase has a precise meaning you
are expected to reproduce. A homologous series is a series of
compounds that:

- have the same [[functional-group]];
- have similar chemical properties;
- show a trend (a gradation) in their physical properties;
- can be described by the same [[general-formula]];
- differ from the next member by a –CH₂– unit.

Each bullet is a statement you can test against any family in
organic chemistry, and the alkanes pass all five, which is why they
are called the simplest homologous series. Move from methane to
ethane and you add exactly one –CH₂– unit; move from ethane to
propane, another –CH₂–; every step of the ladder is the same
sized step, and Sections 4 and 5 turn that regularity into a
formula and a property trend respectively.

One honest wrinkle: the alkanes do not really have a functional
group in the way other families do, because they just contain
single C–C and C–H bonds — and that C–C and C–H skeleton is
precisely what all other organic chemistry is built on. Every other
homologous series you will meet (the alkenes of u4:c25, the alcohols
of u4:c26, the carboxylic acids of u4:c27) is the alkane skeleton
with one bond upgraded into a reactive group. That is why the
alkanes are the right family to learn first: they are the plain
fabric on which all the embroidery of organic chemistry is stitched.

:::exam-hint
When a question asks about a homologous series, the safest answer
writes about the *properties* — the second and third bullets of the
definition list above. Questions often do not award separate marks
for "the same functional group" and "similar chemical properties"
as two independent statements, and a stronger single sentence earns
them together: **they have similar chemical properties because they
have the same functional group**. If a third mark is available, add
the trend statement — a gradation in physical properties as the
molecules increase in size by –CH₂– units.
:::

:::key-point
A **homologous series** is a family of compounds with the same
functional group, similar chemical properties, a gradation in
physical properties, one shared general formula, and consecutive
members that differ by a single –CH₂– unit. The alkanes are the
simplest example — their "functional group" is just the C–C and
C–H single-bond skeleton itself.
:::

:::misconception
"Every organic family must have an obviously reactive functional
group, so alkanes cannot be a homologous series." They can and
they are. The series definition asks for the *same* group across
all members — and for alkanes that shared feature is the saturated
C–C/C–H single-bond skeleton, present identically in every member.
The consequence is real and examinable: with no reactive group to
attack, alkanes are fairly unreactive, which is exactly the
chemical similarity the definition predicts.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you reproduce all five bullets of the homologous-series
definition without peeking? Can you deploy the one-sentence
properties answer — similar chemical properties *because* the same
functional group? And can you explain why the alkane skeleton
counts as the family's shared feature even though alkanes have no
reactive group? These sentences are the family passport: alkenes,
alcohols and acids will all demand them again.
:::
