Chemical properties are decided by [[functional-group]]s and bonding.
Since every member of the [[alkane]] family contains exactly the same
bonding — carbon–carbon single bonds and carbon–hydrogen single
bonds and nothing else — every member is going to behave in the
same way chemically. That is the second properties bullet of the
homologous-series definition doing its job: one shared bonding
pattern, one shared reaction behaviour. Learn how one alkane reacts
and you have, to a good approximation, learned them all.

Those strong C–C and C–H single bonds are hard to break, and so the
alkanes are **fairly unreactive** organic compounds — they are
often thought of as being quite inert. The description is
comparative, and the comparison matters. Alkanes are not inert in
the sense that they do not react with anything, like neon does;
rather, they are not very reactive *for organic compounds*, and
they really undergo only two reactions. Those two reactions —
combustion and substitution with halogens — are the whole of the
alkane reaction list at International GCSE, and they are the
business of Section 7.

Why does saturation produce this quietness? A [[saturated-hydrocarbon]]
has no double bond available as a reactive handle. Compare that
with the unsaturated alkenes coming in u4:c25, whose C=C double bond
is electron-rich and eager to attack: every dramatic organic
reaction you will later watch happens at a double bond, and the
alkanes have none. The family's saturation — statement 4.20 — is
therefore not just a structural label but the chemical explanation
of the family's restraint.

:::key-point
The alkanes are **fairly unreactive**: they are not inert in the
sense of never reacting (unlike neon), but they are not very
reactive *for organic compounds*, and they really undergo only two
reactions — combustion, and substitution with halogens. Their
strong C–C and C–H single bonds and the absence of any C=C double
bond explain the restraint.
:::

:::exam-hint
Handle the word *inert* with care — it is a comparison, not an
absolution. If a question says "alkanes are unreactive. Explain
why," the mark scheme wants the bonding argument: strong C–C and
C–H single bonds that are difficult to break, and no reactive
double bond. If a question shows a reaction happening, do not
insist the alkane "cannot react" — it can, and Section 7 gives you
both reactions it does undergo. Match the strength of your claim to
the strength of the evidence in front of you.
:::

:::misconception
"Unreactive means no reactions at all, so alkanes are chemical
dead-ends." The family has exactly two reactions and one of them
powers civilisation. What *unreactive* means here is narrower: few
reactions, and only under specific conditions — high temperature
for combustion, ultraviolet light for substitution. The quietness
is also what makes the reactions that do happen so clean to learn:
two behaviours, memorably different, shared by every member of the
family.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you explain why all alkanes share similar chemical properties
(one bonding pattern, one behaviour)? Can you qualify the word
*inert* correctly — fairly unreactive for organic compounds, not
neon-like? And can you name the two reactions the family really
undergoes before reading the next section? Hold those two names
(combustion, substitution) in mind; Section 7 now earns them their
equations.
:::
