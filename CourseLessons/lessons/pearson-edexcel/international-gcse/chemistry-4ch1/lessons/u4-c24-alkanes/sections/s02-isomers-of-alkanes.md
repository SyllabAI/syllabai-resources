Hand any two chemists the same molecular formula and they can build
different molecules from it — and that possibility has a name. An
[[isomer]] is a compound that has the same [[molecular-formula]] as
another compound but a different [[structural-formula]]. The atoms
are identical in kind and number; only their connection pattern
differs. Isomerism is one of the reasons organic chemistry has
millions of compounds while inorganic chemistry has thousands: with
a few dozen atoms to arrange, the number of legal connection
patterns explodes fast.

The first three alkanes have no isomers at all. There is exactly
one way to join one carbon to four hydrogens (methane), one way for
two carbons and six hydrogens (ethane), and one way for three
carbons and eight hydrogens (propane) — the carbon atoms have no
choice but to sit in a line. The alkanes in the table back in
Section 1 are therefore all described as **straight-chain alkanes**,
or as having an **unbranched chain**: every carbon atom sits at the
end or in the run of the chain, with no carbon hanging off the
middle of another.

From butane upwards, the choice appears. There are isomers of
butane (C₄H₁₀) and pentane (C₅H₁₂) that have **branched chains** —
chains in which one carbon atom is attached to the middle of the
chain rather than at its end. For butane, the unbranched member
chains all four carbons in a row (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃), while the branched
isomer takes three of the carbons in a chain and hangs the fourth
off the middle one, giving the structural formula CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃.
Pentane has more arrangements still: the straight chain, one
branched form with a single carbon branch on the second carbon
(CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃), and one doubly-branched form where two carbon
branches sit on the middle carbon of a three-carbon backbone
(CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₃). Each of these is a distinct compound with its own
melting point, boiling point and reaction behaviour, even though
every one of them is C₅H₁₂.

To draw a branched isomer's [[displayed-formula]] correctly, build
the carbon backbone first, count the bonds each carbon still has
spare, and fill every spare bond with a hydrogen atom. A middle
carbon bonded to two chain neighbours has two spare bonds; an end
carbon has three; a carbon carrying a branch has one. When every
carbon shows exactly four bonds and every hydrogen exactly one, the
drawing is right — and the same atom-counting discipline is what
makes the equations in Section 7 come out balanced.

:::key-point
An **isomer** is a compound with the *same* molecular formula but a
*different* structural formula compared with another compound.
Methane, ethane and propane have no isomers; butane and pentane
have branched-chain isomers in which a carbon atom attaches to the
middle of the chain instead of the end.
:::

:::exam-hint
Isomer questions are atom-counting questions in disguise. Before
drawing anything, write the molecular formula and total the atoms —
for C₄H₁₀ that is 4 carbons and 10 hydrogens, no more and no less.
Then draw the unbranched chain, and only then move one carbon to
the middle and re-fill the bonds. Finish by counting every atom in
your drawing against the formula; one hydrogen short or long means
a carbon has the wrong number of bonds, and the examiner will find
it in seconds.
:::

:::misconception
"Isomers are the same compound drawn differently." No — they are
*different compounds* that happen to share a molecular formula.
Straight-chain butane and its branched isomer have different
boiling points, different densities and slightly different
reactivities, because molecule shape controls how strongly
molecules attract each other. If two structures differ in how the
atoms are connected, they are two substances; if they differ only
in how the paper is rotated, they are one.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define an isomer using both halves of the sentence — same
molecular formula, different structural formula? Can you explain
why propane has no isomers while butane does? And can you draw the
displayed formula of a branched-chain isomer of C₄H₁₀ with all four
bonds on every carbon accounted for? Draw it now before moving on —
the substitution reactions in Section 7 will hand you isomeric
products, and you will want this skill warm.
:::
