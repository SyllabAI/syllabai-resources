Because the members of a [[homologous-series]] grow in size in a
regular way — one –CH₂– unit at a time — their physical properties
change in a regular way too, and the clearest example is the
[[boiling-point]]. Plot the boiling points of the first eight alkanes
against the number of carbon atoms and the points rise in a smooth,
regular sweep: methane boils near −162 °C, and each extra carbon
lifts the boiling point a step further. This graph-like pattern is
what the series definition means by a **trend (gradation) in
physical properties** — no member is out of line, because no member
differs from its neighbours except by the same –CH₂– unit.

The trend sorts the family into states for you. The first four
alkanes — methane, ethane, propane and butane — are gases at room
temperature (about 25 °C). All the other alkanes you are likely to
meet at International GCSE are liquids. Solids start to appear at
about C₁₈H₃₈, where the molecules are so large that the attractions
between them rarely let go at room temperature. That is why the
fuel table from Section 1 reads the way it does: the small alkanes
are gases burned as fuels (natural gas, LPG), the mid-sized members
are the liquid fuels of the petrol and diesel fractions, and the
giants are the waxy solids of the heavier fractions.

The root cause of the trend is the same force you met in covalent
bonding (u1:c08): [[intermolecular-forces]], the attractions between
one molecule and its neighbours. As the molecules become bigger,
the strength of these attractions increases — a bigger molecule
presents more surface to its neighbours and gathers more of their
attractions. More energy therefore has to be put in to break the
attractions between one molecule and the ones around it, and one
effect of that is the one the graph shows: the boiling points
increase in a regular way. Nothing about the covalent bonds *inside*
the molecule changes as the family grows — it is the
between-molecule attractions that strengthen, and the boiling point
merely reports the energy bill.

:::key-point
The boiling points of the alkanes increase regularly with molecule
size because bigger molecules have stronger
[[intermolecular-forces]]; more energy is needed to separate
neighbouring molecules, so bigger alkanes boil higher. The first
four alkanes are gases at room temperature, the IGCSE-relevant
mid-range members are liquids, and solids appear at about C₁₈H₃₈.
:::

:::exam-hint
Trend questions pay two marks for two statements, so always write
both: the *direction* (boiling point increases as molecules get
bigger) and the *cause* (stronger intermolecular forces of
attraction between the larger molecules, needing more energy to
overcome). Writing only the direction earns half; writing the cause
with the wrong direction earns nothing. And keep the force
*between* molecules — a sentence that breaks covalent bonds during
boiling loses the mark it was aiming at.
:::

:::misconception
"When a big alkane boils, its covalent bonds break and that is why
more energy is needed." No bond breaks. Boiling separates
*molecules from molecules*, and the only thing being overcome is
the intermolecular force of attraction between neighbouring
molecules. The C–C and C–H covalent bonds inside each molecule
survive the process untouched — the same molecule that was a vapour
condenses back to a liquid with its bonds intact. Energy bills
differ between sizes because the *between-molecule* attractions
differ, not because the molecules themselves change.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the direction of the boiling-point trend across the
first eight alkanes? Can you name the state of the first four
members at room temperature, the state of the mid-range members,
and the size at which solids appear? And can you explain the trend
in a two-step cause chain — bigger molecules, stronger
intermolecular forces, more energy to separate neighbours? That
cause-chain is the same one that sorted crude oil's fractions into
a boiling-order column (u4:c23, statement 4.10) — here it reappears
inside a single family, which is why the alkane ladder boils
smoothly instead of in jumps.
:::
