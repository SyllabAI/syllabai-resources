Because every step up the alkane ladder adds exactly one carbon and
two hydrogens — the –CH₂– unit from the series definition in
Section 3 — the whole family collapses into one expression. For the
alkanes, if there are *n* carbons there are 2*n* + 2 hydrogens,
which gives the [[general-formula]]:

**CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**

Substitute any whole number for *n* and the formula hands you a real
alkane. One carbon (n = 1) gives CH₄, methane; two give C₂H₆,
ethane; three give C₃H₈, propane. Check it against the table in
Section 1 and every row agrees — that is what it means for a
[[homologous-series]] to have a general formula. The formula works
because each member differs from the next by a –CH₂– unit: grow the
carbon count by one and the hydrogen count grows by exactly two,
which is the 2*n* + 2 pattern stated in algebra.

:::worked-example
Find the [[molecular-formula]] of the alkane with 15 carbon atoms.

The general formula says hydrogens = 2*n* + 2, and here *n* = 15.
So hydrogens = (2 × 15) + 2 = 32, and the answer is **C₁₅H₃₂**.
Verify the method on a case you can count by hand first: for
propane, *n* = 3 gives (2 × 3) + 2 = 8 hydrogens — C₃H₈, exactly as
the table in Section 1 shows. The same one-line method produces any
member of the family, which is why exam questions like to name
alkanes with large carbon counts: undecane (11 carbons) is C₁₁H₂₄
without a table in sight.
:::

The power of a general formula runs both ways. Given a name and a
carbon count, it produces the formula (the worked example above);
given a formula, it confirms family membership. Any compound whose
formula fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for some whole number *n* is an alkane — the
formula is a fingerprint. An alkane with 11
carbons must be C₁₁H₂₄; a formula like C₃H₆ fails the test
(2 × 3 + 2 = 8, not 6), and that failure is meaningful: C₃H₆ is not
a member of *this* family (it belongs to the alkenes of u4:c25, and
the missing two hydrogens are exactly what its C=C double bond
implies).

The same regularity is visible in the pictures. Draw the displayed
formulae of the three smallest alkanes — methane, ethane, propane —
side by side and each one is the previous drawing with one more
carbon inserted into the backbone and two more hydrogens packed on:
methane a single carbon crowned with four hydrogens, ethane two
carbons sharing one bond, propane three carbons sharing two. Each
member differs from the next by exactly a –CH₂– unit, which is the
structural reason the formula arithmetic works.

:::cross-curricular
The general formula is algebra wearing a chemistry coat. The
expression C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub> is a function: input an
integer, output a compound. This is the same move mathematics makes
when it writes the *n*th term of a sequence — here the sequence is
CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀, C₅H₁₂, … and the "term-to-term rule" is
"add CH₂". When you meet formulae like this across science, you are
looking at one of the deepest ideas in the subject: a family too
large to list can be captured in one line of algebra.
:::

:::misconception
"A formula that fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ always describes exactly one
compound, so butane must be a single substance." The general formula counts
atoms; it does not fix how they are joined. C₄H₁₀ fits the
formula perfectly and is TWO compounds — the straight chain and its branched
isomer of Section 2. The formula is a family fingerprint, not a structural
diagram: it narrows the world to the alkanes, and the isomer skill then
distinguishes the members within it.
:::

:::exam-hint
The general formula is your marking-proof checking tool. After
balancing any alkane combustion equation in Section 7 or answering
any "write the molecular formula of ..." item, run the formula
through CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: if your alkane's hydrogen
count does not equal twice the carbon count plus two, something is
wrong — a mis-drawn structure, a lost subscript or an unbalanced
equation upstream. Questions love the "undecane" style (a named
alkane with a carbon count you must decode from the name, not the
table): undecane has 11 carbons (uni- = one, dec- = ten), so its
formula is C₁₁H₂₄ by the general formula alone.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the general formula of the alkanes and explain what
*n* stands for? Can you derive the formula of the alkane with 15
carbons in one line of arithmetic? Can you use the formula in
reverse — to test whether C₃H₆ could be an alkane — and say which
family it actually belongs to? And can you connect the formula back
to the –CH₂– unit rule from the homologous-series definition? Four
yeses here and 4.19 is banked.
:::
