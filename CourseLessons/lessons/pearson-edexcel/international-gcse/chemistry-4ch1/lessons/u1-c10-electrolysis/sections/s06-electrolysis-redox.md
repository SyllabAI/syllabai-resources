Look again at the two lead(II) bromide half-equations and classify them by
the electron ledger, and you have the redox definitions used across the
whole course:

- **[[oxidation]] occurs when something loses electrons.**
- **[[reduction]] occurs when something gains electrons.**

The memory hook is **OILRIG**: **O**xidation **I**s **L**oss (of electrons),
**R**eduction **I**s **G**ain (of electrons). Applied to the electrolysis:
Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb is **reduction** — the lead ions gain electrons and are
reduced to lead atoms. 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ is **oxidation** — the bromide ions
lose electrons and are oxidised to bromine molecules. Because one species
losing electrons means another species gaining them, oxidation and reduction
always occur together, and such reactions are called **[[redox]]**
(re**d**uction + **ox**idation). Each half-equation shows only one of the two
processes — hence the name — and in the exam you will be asked to write
**ionic [[half-equation]]s** for the reactions at the electrodes and label each
as oxidation or reduction.

**Other molten substances — the half-equation bank grows.** Electrolyse
molten sodium chloride and you get sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the
anode:

- cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na  *(reduction — sodium ions reduced)*
- anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻  *(oxidation — chloride ions oxidised)*

The 2 in the anode equation is the charge-balance rule in action
(section 4): two 1− ions must shed two electrons to build one Cl₂ molecule,
balancing atoms *and* charge. Molten aluminium oxide gives aluminium at the
cathode and oxygen at the anode. Aluminium sits in Group 3, so it forms 3+
ions, and cancelling a 3+ charge takes three electrons:

- cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al  *(reduction)* — total charge 0 on both sides
  (3+ combined with 3−);
- anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻  *(oxidation)* — each oxide ion carries 2 extra
  electrons, and oxygen atoms pair into O₂, so two ions must shed 4 electrons
  between them.

Molten zinc chloride behaves exactly like its sodium-chloride template:
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn at the cathode, 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode. From these
cases come the two generalisations that predict **every** two-element molten
ionic compound: the **metal appears at the cathode** (metals form positive
ions) and the **non-metal at the anode** (non-metals form negative ions); and
**reduction always occurs at the cathode, oxidation always at the anode**.
The mnemonics do this job: **AN OX / RED CAT** — anode oxidation, reduction
cathode. And the ion names from section 2 now earn their origin story:
positive ions are called **[[cation]]s because they are attracted to the
cathode**; negative ions are called **[[anion]]s because they are attracted
to the anode**. Name, electrode, one-to-one.

**The aqueous pivot.** Not every ionic compound can be melted and split: some
decompose before their melting point — copper(II) carbonate breaks into
copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide on gentle heating, so it can never be
electrolysed as a melt. Solutions rescue the situation, but water changes the
game: **when aqueous solutions are electrolysed, the products are not always
the same as when molten salts are.** Water itself is a **[[weak-electrolyte]]** — it ionises very slightly, splitting into hydrogen
ions and hydroxide ions which constantly recombine (the reaction is
reversible). Wherever water is present, those ions compete in the electrolysis
alongside the compound's own ions — and which ion wins the [[discharge]]
competition is decided by the **[[reactivity-series]]**: the more reactive an
element, the greater its tendency to form an ion, and the harder it is to
turn that ion back into an atom. For positive ions, the further down the
reactivity series an element sits, the more easily its ion accepts an
electron.

The apparatus for investigating solution electrolysis (spec 1.60C): an
electrolytic cell — a glass tube fitted with a rubber bung holding two carbon
electrodes — is filled with the solution (concentrated sodium chloride, say),
a small test-tube of the same solution is placed over each electrode (not
fully covering them, or ion flow stops and the current dies), and the
electrodes are wired to a battery or power pack. Bubbles of gas at either
electrode are the sign that something is happening; the gases are collected
and tested. For concentrated sodium chloride solution the results: **hydrogen
at the cathode, chlorine at the anode**. Expect equal volumes in theory but
slightly **less chlorine than expected in practice** — chlorine is more
soluble in water than hydrogen is, so some of it dissolves before it can be
collected (the same solubility quirk, in mirror image, explains section 9's
oxygen shortfall). And chlorine changes everything about the experiment's
handling:

:::safety
Wear eye protection throughout. Chlorine is poisonous — run chlorine-producing
electrolyses in a fume cupboard or well-ventilated room, and do not smell the
products (particularly hazardous for people with asthma). Once chlorine is
detected, switch the current off.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two marks hide in this section that almost every candidate collects.
**Charge-balance check**: after writing any half-equation, add the charges on
both sides — 2O²⁻ (4−) → O₂ + 4e⁻ (4−) balances; an unbalanced total charge
means the electron count is wrong. And the "either equation" courtesy: for
solution electrolyses you may see the hydrogen half-equation written from H⁺
ions (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂) or from water molecules directly (2H₂O + 2e⁻ →
H₂ + 2OH⁻) — both are accepted in answers. The second form earns its keep in
the next section, where it explains why the solution near the cathode turns
alkaline.
:::

:::misconception
"Electrons are lost at the anode, so the anode must be negative — negative
things gain electrons." The sign of an electrode is set by the **power
supply**, not by what lands on it: the supply pulls electrons out of the
anode, making it positive, and anions crawling to it are exactly the species
whose extra electrons it can absorb. Oxidation at a positive electrode sounds
backwards until you separate the two questions — who is short of electrons
(the anode, by construction) and who has electrons to give (the anions, by
charge). AN OX / RED CAT keeps the pairing straight.
:::
