Every solution electrolysis of the last three sections was one instance of
two rules, and stated plainly, the rules predict **any** solution the exam
can name (spec 1.58C):

- **Cathode (−):** if the metal is **high in the [[reactivity-series]]** (above
  hydrogen), **hydrogen** is produced instead of the metal; if the metal is
  **below hydrogen**, **the metal itself** is produced.
- **Anode (+):** for solutions of **halides** — chlorides, bromides,
  iodides — the **halogen** (chlorine, bromine or iodine) is produced; for
  solutions of other common negative ions — **sulfate, nitrate, hydroxide**
  — **oxygen** is produced (from the water's hydroxide ions).

The rulebook at work, one table, four solutions with inert (carbon or
platinum) electrodes:

| Solution | Cathode product | Cathode half-equation | Anode product | Anode half-equation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KI(aq) | hydrogen | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | iodine | 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ |
| MgBr₂(aq) | hydrogen | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | bromine | 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ |
| H₂SO₄(aq) | hydrogen | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | oxygen | 4OH⁻ → 2H₂O + O₂ + 4e⁻ |
| CuSO₄(aq) | copper | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | oxygen | 4OH⁻ → 2H₂O + O₂ + 4e⁻ |

Check each row against the rules: potassium and magnesium sit above hydrogen
(hydrogen wins the cathode); copper sits below (copper wins); iodide and
bromide are halides (halogen at the anode); sulfate is not (oxygen at the
anode). Two honest exceptions to keep in view: the covalent substances that
*do* conduct in solution because they ionise in water — the **acids and
ammonia solution** — and the reason every question hammers the word
**inert**. For electrolysis to work at all, **ions must be present** and free
to move: the external circuit can carry current only if the [[electrolyte]]
completes it. Electrolyse a covalent molecular substance such as sugar
solution (or molten sugar) and nothing happens — no ions, no current, no
decomposition. Sugar is a **[[non-electrolyte]]**. Flip that logic and you
own a diagnostic: **if a substance undergoes electrolysis, molten or in
solution, it must contain ions; if it does not, it has none.** Electrolysis
is itself the test for ionic character.

**The quantitative experiment (spec 1.60C).** "How much?" can be measured:
electrolysis products are produced in amounts that depend on the current, and
the experiment that shows it is a planning favourite. The apparatus extends
the solution-electrolysis cell with three measuring instruments — a
**variable resistor** to set the current, an **ammeter** to read it, and a
**gas burette** (or measuring cylinder) over the cathode to collect and
measure the hydrogen. The procedure, as a variables-and-controls script:

- pour 50 cm³ of concentrated sodium chloride solution into the cell and
  place the gas burette over the cathode;
- switch on and **set the current to 0.2 A with the variable resistor**
  (reading taken from the ammeter — the current must be flowing before the
  burette can be read, so the initial burette reading is taken after
  switching on);
- run for **5 minutes** (timed), then record the volume of hydrogen
  collected;
- **repeat for currents of 0.4 A, 0.6 A, 0.8 A and 1.0 A** — the independent
  variable is the current, the dependent variable the gas volume, and the
  controlled variables include the solution volume and concentration and the
  electrolysis time;
- **repeat each experiment** and average, to improve reliability.

A representative set of results:

| Current / A | Volume of hydrogen / cm³ |
|---|---|
| 0.2 | 7.0 |
| 0.4 | 13.9 |
| 0.6 | 15.1 |
| 0.8 | 28.0 |
| 1.0 | 34.9 |

Current is continuous data, so the graph is drawn as a **line of best fit**
— and here sits the analysis skill the examiner wants: the 0.6 A reading of
15.1 cm³ is **anomalous** (too low; the current may have dipped or some gas
escaped), so it is **excluded when drawing the line of best fit**. The
remaining points sit on a straight line **through the origin**, and a
straight line through the origin is the signature of a **directly
proportional** relationship: **the volume of gas produced is directly
proportional to the current passed.** Bigger current, more electrons per
second, more product — the chemistry's electron ledger made visible in a
graph.

:::summary
Electrolysis is the chemical change caused by passing a current through a
molten compound or solution — possible only where ions are free to move, and
driven at two electrodes: the **positive anode**, where anions are
discharged and **oxidation** happens, and the **negative cathode**, where
cations are discharged and **reduction** happens (OILRIG; AN OX / RED CAT).
Molten two-element compounds split into metal at the cathode and non-metal
at the anode, each event written as a charge-balanced ionic half-equation.
In solution, water joins as a weak electrolyte and the reactivity series
decides: above hydrogen → hydrogen at the cathode; below → the metal;
halides → halogen at the anode, other ions → oxygen. The products obey a
measurable law: gas volume directly proportional to current — electrolysis
as electron bookkeeping made visible.
:::

:::cross-curricular
Electrolysis is one of the load-bearing processes of modern industry. The
aluminium in aircraft and drink cans is won from its ore by molten electrolysis
— chemical reduction cannot manage it, and the lesson's Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
half-equation is the industrial reaction, run at enormous scale. The brine
electrolysis of section 7 supplies chlorine for water treatment and plastics,
hydrogen for the chemical industry, and sodium hydroxide for soap — three
products, one cell. Electroplating uses the copper-style cathode deposition to
coat objects with metal. And the quantitative law you plotted — product
amount proportional to current — is the working rule every electroplater and
chlorine plant operator sets their dials by.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection. Where chlorine is produced (concentrated chloride
solutions), work in a fume cupboard or well-ventilated room, do not smell the
gas — a particular hazard for people with asthma — and switch the current off
as soon as chlorine is detected.
:::

:::exam-hint
The graph question is where this chapter quietly examines experimental
skills: name the anomaly, justify excluding it from the line of best fit,
state the proportional conclusion, and — the top mark — connect the physics
to the chemistry (more current = more electrons per second = more product,
because each product molecule costs a fixed number of electrons). When
predicting unfamiliar products, quote the rules *and* the ionic contest they
summarise; the rule earns the mark, the contest explains it if the question
pushes to "why".
:::

:::misconception
"The anomalous 0.6 A point should be joined up with the others — excluding
data sounds like cheating." Discarding an anomaly from a line of best fit is
not dishonesty; it is the standard treatment of a known bad reading, provided
you say so. The line of best fit describes the *trend* supported by the
reliable points; one suspect reading (15.1 cm³ when the trend says roughly
21 cm³) would drag the line off and hide the proportionality that four other
points agree on. The dishonest options are the other two: keeping the point
and faking the line, or deleting the reading without declaring it.
:::
