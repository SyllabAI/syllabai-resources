Lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂, is the set-piece experiment of spec 1.58C, and every
observation in it is explained by one idea from section 2 — ions locked in a
solid, freed by melting. The experiment: carbon electrodes dip into solid
PbBr₂ in a crucible, wired through a bulb to a power supply, and a Bunsen
burner heats the crucible. **Nothing at all happens until the lead(II)
bromide melts.** The moment it melts, four things occur, and you should be
able to narrate them in order:

- **the bulb lights** — electrons are flowing round the external circuit, so
  the circuit is complete: mobile charge carriers now exist inside the
  crucible;
- **bubbling and brown vapour appear at the anode** — the positive electrode,
  connected to the positive terminal, gives off brown bromine gas;
- **metallic lead collects at the cathode** — the negative electrode shows
  nothing dramatic while running, but underneath it, silvery molten lead is
  found;
- **when heating stops and the PbBr₂ solidifies again, everything stops** —
  no more bubbling, the bulb goes out. Refreeze the ions into the lattice and
  the circuit dies.

The explanation runs straight off the ionic model. Solid PbBr₂ is a giant
lattice of Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ ions packed in regular rows — the ions are locked
tightly in place, so the solid does not conduct and nothing happens. Melting
frees the ions to move. The power supply then creates the electron imbalance
at the electrodes (section 4), and the two ion populations walk to their
doom.

**At the cathode (−):** the positive Pb²⁺ ions are attracted to it, and each
arriving ion picks up two electrons from the electrode surface. Neutral lead
atoms form, and being dense molten metal, they pool underneath the electrode:

Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb  *(reduction — gain of electrons)*

**At the anode (+):** the negative Br⁻ ions are attracted to it, and each
arriving ion's extra electron moves onto the electron-starved electrode.
Neutral bromine atoms form:

Br⁻ → Br + e⁻  *(oxidation — loss of electrons)*

The bromine atoms do not stay single. A bromine atom has only 7 outer-shell
electrons, so each one joins to a partner through a covalent bond, giving
both atoms a full outer shell of 8 — bromine molecules, Br₂:

2Br → Br₂, and the overall anode **[[half-equation]]** is  2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻

The new electrons on the anode flow away through the external circuit — which
is precisely why the bulb lights. Electrons in the wire; ions in the melt;
handover at the surfaces.

Now the vocabulary discipline that examiners police. We say the ions are
**[[discharge]]d** at the electrodes — discharge means the ion either gives
up electron(s) to the electrode or receives electron(s) from it, losing its
charge and becoming an atom. And keep the species names straight: **bromide
ions** travel to the anode and are converted into **bromine molecules** —
"bromide" and "bromine" name different species (ion versus molecule), and
mixing them in a written answer is a mark silently dropped. The same
precision applies at the cathode: lead ions are reduced to lead atoms.

:::key-point
Two carriers, one loop — the sentence examiners want for the bulb:
**electrons flow in the external circuit** (wire, power pack, bulb and the
electrode surfaces themselves), while **ions flow in the electrolyte** (the
molten compound). Neither carrier can cross the electrolyte; the electron
bookkeeping is completed by the discharge reactions at the two electrode
surfaces. Solid electrolyte = locked ions = no carriers = dark bulb.
:::

:::exam-hint
The lead(II) bromide question is a chain, and each link is its own mark:
(1) *why nothing happens while solid* — ions are held in fixed positions in
the lattice, not free to move; (2) *why the bulb lights on melting* — ions
become free to move and carry charge through the electrolyte, completing the
circuit; (3) *products* — bromine at the anode, lead at the cathode, each
with its half-equation; (4) *why it stops on cooling* — the ions return to
fixed lattice positions. Learn the chain as a script; the half-equations are
the two you met in section 4 with real species.
:::

:::misconception
"The lead forms at the anode because lead is dense and sinks to the bottom
where the anode is." Product placement is decided by **charge, not density**:
positive lead(II) ions are pulled to the negative cathode, where they gain
electrons and become lead atoms. Density only explains *where the lead
collects* (underneath the cathode, as molten metal pools), never *which
electrode it forms at*. Pair every product with its ion's charge — positive
ion → cathode, negative ion → anode — and placement errors disappear.
:::
