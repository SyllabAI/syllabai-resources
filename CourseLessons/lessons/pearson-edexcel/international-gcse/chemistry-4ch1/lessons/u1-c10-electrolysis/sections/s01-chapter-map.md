Connect a piece of copper to a battery and a complete circuit: the copper
warms up slightly, and nothing else happens. Connect a solution of potassium
iodide into the same kind of circuit instead, and something genuinely new
occurs — a chemical change. Bubbles of hydrogen gas appear at one electrode,
purple iodine is produced at the other, and neither substance was there
before. That contrast is the whole business of this chapter: electricity
passing through some substances just makes them warm, while electricity
passing through others **tears them apart into new substances**. The process
that does the tearing is **electrolysis**, and by the end of the lesson you
will be able to predict its products for any compound the exam throws at you.

Every objective of record for this lesson is a **chemistry-only**
specification statement — assessed in **Paper 2 (2C) only**; none of them
appears on Paper 1 (1C), and none is shared with Science Double Award:

- understand why **covalent compounds do not conduct** electricity
  (spec 1.55C);
- understand why **ionic compounds conduct only when molten or in aqueous
  solution** (spec 1.56C);
- know that **anion** and **cation** name negative and positive ions
  respectively (spec 1.57C);
- **describe experiments** to investigate electrolysis, using inert
  electrodes, of molten compounds (including lead(II) bromide) and aqueous
  solutions (including sodium chloride, dilute sulfuric acid and copper(II)
  sulfate) — and **predict the products** (spec 1.58C);
- **write ionic half-equations** for the electrode reactions and classify
  them as oxidation or reduction (spec 1.59C);
- **practical**: investigate the electrolysis of aqueous solutions
  (spec 1.60C).

The lesson runs in four movements after this map. First, the conduction
rulebook — which structures carry a current and which refuse, and why, ending
in the definitions of electrolysis itself and the apparatus it needs. Second,
the molten-compound case study: molten lead(II) bromide, every observation
explained, and the redox language (oxidation, reduction, OILRIG) that grades
the reactions at the two electrodes. Third, the aqueous case studies — sodium
chloride, copper(II) sulfate and dilute sulfuric acid solutions — where water
itself joins the competition and changes the products, closing with the
prediction rules that work for every solution. Fourth, the quantitative
experiment: measuring how the volume of gas produced depends on the current,
and reading a proportional relationship off a graph. Work through the
sections in order — each movement's equipment feeds directly into the next.

:::prior-knowledge
Four pieces of earlier work carry this lesson. **Ionic bonding** from
Chapter 7 (u1:c07) — ionic compounds are lattices of positive and negative
ions, and in the solid state those ions are locked in place. **Covalent
bonding** from Chapter 8 (u1:c08) — covalent molecular substances are made of
individual molecules with their electrons held tightly in atoms and bonds.
**Metallic bonding** from Chapter 9 (u1:c09) — metals conduct because
delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice. **Charges and
formulae** from Chapters 5 and 6 — you must write ion charges instantly
(Pb²⁺, Br⁻, Al³⁺, O²⁻), because half-equations are charge bookkeeping.
:::

:::exam-hint
Everything here is chemistry-only: Paper 2C territory, never Paper 1C, and
Double Award candidates can set the chapter aside. The question shapes are
extremely predictable: state the products at each electrode; explain why the
solid did not conduct but the melt did; write and balance a half-equation and
label it oxidation or reduction; predict products for an unfamiliar solution
from the reactivity rules; and describe or interpret the quantitative
experiment. Learn the half-equations as a fixed set and the prediction rules
as two sentences — the marks follow mechanically.
:::

:::misconception
"Passing electricity through any substance causes a chemical change." The
copper wire at the start of this section is the standing counterexample: it
conducts, it warms, and **nothing new is made**, because conduction in a metal
is electrons drifting through a structure that stays intact. A chemical change
needs a structure that is **broken apart by the process** — that requires
mobile **ions**, and only molten or dissolved ionic compounds (plus a few
covalent exceptions) offer them. Conduction and decomposition are different
events; this chapter is about the second.
:::

Question-bank join: spec 1.55C–1.60C items across the whole lesson — the

:::
