One rule runs every conduction question: **for a substance to
conduct electricity it must contain charged particles, and those charged
particles must be free to move.** Both conditions matter. Charged but
immobile conducts nothing; mobile but neutral conducts nothing either. The
charged particles are always one of two kinds — **electrons** or **ions** —
and the first thing to decide in any conduction question is which of the two
is doing the moving in the structure in front of you.

**Metals** conduct because the moving particles are electrons. The metal
structure is a [[lattice]] of positive ions surrounded by a sea of
[[delocalised-electron]]s, and those delocalised electrons are free to move
throughout the whole structure — that is why a solid metal wire carries a
current with no fuss and no chemical change. **Ionic compounds** behave
completely differently. In the **solid** state their ions are held tightly in
position in the lattice — they can vibrate on the spot, but they cannot leave
their lattice positions — so although charged particles are present, they are
not free to move, and the solid does not conduct. **Melt** the compound, or
**dissolve** it in water, and the ions break loose: now they are free to move
around, and the molten liquid or aqueous solution conducts. The ions do not
drift aimlessly, though — under the pull of a power supply they move
purposefully towards the electrodes, and that purposeful movement is exactly
what makes the next section's chemical changes possible.

That mobility story is also where the two ion names come from, and both names
must be used precisely:

- **[[anion]]** — a **negative** ion (think *A* for the *A*-minus charge:
  anions crawl to the positive electrode);
- **[[cation]]** — a **positive** ion.

Where each name comes from — the electrodes themselves — is a story the
lesson returns to once the electrodes have been introduced properly.

**Covalent molecular substances** are the third structure type, and they are
the refuseniks. Substances such as methane (CH₄), carbon dioxide (CO₂) and
hexane consist of **individual molecules** with no overall electrical charge
and no ions anywhere: every electron is held tightly either in an atom or in
a covalent bond between atoms, and none can move from molecule to molecule.
No charged particles, no movement, no conduction — **in any state, solid,
liquid or gas, and in solution too**. That triple refusal is what spec 1.55C
asks you to explain, and the explanation is always the same two-clause
sentence: molecules carry no overall charge, and the electrons are not free
to move.

There is one honest crack in the covalent wall, and examiners love it. A few
covalent substances **react with water to form ions**, and once ions exist,
the solution conducts. Ammonia dissolves in water to give a solution
containing ammonium ions and hydroxide ions. Hydrogen chloride gas dissolves
in water to form hydrochloric acid, HCl(aq), in which the HCl has ionised.
The conduction there is real — but it is ionic conduction borrowed through a
chemical reaction with water, not molecular conduction. The molecules
themselves never conducted anything.

:::key-point
The conduction rulebook in one box. **Metals**: conduct solid and molten —
delocalised electrons are the mobile charge carriers. **Ionic compounds**:
do not conduct solid (ions locked in the lattice); conduct when **molten or
in aqueous solution** (ions free to move). **Covalent molecular compounds**:
never conduct — molecules are uncharged and the electrons are locked in atoms
and bonds — except when they react with water to make ions (ammonia solution,
hydrochloric acid). Say *which* particle moves in every answer: electrons
there, ions here.
:::

:::exam-hint
The classic question hands you a table of substances and states — solid
sodium chloride, molten sodium chloride, solid copper, aqueous copper(II)
sulfate — and asks which conduct. Answer with the rulebook, and always name
the mobile particle: solid ionic does not conduct because "the ions are held
in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move"; molten or aqueous does
because "the ions are free to move". Covalent molecular never conducts
because "there are no charged particles — the molecules are neutral and the
electrons are held in atoms and bonds". Presence of ions is not enough:
**mobility** is the mark-winning word.
:::

:::misconception
"Solid sodium chloride conducts because it is full of ions." It is indeed
full of ions — and it still does not conduct, because every one of those ions
is locked into its lattice position and can only vibrate on the spot. The
conduction rule needs charged particles **and** freedom to move, and the
solid state fails the second condition. Melting or dissolving is what buys
the freedom. Learners who write only "it has ions" for the solid case lose
the mark every time; write "ions present but not free to move" and the mark
is yours.
:::
