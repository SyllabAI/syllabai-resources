Copper(II) sulfate solution, CuSO₄(aq), is blue — the colour of its copper
ions — and that colour is the experiment's built-in indicator. The solution
holds four ions: Cu²⁺(aq) and SO₄²⁻(aq) from the salt, H⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq)
from the water. Run the electrolysis with **inert** (carbon or platinum)
electrodes and watch which ions win each contest.

**At the cathode**, the copper(II) ions and hydrogen ions both arrive.
Copper sits **below hydrogen in the [[reactivity-series]]**, which flips the
sodium case of the last section: an ion of a less reactive metal accepts
electrons easily, so the copper(II) ions are [[discharge]]d in preference to the
hydrogen ions. The cathode becomes coated with **pink-brown copper**:

Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)  *(reduction)*

**At the anode**, sulfate ions and hydroxide ions both arrive — but sulfate
ions are not easy to oxidise at all — they resist [[oxidation]]. The hydroxide ions from the water are
oxidised instead, giving oxygen gas:

4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻  *(oxidation)*

As with the sodium chloride cathode, there is an alternative form written
straight from water molecules — and this one reveals what happens to the
solution near the anode:

2H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻  *(oxidation)*

Because this equation **produces** hydrogen ions, the solution becomes
**acidic in the region around the anode** — the mirror image of the alkaline
cathode region in the brine experiment.

Now run the electrolysis for a long time and track the colour. The copper
ions are being removed at the cathode and nothing replaces them, so **the
blue colour fades from blue to colourless** as the copper(II) ions are used
up. Then the residue question — what is left in the solution? The ions
originally present were Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ (from the salt) and H⁺ and OH⁻ (from
the water). Copper ions and hydroxide ions are discharged at the electrodes;
hydrogen ions and sulfate ions are not. The leftover ions are H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ —
**the solution has become dilute sulfuric acid**, and from that point the
electrolysis simply continues as the dilute sulfuric acid case of the next
section. The colour-to-colourless fade is the exam's favourite observation
in this experiment; the acid residue is its favourite follow-up.

This experiment doubles as evidence about water itself: collecting hydrogen
at the cathode and oxygen at the anode from solutions where water is the
shared reactant is a classic route to showing that water contains hydrogen
and oxygen — the building blocks of H₂O.

One loose end — what if the dissolved metal is neither above hydrogen like
sodium nor below like copper, but in between, such as **zinc**? For a
moderately reactive metal the answer depends on concentration: reasonably
concentrated solutions give the metal; very dilute solutions give mainly
hydrogen; in between, you get both. The International GCSE keeps its
examples in the clear-cut cases — sodium (hydrogen) and copper (metal) — but
now you know why the rule is a rule about *reactivity*, not an arbitrary
list.

:::key-point
The two-contest summary for copper(II) sulfate solution: **cathode — copper
wins because copper is below hydrogen (less reactive metals' ions reduce
easily)**; anode — hydroxide wins because sulfate refuses to oxidise. Blue
fades to colourless; pink-brown copper builds on the cathode; colourless gas
(oxygen) bubbles at the anode; the final solution is dilute sulfuric acid.
Four observations, four explanations, one reactivity-series rule.
:::

:::exam-hint
This is the observation-to-equation question in its purest form: state what
you would *see* at each electrode (pink-brown coating forming on the cathode;
bubbles of oxygen at the anode; the blue colour fading), then write the
half-equation that explains each observation. Remember the state-symbol
detail in the cathode equation — Cu(s) — because the solid deposit is the
observation. And if the question asks what remains after long electrolysis,
answer in ions first (H⁺ and SO₄²⁻) and only then name the compound (dilute
sulfuric acid): the ionic answer shows the mechanism.
:::

:::misconception
"Copper(II) sulfate solution gives hydrogen at the cathode — all solutions
do." Only solutions of metals **more reactive than hydrogen** surrender the
cathode to hydrogen. Copper is below hydrogen, so its ions are the easy
reduction target and the metal plates out. The reactivity series is the
arbiter in every solution electrolysis: above hydrogen → hydrogen; below
hydrogen → metal. Sodium chloride and copper(II) sulfate, side by side, are
the two exam-standard demonstrations of exactly this split.
:::
