Electrolyse **molten** sodium chloride and the products are the ones the
molten rulebook promises: sodium at the cathode, chlorine at the anode.
Electrolyse sodium chloride **solution** and only half the story survives:
**chlorine is still formed at the anode, but hydrogen is produced at the
cathode instead of sodium.** The chlorine comes from the compound's own
chloride ions — but the hydrogen comes from the **water**. That single change
of product is the doorway into every solution electrolysis in this chapter.

Why does water get a vote? Because water is a [[weak-electrolyte]] (section 6):
it ionises very slightly into hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions. So the
solution actually contains four ions — Na⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) from the salt,
H⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq) from the water — and both positive ions arrive at the
cathode together. Which one is [[discharge]]d is decided by the [[reactivity-series]]
rule: **the more reactive the metal, the harder it is to convert its ion back
into an atom.** Sodium is a very reactive metal, so adding an electron to a
Na⁺ ion is extremely difficult; hydrogen is less reactive than sodium, so a
hydrogen ion accepts an electron easily. Hydrogen wins the competition, and
each hydrogen atom formed pairs up with another:

2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)  *(reduction)*

The hydrogen ions are taken from water molecules splitting up — and each
ionisation that released an H⁺ also released an OH⁻. With the hydrogen ions
being discharged, the hydroxide ions are left accumulating around the
cathode, so **the solution becomes alkaline in the region around that
electrode**. You can see the same story told in a single line by writing the
cathode **[[half-equation]]** straight from water molecules:

2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq)  *(reduction)*

Both forms are accepted in answers; the water form shows the alkalinity
outright because OH⁻ appears as a product.

**At the anode**, chloride ions and hydroxide ions both arrive. The contest
here is close: it is slightly easier to oxidise hydroxide ions than chloride
ions, but there is no great difference — and there are **many, many more
chloride ions** present in a concentrated solution, so it is mainly the
chloride ions that are oxidised to chlorine:

2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻  *(oxidation)*

Two refinements complete the picture. In a **dilute** sodium chloride
solution, noticeable amounts of **oxygen** appear at the anode alongside the
chlorine, also coming from the hydroxide ions. And the reason every question
stresses [[inert-electrode]]s: the electrode material can change the
products. With **copper** electrodes, oxygen is not produced at the anode at
all — the anode itself gets smaller as the copper goes into solution. Carbon
or platinum electrodes keep the products predictable.

Finally, the long-game question: electrolyse concentrated sodium chloride
solution for a long time and what is left in solution? The ions present were
Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (from the salt) and H⁺ and OH⁻ (from the water). Hydrogen ions
and chloride ions are removed from the solution by discharge at the
electrodes. That leaves **Na⁺ and OH⁻ — sodium hydroxide solution**. The
electrolysis of brine, run industrially, makes all three products: hydrogen,
chlorine and sodium hydroxide.

:::key-point
The one-sentence prediction rule this section teaches: **at the cathode, if
the metal is more reactive than hydrogen, hydrogen is produced instead of
the metal.** Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and aluminium solutions
all yield hydrogen; copper and the metals below hydrogen yield the metal
itself. The hydrogen always comes from the water — a reminder that the
solvent is a player, not a spectator.
:::

:::exam-hint
The observation marks in this experiment are as bankable as the equations:
hydrogen bubbles at the cathode (tested with a lit splint — squeaky pop),
chlorine at the anode (bleaches damp litmus), and the solution turning
**alkaline near the cathode**. If asked to explain the alkalinity, quote the
water-form half-equation — H⁺ discharged leaves OH⁻ behind — rather than
asserting "sodium hydroxide forms" without the mechanism. And expect the
molten-versus-solution comparison as a two-part question: sodium from the
melt, hydrogen from the solution, same anode product both times.
:::

:::misconception
"Electrolysing sodium chloride solution gives sodium, because the compound
is sodium chloride." The compound's identity decides the **anode** product
(chlorine) but not the cathode one: at the cathode the contest is between
the metal ion and the water's hydrogen ions, and the **more reactive metal
loses** — its ion is too hard to reduce. Sodium's own reactivity, the very
property that makes it a famous metal, is precisely what disqualifies it
here. Hydrogen appears in its place, from water.
:::
