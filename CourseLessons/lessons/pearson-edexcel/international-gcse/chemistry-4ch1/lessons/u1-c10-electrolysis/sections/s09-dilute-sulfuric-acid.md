Dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄(aq), strips the contest down to its simplest
form. The solution's ions: H⁺(aq) and SO₄²⁻(aq) from the acid, plus H⁺(aq)
and OH⁻(aq) from the water. At the cathode the only positive ions arriving
are hydrogen ions — from both the acid and the water — so there is no
competition at all: they are [[discharge]]d directly, pairing into molecules as
they form:

2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)  *(reduction)*

At the anode the sulfate ions arrive with the hydroxide ions, and exactly as
in the copper(II) sulfate experiment, the sulfate ions are too difficult to
oxidise. The hydroxide ions from the water give up their extra electrons
instead, yielding oxygen:

4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻  *(oxidation)*

So dilute sulfuric acid electrolysis is really the **electrolysis of water**,
with the acid's role being to carry the current: **hydrogen at the cathode,
oxygen at the anode, and nothing consumed but water.**

The volume story is the part examiners examine. Read the two
**[[half-equation]]s** as an electron ledger: every 4 electrons flowing round the circuit buy
**1 molecule of O₂** at the anode — but the same 4 electrons would have
produced **2 molecules of H₂** at the cathode (each H₂ molecule costing 2
electrons). Twice the molecules, twice the volume: **twice as much hydrogen
is produced as oxygen.** Equal numbers of gas molecules occupy equal volumes
at the same temperature and pressure, so the ratio of the equations carries
straight through to the gas syringes.

Reality adds one honest footnote: when the experiment is actually done, the
hydrogen collected is **more than twice** the oxygen. Oxygen is slightly
more soluble in water than hydrogen, so a little of it dissolves away before
it can be collected. The standard fix is procedural, and describing it earns
its mark: run the electrolysis for a few minutes **before** starting to
collect, letting the water become saturated with dissolved oxygen — then
collect the gases, and the measured ratio improves dramatically.

:::key-point
The 2 : 1 ratio is a half-equation argument, not a memory item: 4 electrons
→ 2H₂ (cathode) but only 1O₂ (anode), and equal molecule counts mean equal
volumes for gases. Whenever a question asks "explain why twice as much
hydrogen is produced", the answer is the electron arithmetic of these two
half-equations — never "hydrogen is lighter" or any density-flavoured
guess.
:::

:::exam-hint
Expect three question shapes here. (1) *Predict the products*: hydrogen and
oxygen — the "no contest" electrolysis, since the only positive ion is H⁺
and sulfate refuses to oxidise. (2) *Explain the 2 : 1 volume ratio* from
the half-equations. (3) *Why is the measured ratio wrong, and how do you
fix it?* — oxygen's solubility, and pre-saturating the water by running the
electrolysis before collecting. The apparatus for measuring the volumes is
the gas-syringe or measuring-cylinder-over-electrode setup, and it returns
in the next section doing quantitative work.
:::

:::misconception
"Electrolysing sulfuric acid must give sulfur, because sulfate ions contain
sulfur." Sulfate ions are never discharged in these experiments — they are
too difficult to oxidise, which is precisely why the water's hydroxide ions
do the anode work. Nothing sulfur-flavoured appears at either electrode: the
acid's hydrogen ions join the water's at the cathode, its sulfate ions sit
in the solution unused, and both products come from water. The same logic
saw off "sulfur at the cathode" in the copper(II) sulfate residue question.
:::
