The bulb lights because a complete circuit exists — but the chemical change
happens at the two electrode surfaces, and this section is the manual for
what occurs there. Start with what the power supply does. It pumps electrons
around the external circuit: it pulls electrons out of one electrode and
pushes them onto the other. The electrode **stripped of electrons** becomes
**positive** — that is the anode. The electrode **loaded with extra
electrons** becomes **negative** — that is the cathode. The signs of the
electrodes are not painted on; the power supply creates them, and the ions in
the electrolyte respond instantly to the electric field between them.

Now the ions move. **Positive ions ([[cation]]s) are attracted to the
negative electrode — the cathode.** When a cation touches the cathode, it
picks up the electrons it is short of, straight off the electrode surface.
Losing its charge, it becomes a neutral atom: it is **[[discharge]]d**.
**Negative ions ([[anion]]s) are attracted to the positive electrode — the
anode.** When an anion arrives, its **extra** electron — the one that made
it negative — moves off the ion onto the electron-hungry anode. The ion is
discharged too, becoming a neutral atom. Discharge is the whole event: ions
arrive, electrons cross the electrode surface one way or the other, neutral
atoms form. Those atoms are the products — metals at the cathode, non-metals
at the anode — and for elements whose atoms pair into molecules (bromine,
chlorine, oxygen, hydrogen), the atoms then join up two by two.

Each electrode event is written as a **[[half-equation]]** — the equation
for one half of the overall redox story, one electrode at a time. The
notation rules are short and strict:

- electrons are written as **e⁻** and appear on **one side only** — a
  half-equation shows a single process, either electron **gain** or electron
  **loss**, never both;
- the species before and after are **ions and atoms**: Pb²⁺ → Pb, Br⁻ → Br,
  H⁺ → H — with state symbols added when the question asks;
- the equation must balance in **atoms** and in **charge**: the total charge
  on the left must equal the total charge on the right.

The charge-balance rule is what fixes how many electrons each half-equation
carries. An ion's charge tells you directly: a 2+ ion needs **2** electrons
on the right-hand side to reach neutral; a 1− ion sheds exactly **1**
electron; a 2− ion sheds **2**; a 3+ ion absorbs **3**. For molecular
products the electrons also make the *molecule* arithmetic come out — two
1− halide ions shed 2 electrons between them to make one halogen molecule —
and the lesson's case studies build every one of those equations in full.

Finally, the classification vocabulary that spec 1.59C hangs on these
equations. An event in which a species **gains** electrons is
**[[reduction]]** — that is the cathode's job, so *reduction always happens
at the cathode*. An event in which a species **loses** electrons is
**[[oxidation]]** — the anode's job, so *oxidation always happens at the
anode*. Every half-equation is therefore labelled one or the other, and the
overall electrolysis — loss and gain running together — is called redox, a
word the next section unpacks properly with the OILRIG mnemonic.

:::key-point
The electron ledger. **Cathode (−)**: cations arrive, **gain** electrons from
the electrode, become atoms — **reduction**. **Anode (+)**: anions arrive,
**lose** their extra electron to the electrode, become atoms — **oxidation**.
Electrons supplied to the cathode by the power supply are carried away from
the anode by the same supply: the ledger always balances, because every
electron gained at the cathode was lost at the anode.
:::

:::exam-hint
Half-equation marks are won and lost on two checks. **Electron count**: read
it off the ion charge — charge magnitude = number of electrons transferred.
**Charge balance**: total charge left = total charge right (Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻
carries total 0 on the left, matching neutral Pb on the right). Then label
the event: electrons on the **left** (gain) = reduction; electrons on the
**right** (loss) = oxidation. Practise saying the label out loud as part of
the answer — "reduction, because the lead ions gain electrons" — because the
label is often a separate mark from the equation itself.
:::

:::misconception
"A half-equation should show electrons on both sides, one entering and one
leaving — otherwise the electrons don't balance." No — a *half*-equation
deliberately shows **one electrode's half** of the story, so electrons enter
or leave exactly once. If electrons appear on both sides, you have written
two processes in one equation, and that is not a half-equation at all. The
full electron balance across electrolysis comes from *pairing* the two
half-equations — one reduction at the cathode, one oxidation at the anode —
which is why they are called halves.
:::
