Pass a current through a metal and you observe almost nothing — the metal
gets slightly warmer, and that is all, because electrons drift through an
intact structure. Pass a current through an **ionic compound that is molten
or in solution** and you observe a great deal: bubbling at the electrodes,
metals appearing, colours forming — new substances being made. That chemical
change has a name and a definition worth memorising word-for-word, because it
is a one-mark banker in every paper it can appear in:

**[[electrolysis]] is a chemical change caused by passing an electric current
through a compound which is either molten or in solution.**

The word is worth taking apart, because it is its own explanation:
**electro**-lysis is literally "splitting by electricity". The current does
not merely flow through the compound — it decomposes it. The substance being
split is carried in the second definition: an **[[electrolyte]]** is a liquid
or solution that undergoes electrolysis. Every electrolyte contains **ions**,
and the movement of those ions is responsible for two things at once — the
conduction of the electricity and the chemical changes at the electrodes. One
particle population, two jobs.

The current is fed into and out of the electrolyte through two
**[[electrode]]**s. **Carbon (graphite) is the standard electrode material**
because it conducts electricity and is chemically fairly **inert** — an
[[inert-electrode]] does not react with the substances being
produced. Platinum is also inert and can replace carbon; various other
metals are sometimes used, and what happens when the electrode material is
*not* inert is a story told later in the lesson. The two electrodes take
their names from their signs, and these names are fixed vocabulary from here
to the end of the course:

- the **positive** electrode is the **[[anode]]**;
- the **negative** electrode is the **[[cathode]]**.

The apparatus for molten-compound electrolysis is deliberately simple, and
you must be able to describe and draw it (spec 1.58C). A heat-proof crucible
holds the solid ionic compound; two carbon electrodes dip into it, held by a
lid with holes; the electrodes connect through wires and a bulb (or ammeter)
to a power supply — a 6 volt battery or a power pack, and the voltage is not
critical. Heat the crucible with a Bunsen burner. The bulb is the
experiment's storyteller: it stays dark while the compound is solid, and
lights the moment the compound melts. Light bulb = complete circuit = mobile
ions = conduction — and, as the next section explains, much more than
conduction.

:::key-point
Keep the two circuits in your head as one loop with two different carriers.
**Outside** the electrolyte — wires, power pack, bulb, the electrodes
themselves — the current is carried by **electrons**. **Inside** the
electrolyte, electrons cannot swim; the current is carried by **ions**
crawling towards the electrodes. The handover happens at the electrode
surfaces, and that handover *is* the chemistry of electrolysis.
:::

:::exam-hint
Three definitions come up again and again, and each must be exact.
**Electrolysis**: a chemical change caused by passing an electric current
through a compound that is molten or in solution — the "molten or in
solution" clause is compulsory, because it is the condition for mobile ions.
**Electrolyte**: a liquid or solution that undergoes electrolysis — and it
must contain ions. **Electrodes**: the conductors (carbon or platinum are
the named examples) that carry the current into and out of the electrolyte.
And the sign-pair — anode positive, cathode negative — has its own
mnemonic, **PANiC**: **P**ositive **A**node, **N**egative (is) **C**athode.
Carry it for the rest of the course; the paired AN OX / RED CAT arrives in
section 6.
:::

:::misconception
"The power supply must be powerful to force electrolysis to happen — more
volts, more splitting." A 6 volt battery or a standard power pack does the
job, and the voltage is not critical; what matters is that the electrolyte
contains mobile ions to complete the circuit. Raise the voltage and you may
push more current through, but the *existence* of the chemical change
depends on the ions, not the punch of the supply. Answers that blame "weak
electricity" for a non-conducting solid have the physics backwards — it is
the locked lattice that breaks the circuit, not a feeble battery.
:::
