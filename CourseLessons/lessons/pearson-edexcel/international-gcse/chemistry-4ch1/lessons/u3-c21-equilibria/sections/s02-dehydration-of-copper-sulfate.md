Heat blue copper(II) sulfate crystals gently and something quietly
dramatic happens: the blue colour drains away, the crystals crumble into
a white powder, and water is driven off. The heating makes the crystals
lose their [[water-of-crystallisation]] — the water packed into the
crystal's structure — and what remains is white
[[anhydrous]] copper(II) sulfate. *Anhydrous* simply means *without
water*. No new substances entered the tube; the crystal gave up part of
itself, and that part was water.

Now reverse the film. Add water to the white solid and it turns blue
again — and it also becomes very warm. The rehydration releases heat
energy, which you can feel through the tube. The original change has
been exactly reversed: white back to blue, water restored into the
crystal structure, and the heat that went in on heating comes back out
on re-wetting. Every step of the forward change has a matching step in
the reverse direction.

This exact-reversal — including the heat — is what earns the reaction
its name and its special notation. This is called a
[[reversible-reaction]], and it is indicated in equations by a special
double arrow, ⇌, with half arrowheads pointing each way. The double
arrow is a compact promise: everything on the left can become
everything on the right, and everything on the right can become
everything on the left, under the right conditions.

Keep this reaction ready in your memory. It is one of the two named
examples the specification asks you to describe, and its colour change
makes it the chapter's built-in indicator: whenever a later section
needs a visible signal that an equilibrium has shifted, the blue-white
switch of copper(II) sulfate is the picture to hold. In s08 you will
meet its colour-based partner for gases — the brown-pale switch of
nitrogen dioxide — and the two together cover every equilibrium-shift
question the exam can colour-code.

:::misconception
"Heating drove the water out, so the crystal was destroyed." Nothing was
destroyed — the water of crystallisation is part of the crystal's
structure, not an impurity, and removing it is a chemical change that
can be undone completely. The evidence is in your hand: add water back
and the original blue solid returns, crystal structure rebuilt, with
the heat balance reversed just as exactly. Destruction would leave no
path home.
:::

:::exam-hint
A describe question on this reaction earns its marks in ordered
observations: blue crystals turn white on gentle heating (water driven
off, anhydrous white solid formed); adding water turns the solid blue
again and the tube becomes warm. The warmth is a mark-scoring
observation, not decoration — it is the evidence that the reverse
change is exothermic, and it connects this section directly to the ∆H
reasoning you will use on the chemistry-only track.
:::
