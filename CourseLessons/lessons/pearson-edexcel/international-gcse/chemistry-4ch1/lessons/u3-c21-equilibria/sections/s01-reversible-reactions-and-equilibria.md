Some chemical changes refuse to be one-way journeys. Heat a blue crystal
and it turns white; add water and the blue returns. Burn fuel and the
products sit there forever; but split ammonium chloride by heating and
the two products quietly recombine the moment they cool. A
[[reversible-reaction]] is a reaction whose products can turn back into
the original reactants — and once you start looking, you find them
everywhere in chemistry.

This chapter has one driving question. If a reaction can go both ways,
how do you arrange conditions so that the mixture ends up containing as
much as possible of the substance you want? That question runs the whole
chapter: first the idea of reversibility and its arrow, then what happens
inside a sealed container where the two opposing changes fight to a
stand-off called [[dynamic-equilibrium]], and finally how shifting
temperature, pressure or a catalyst moves that stand-off one way or the
other.

The chapter runs on two tracks. The **core track** (assessed in both
Paper 1C and Paper 2C) covers reversibility and the special double arrow
⇌ used in equations, plus two named reversible reactions in full: the
dehydration of hydrated copper(II) sulfate and the effect of heat on
ammonium chloride. The **chemistry-only track** (Paper 2C) builds on
that foundation: dynamic equilibrium in a sealed container, the two
characteristics that define it, why a catalyst leaves the position of
equilibrium untouched, and how changing temperature or pressure moves
the position of equilibrium in a predictable direction.

Two habits will carry you through. First, always ask "which direction?"
— for any change you make to a reversible reaction, the mixture responds
in a direction, and naming that direction precisely (towards reactants
or towards products) is the mark-earning skill. Second, keep the two
words in *dynamic equilibrium* separate in your mind: *dynamic* means
both reactions never stop, *equilibrium* means nothing overall changes.
Every exam answer in this chapter is built from those two halves.

:::cross-curricular
"As much as possible of what you want" is the daily business of
industrial chemistry. Ammonia for fertiliser, sulfuric acid for almost
every manufactured product — both are made by reversible reactions where
engineers choose temperature and pressure to push the equilibrium towards
the product side. The economics of entire chemical plants ride on the
prediction rules you will learn in s06 and s07.
:::

:::misconception
"Reversible reactions are rare, special reactions." The opposite is true
once you know the sign. Any reaction where the products can meet each
other and re-react is potentially reversible, and the two examples this
chapter names in full — a salt crystal losing and regaining water, and a
white solid splitting into two gases that recombine on cooling — are
ordinary chemistry, not exotic cases. The reversible arrow ⇌ is the
everyday notation for them, not a rare warning sign.
:::

:::exam-hint
Specification wording you can quote: know that some reactions are
reversible and this is indicated by the symbol ⇌ in equations. Notice
the precise claim — the *symbol* indicates reversibility. If an exam
question shows an equation with ⇌ and asks what it means, one mark is
waiting for the statement that the reaction can proceed in both
directions, not a vague "it goes backwards".
:::
