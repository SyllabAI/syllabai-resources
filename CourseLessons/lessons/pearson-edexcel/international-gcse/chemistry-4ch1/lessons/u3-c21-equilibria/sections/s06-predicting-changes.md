This section looks at what can be done to change the
[[position-of-equilibrium]] to produce as much as possible of what you
want in the equilibrium mixture. When the conditions in a system in
dynamic equilibrium are changed — for example the temperature or the
pressure — the position of equilibrium might shift left or right,
giving less or more of the products.

There is one master rule for predicting the direction. When conditions
change, the reaction always sets about counteracting the change you
made. It works like choosing clothes: if a room is too warm you take
off your jacket so you can cool down; if the room is too cold you put
more clothes on. The equilibrium mixture behaves the same way — every
shift you will ever predict is the reaction undoing your change as far
as it can.

The levers we might pull to influence the reaction are: changing the
pressure, changing the temperature, and adding a catalyst — which turns
out to have no effect on the position of equilibrium at all (the
catalyst gets its own section shortly). Before applying the pressure
lever, note its scope: the prediction only really applies to reactions
in which at least one of the reactants or products is a gas, and where
the total numbers of gaseous molecules on the two sides of the equation
are different.

Pressure is caused by molecules hitting the walls of their container;
fewer molecules in the same volume at the same temperature means lower
pressure. Take the general gas reaction:

A(g) + 2B(g) ⇌ C(g) + D(g)

There are three gaseous molecules on the left but only two on the
right. If you increase the pressure, the reaction responds by reducing
it again — and it can reduce the pressure by producing fewer gaseous
molecules to hit the walls, converting A + 2B (three molecules) into
C + D (two molecules). Increasing the pressure will always cause the
position of equilibrium to shift in the direction which produces the
smaller number of gaseous molecules.

The classic application is ammonia. Consider the reaction between
nitrogen gas and hydrogen gas:

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)

If we want to make as much ammonia as possible — to raise the
[[yield]] — we need to make the pressure as high as possible. There are
four molecules of gas on the left-hand side but only two on the
right-hand side, so increasing the pressure shifts the position of
equilibrium to the right: the ammonia side, the side with fewer gas
molecules.

The boundary case completes the rule. If there is the same number of
gaseous molecules on both sides of the equation, changing the pressure
makes no difference to the position of equilibrium. For example:

H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)

Here increasing or decreasing the pressure has no effect on the
position of equilibrium — the amount of hydrogen iodide present at
equilibrium stays the same. Three molecules against three: nothing for
the reaction to gain by either direction, so the position stands still.
And remember from s05 why solids and liquids never join this
accounting: their particles are already packed close, so squeezing the
container changes their concentration hardly at all.

:::key-point
At equilibrium, the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate
of the reverse reaction, so as much heat is being given out as is taken
in — nothing appears to change.
:::

:::key-point
In an endothermic reaction the heat is converted to a different form
(chemical energy), so it is being removed. That is why raising the
temperature pulls the position of equilibrium in the endothermic
direction, and why lowering it pushes the exothermic direction — the
mixture always works to counteract your change.
:::

:::did-you-know
The counteract rule you just learned is the working tool of industrial
chemistry. The ammonia synthesis above is carried out under high
pressure precisely because the equilibrium rewards high pressure with
more product — and the same rule, applied in reverse, tells engineers
which reactions gain nothing from squeezing. One sentence of prediction
logic, entire plant designs.
:::

:::misconception
"Increasing the pressure speeds everything up, so it also gives more
product." Pressure and rate are the last chapter's story; position is
this chapter's. For an equilibrium with equal gas-molecule counts on
both sides, a pressure change leaves the position of equilibrium
untouched even though the mixture is compressed. The prediction needs
the molecule count — three-against-two shifts, three-against-three
does not.
:::

:::exam-hint
Prediction questions are marked in two steps: state the direction
("shifts right / towards ammonia"), then give the counteract reason
("the right-hand side has fewer gas molecules, which reduces the
pressure"). Quote molecule counts from the balancing — four against two
for the ammonia reaction — because the number is the evidence. If the
counts are equal, say so and state that there is no effect; "no change"
with the equal-counts reason is a full-mark answer, not a refusal.
:::

:::quick-check
State the counteract rule in your own words, then apply it: for
A(g) + 2B(g) ⇌ C(g) + D(g), which way does the position move if the
pressure is increased, and why? Do the same for N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌
2NH3(g), quoting the molecule counts. Finally, explain why H2(g) +
I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) refuses to shift either way under pressure.
:::
