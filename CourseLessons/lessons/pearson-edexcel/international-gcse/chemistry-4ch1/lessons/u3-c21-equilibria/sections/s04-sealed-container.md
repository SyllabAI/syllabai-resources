A [[sealed-container]] means that no substances are added to the
reaction mixture and no substances escape from it. Heat is different:
it may be either given off or absorbed — energy can cross the walls
even when matter cannot. That single restriction is what the
chemistry-only track's equilibrium story is built on, because a
reversible reaction can only show its full behaviour when both
substances and their products are trapped together.

Watch the behaviour with a model. Imagine a substance that exists in
two forms — one shown as a blue square, the other as a yellow square —
and suppose you start with a sample that is entirely blue. Because you
begin with a high concentration of blue squares, the rate at which they
turn yellow starts relatively high, counted as squares changing colour
per second. But as the blue gradually gets used up, that rate of change
falls. Meanwhile the yellow squares can also change back to blue — it is
a reversible reaction, so the products can react with each other and go
back to form reactants. At the start there are no yellow squares, so the
yellow-to-blue rate is zero; as yellow accumulates, the
yellow-to-blue rate rises.

Make the model numeric. Imagine you started with 64 blue squares, and in
any second there is a one-in-four chance of each of them changing
colour. In the first second, 16 change, leaving 48 blue. In the next
second a quarter of these change — but that is only 12, leaving 36 blue
ones. In the third second, nine change, and so on: 64, then 48, then 36,
then 27. The rate of change falls as the number — the concentration — of
blue squares falls. The same one-in-four rule drives the growing yellow
population in the opposite direction, so the falling blue-to-yellow rate
and the rising yellow-to-blue rate must eventually meet.

Soon the rates of both reactions become equal. At that point blue
squares are changing into yellow at exactly the same rate that yellow
ones are turning blue. What would you see? The total numbers of blue and
yellow squares remain constant — but the reaction is still happening.
Follow the fate of any one particular square and you find it is
sometimes blue and sometimes yellow, flipping back and forth forever.
This is a [[dynamic-equilibrium]]: *dynamic* because both reactions are
still continuing, with the forward rate equal to the reverse rate, and
*equilibrium* because the total amounts — the concentrations — of
reactants and products are now constant.

The sealed container is not optional. Remove the yellow squares as soon
as they form and they never get the chance to turn blue again: what was
a reversible reaction now runs entirely one way, blue turning yellow
with nothing to replace it. The same logic ends the section with a
mineral example. Consider the reversible decomposition of calcium
carbonate:

CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g)

If this were not done in a closed container, all the CO2 would escape
and the reverse reaction would not occur. Reversible reactions will
eventually reach a state of equilibrium if they are left in a sealed
container — that is the track's anchor statement, and every industrial
application later in the chapter depends on it.

:::key-point
Picture 64 blue squares each with a one-in-four chance per second of
flipping: 16 flip first, then 12 of the remaining 48, then 9 of the
remaining 36. The rate of change falls as the concentration of blue
squares falls — and the mirror-image rise of the reverse change means
the two rates must eventually become equal.
:::

:::misconception
"At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products are
equal." They are **constant**, not equal — the exact wording the exam
watches for. The blue-yellow model proves the difference: when the
rates match, the mixture typically holds far more of one form than the
other, yet the totals no longer change. Equal amounts would be a
coincidence, not a requirement; constant amounts are the definition.
:::

:::exam-hint
Definitions in this section are two-mark constructions. For dynamic
equilibrium: one mark for the forward and reverse reactions occurring
at the same rate, one mark for the concentrations of reactants and
products remaining constant. For the sealed container: no substances
added or able to escape. Write both halves every time — answers that
say only "the reactions balance" drop the concentration half, and
answers that say only "concentrations stop changing" drop the rates
half.
:::

:::quick-check
Rebuild the argument in five beats: what a sealed container allows and
blocks; why the blue-to-yellow rate starts high and falls; why the
yellow-to-blue rate starts at zero and rises; what is true at the
moment the rates meet; and why removing product as it forms destroys
the equilibrium. Then check yourself against the calcium carbonate
equation — which species escaping is what prevents its reverse
reaction?
:::
