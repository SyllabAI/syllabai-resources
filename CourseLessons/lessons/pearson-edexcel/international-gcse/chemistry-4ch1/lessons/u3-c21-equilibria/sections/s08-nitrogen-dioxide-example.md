Nitrogen dioxide, NO2, is a dark brown, poisonous gas. It can
[[dimerise]] — join together in pairs — to make molecules of dinitrogen
tetroxide, N2O4, which is colourless. There is a
[[dynamic-equilibrium]] between the two forms:

2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g)

Every prediction rule from the last two sections now runs on one visible
mixture, because the two sides of this equation have different colours
and different molecule counts.

**Pressure.** If you increase the pressure, the position of equilibrium
will shift to reduce it again — by producing fewer gaseous molecules.
Two molecules on the left, one on the right, so the position of
equilibrium moves to the right and the mixture produces more
dinitrogen tetroxide, losing brown colour. If you lower the pressure,
the position of equilibrium shifts to increase the pressure again by
producing more gaseous molecules — it moves to the left, and you obtain
a higher proportion of the brown nitrogen dioxide in the equilibrium
mixture.

**Temperature.** Notice from the equation that the change from nitrogen
dioxide to dinitrogen tetroxide is exothermic: the negative sign for
∆H shows that heat is given out by the forward reaction. If you
decrease the temperature, the position of equilibrium shifts to produce
more heat to counteract the change — it moves in the exothermic
direction, so there is more dinitrogen tetroxide in the equilibrium
mixture and the colour of the reaction mixture fades. If you increase
the temperature, the position of equilibrium shifts to lower the
temperature again — it moves in the reverse, endothermic direction, more
nitrogen dioxide is formed, and the colour of the gas darkens.

**Reading the tubes.** The prediction can be seen directly. Take three
tubes containing this equilibrium mixture: one at lab temperature, one
standing in ice, one in hot water. The hot tube is very dark brown — a
high proportion of nitrogen dioxide, exactly as the endothermic
left-shift under heating demands. The tube in the ice is slightly
paler than the one in the open air, showing that cooling must have
given it a slightly greater proportion of the colourless dinitrogen
tetroxide. Notice the word *slightly*: the ice tube shifted left, but
only a little — equilibrium shifts counteract changes, they do not
flip the mixture.

One caution completes the section. The forward reaction here gives out
heat, so the reverse reaction takes it in — by exactly the same amount,
as always. Whichever way you push this mixture, the composition answers
by moving the colour: darker for more nitrogen dioxide, paler for more
dinitrogen tetroxide. Brown and pale are the readout of the
[[position-of-equilibrium]] itself.

:::misconception
"Cooling the tubes should make the colour disappear if dinitrogen
tetroxide is colourless." The shift is partial. The ice tube is only
*slightly* paler than the lab-temperature tube: cooling moved the
position of equilibrium towards the colourless side, but the mixture
still contains plenty of brown nitrogen dioxide. A shift changes the
proportions — it does not drain the reactant away, because the forward
and reverse reactions are both still running at their new common rate.
:::

:::exam-hint
This reaction is the examiner's favourite equilibrium story because one
mark sits on the molecule count (2 against 1) and one on the ∆H sign.
Write the equation first, annotate "2 → 1 molecules" beside it and
"forward exothermic" under it, and every pressure/temperature question
becomes a two-line quotation of s06's rules applied to those
annotations. The visible colour change is your built-in answer check:
darken means more NO2, fade means more N2O4.
:::

:::quick-check
Write the equilibrium equation, then predict and explain the colour
change for each of: increasing pressure, decreasing pressure,
increasing temperature, cooling in ice. Check each answer against the
tube observations — hot very dark, ice slightly paler than air. If any
prediction contradicts a tube, the error is in your molecule count or
∆H sign, and you can repair it from those two facts alone.
:::
