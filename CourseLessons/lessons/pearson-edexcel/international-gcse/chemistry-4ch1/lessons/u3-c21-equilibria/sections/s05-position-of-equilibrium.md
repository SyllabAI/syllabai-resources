Take a general case where A reacts reversibly to give B:

A ⇌ 2B

The reaction from A to B — the left-to-right reaction — is described as
the [[forward-reaction]]. The reaction from B to A — the right-to-left
reaction — is called the [[reverse-reaction]]. Left and right here mean
exactly what they show on the page: reactants on the left of the arrow,
products on the right, and the double arrow in between promising that
both directions run.

Now let the mixture reach equilibrium and measure how much of each
substance is present. Suppose the measurement finds more A than B.
Because there is more A than B at equilibrium, we say that the
[[position-of-equilibrium]] lies to the left. The position is not a
place — it is a statement about which side of the equation dominates
the equilibrium mixture. Had the measurement found more B, the position
would lie to the right.

It is important to realise that equilibrium does not mean 50% reactants
and 50% products. The key thing is that the concentrations of A and B
stay constant at equilibrium — however lopsided the split. Those
constant concentrations will remain as they are until we change the
conditions, for instance the temperature or the pressure.

Watch what a condition change does. Increase the temperature and allow
the reaction time to reach equilibrium again, and the new measurement
might find less A and more B than before. Because the change decreased
the concentration of what is on the left and increased the concentration
of what is on the right, we say the position of equilibrium has shifted
to the right. Notice the subtlety: it does not matter that we still have
more A than B overall. What we look at is how things *changed* when we
changed the conditions — a shift is a movement, not an arrival.

One boundary case is worth pinning down now, because the next section
leans on it. The particles in solids and liquids are very close
together, and they are affected very little by changes in pressure;
their concentration does not really change as the pressure changes.
Pressure is a lever on gases — on the number of gas molecules in a given
volume — and the next two sections use exactly that picture to predict
which way any equilibrium moves.

:::key-point
The position of equilibrium describes which side dominates the mixture:
more A than B at equilibrium means the position lies to the left.
Equilibrium never means 50% on each side — the requirement is constant
concentrations, and a shift means the concentrations changed when the
conditions did.
:::

:::misconception
"The position of equilibrium lies where the amounts are equal."
Equilibrium positions are usually lopsided — the A ⇌ 2B example holds
more A than B, and that is a perfectly good equilibrium. What makes it
an equilibrium is that the lopsided split stays constant until
conditions change. When examiners ask "which side does the position
lie?", they want more-of/less-of language, never a fifty-fifty
assumption.
:::

:::exam-hint
Shift questions are marked on the *direction of change*, not the final
amount. If a temperature rise moves the mixture from "more A than B" to
"less A and more B", the position shifted right — even though A is
still the majority. Saying "the position moved right because the amount
of B increased and the amount of A decreased" earns the reasoning mark;
saying "there is now more B than A" would be wrong on the same facts.
:::

:::quick-check
Define the forward and reverse directions for A ⇌ 2B. If an equilibrium
mixture contains more B than A, which way does the position lie — and
what would we say after a condition change that produced less B and
more A? Finally, state why pressure changes barely affect solids and
liquids. Those three answers are the entire grammar of the next two
sections.
:::
