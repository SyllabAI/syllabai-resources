You now hold the complete equilibrium toolkit: the ⇌ arrow and what it
promises; the two named reversible reactions with their equations and
their heat directions; the sealed-container route to dynamic
equilibrium, with both of its defining characteristics; the position of
equilibrium and its left/right language; and the three levers —
pressure read from molecule counts, temperature read from the ∆H sign,
and the catalyst that changes neither. The nitrogen dioxide system
straps a colour readout onto all of it, so every shift has a visible
answer. The question bank for this chapter joins the platform by the
specification points below, so every practice item you are served
targets exactly the skills these sections taught.

Before you open the bank, audit yourself against the chapter's core
moves. Can you write both named reversible reactions — copper(II)
sulfate with its blue-white switch, ammonium chloride with its
disappearing-and-reappearing crystals — and say which direction needs
heat? Can you deliver the dynamic-equilibrium definition in its two
halves, rates equal and concentrations constant, and explain why the
container must be sealed? On the chemistry-only track: can you predict
a shift direction from a molecule count, from a ∆H sign, and state
plainly why a catalyst never moves the position? Anything that wobbled
is a section to revisit, not a reason to start over.

The bank itself trains four question shapes. Definition questions ask
what dynamic equilibrium means, with the marking note that *both*
words — dynamic and equilibrium — need explaining. Prediction tables
give several equilibria with their ∆H signs and ask which way the
position moves when temperature or pressure changes — the s06/s07
rules, one row at a time. Industrial questions run the full arc: a
reaction such as the reversible stage of the Contact process or the
methane-plus-steam route to hydrogen, with parts on defining the
equilibrium, choosing a pressure, and explaining what the catalyst does
for both the rate and the position. And the ammonia manufacture
question ties the whole chapter together — why high pressure raises the
conversion, why even higher pressure is not used (think risk), and why
the chosen temperature is a compromise that accepts less than the ideal
conversion. Practise all four shapes against the clock, and keep the
chapter's discipline: equation first, count the molecules, read the
sign, name the direction.

:::summary
- **Reversible reactions**: products can re-form the reactants;
  indicated by the ⇌ arrow. Named examples: dehydration of hydrated
  copper(II) sulfate (blue ⇌ white, heat in on dehydration, heat out on
  rehydration) and heating ammonium chloride (white solid ⇌ ammonia +
  hydrogen chloride gases, recombining where it is cooler).
- **Thermal decomposition**: the copper(II) sulfate forward change —
  one substance split into simpler substances by heat alone. The
  recombination step is acid (HCl) + base (NH3) making a salt.
- **Sealed container**: nothing added, nothing escapes (heat may pass).
  Reversible reactions reach equilibrium only in a closed system —
  escaping CO2 is why open-tube calcium carbonate decomposition never
  reverses.
- **Dynamic equilibrium**: forward and reverse reactions occur at the
  same rate AND the concentrations of reactants and products remain
  constant. Constant, never equal. The mixture keeps reacting —
  individual particles flip both ways forever.
- **Position of equilibrium**: which side dominates the mixture. Lies
  left if more reactant, right if more product; a *shift* is a change
  in the split when conditions change, not an arrival at 50/50.
- **Pressure**: shifts towards the side with fewer gas molecules
  (higher pressure) or more gas molecules (lower pressure). Equal
  molecule counts mean no effect. Solids and liquids are unaffected —
  their particles barely change concentration under pressure.
- **Temperature**: ∆H on the equation belongs to the forward reaction;
  the reverse is endothermic/exothermic by the same amount. Higher
  temperature shifts the endothermic direction; lower temperature the
  exothermic direction. Example: 80%/20% reactants/products at 500 °C
  became 60%/40% at 100 °C for the exothermic-forward A + 2B ⇌ C + D.
- **Catalysts**: speed forward and reverse by the same proportion — no
  change in the position of equilibrium; they shorten the time taken to
  reach it.
- **Model system**: 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g), brown ⇌ colourless; squeeze →
  paler (2 → 1 molecules), cool → paler (forward exothermic), heat →
  darker (reverse endothermic), with two-phase colour answers under
  compression.
:::

:::can-you
- State what the ⇌ symbol indicates in an equation?
- Describe both named reversible reactions — observations, equations,
  and which direction needs heat?
- Define dynamic equilibrium with both required halves, and explain why
  it needs a sealed container (calcium carbonate as the counterexample)?
- Say what the position of equilibrium means, and what a shift is —
  without assuming 50/50?
- Predict the direction of a pressure shift from molecule counts,
  including the equal-counts no-effect case?
- Predict the direction of a temperature shift from the ∆H sign,
  quoting the endothermic/exothermic rule?
- Explain why a catalyst does not affect the position of equilibrium,
  using the same-proportion wording?
- Read the NO2/N2O4 tube colours — including why the ice tube is only
  *slightly* paler?
:::

:::misconception
"Once you know the rules, the named reactions are just trivia." The
named reactions are where the rules get *examined*. The copper(II)
sulfate switch carries the core-track marks and hands you a colour
readout for exothermic rehydration; the ammonium chloride tube tests
whether you can narrate a reversible change with its heat directions;
the calcium carbonate system is the standard sealed-container
counterexample. Every abstract rule in this chapter has one concrete
reaction attached — learn the pairs together and the rules stop being
abstract.
:::
