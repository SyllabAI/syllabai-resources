Two summary lines close the pressure account. Increasing pressure: the
[[position-of-equilibrium]] shifts to the side which has fewer gas
molecules. Decreasing pressure: the position of equilibrium shifts to
the side which has more gas molecules. One sentence each, both read
straight off the balancing of the equation.

Temperature needs one preparatory idea first. When we write a
reversible reaction showing an enthalpy change, the ∆H always shows the
enthalpy change for the [[forward-reaction]] — the value is given as if
the reaction were a one-way process. Take the general example:

A + 2B ⇌ C + D   ∆H = −100 kJ/mol

The ∆H being negative tells us the forward reaction is exothermic. The
[[reverse-reaction]] will be endothermic — by exactly the same amount.
Every reversible equation you meet comes with this built-in pairing:
one direction releases the heat, the other absorbs it, and the sign
written with the equation names which is which.

Now predict with the counteract rule. Suppose you change the conditions
by decreasing the temperature of an equilibrium — for example the
mixture is in equilibrium at 500 °C and you lower it to 100 °C. The
reaction will respond in a way that increases the temperature again.
How? If some A and B are converted into C and D, heat is given out,
because that is the exothermic change; the extra heat warms the
reaction mixture up again. So decreasing the temperature moves the
position of equilibrium to the right-hand side, and more C and D are
formed. You can watch it in the composition: this example's equilibrium
mixture at 500 °C is 80% reactants (A and B) and 20% products (C and
D); cooled to 100 °C and left to re-establish, it becomes 60% reactants
and 40% products. The product share grew exactly as the
rightward-shift demands.

Increasing the temperature has exactly the opposite effect. The
reaction equilibrium changes to remove the extra heat by absorbing it
in an endothermic change — this time the reverse reaction is favoured
and the position of equilibrium moves to the left. The summary lines
for the whole chapter: increasing temperature, the position of
equilibrium shifts in the endothermic direction; decreasing
temperature, it shifts in the exothermic direction.

Which brings the chapter to its third lever. Adding a catalyst speeds
up reactions — and in a reversible change it speeds up the forward and
reverse reactions by the same proportion. If a catalyst speeds up the
forward reaction ten times, it speeds up the reverse reaction ten times
as well. The net effect: no change in the position of equilibrium if
you add a catalyst. Both rates rise together, so the point where they
meet — the equilibrium itself — keeps exactly the same composition. The
catalyst is added to increase the *rate at which equilibrium is
reached*: the same destination, reached sooner. That is why industrial
plants can afford catalysts — they never change how much product the
equilibrium holds, only how fast the mixture gets there.

:::key-point
∆H attached to a reversible equation belongs to the forward reaction
alone: A + 2B ⇌ C + D with ∆H = −100 kJ/mol means the forward reaction
is exothermic and the reverse reaction is endothermic by exactly the
same amount.
:::

:::misconception
"A catalyst increases the yield of a reversible reaction." It never
does — and this exam loves the distinction. The catalyst speeds both
directions by the same proportion, so the equilibrium composition is
untouched; what improves is the *time* taken to reach it. Yield belongs
to temperature and pressure; speed belongs to the catalyst. Answers
that promise extra product from a catalyst hand back the mark.
:::

:::exam-hint
Colour-change predictions need extra care. If you increase the pressure
on a coloured gas equilibrium, you squeeze the same number of molecules
into a smaller space, so the colour darkens *initially*; then it fades
a bit as the equilibrium re-establishes — but not back to its original
colour, because the gases are still compressed. Two-phase answers
(immediate effect, then the shift's partial correction) are what the
mark scheme rewards; single-phase answers miss the first phase.
:::

:::quick-check
For an exothermic forward reaction, state which way the position moves
when temperature rises and when it falls — quoting the endothermic/
exothermic rule each time. Then verify the 500 °C → 100 °C example: if
the product share rose from 20% to 40%, which direction shifted? Finish
by explaining, in the same-proportion language, why a catalyst leaves
the position of equilibrium exactly where it was.
:::
