To determine the enthalpy change for the [[displacement-reaction]] between zinc
and copper(II) sulfate, the procedure is:

1. Place a polystyrene cup in a 250 cm³ glass beaker.
2. Transfer **50 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ copper(II) sulfate solution** into the
   cup using a measuring cylinder.
3. Weigh **1.20 g of zinc** using a weighing boat on a balance.
4. Record the **initial temperature** of the copper(II) sulfate solution.
5. Add the zinc.
6. Stir the solution as quickly as possible.
7. Record the **maximum temperature** reached.

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Initial temperature of copper(II) sulfate solution / °C | 17.0 |
| Maximum temperature of the reaction mixture / °C | 27.3 |

The calculation uses the same assumptions as every solution experiment: the
solution's density is that of water (1 cm³ has mass 1 g) and its specific
heat capacity is 4.18 J/g/°C, because the mixture is mostly water.

:::worked-example
**Calculate the enthalpy change for this displacement reaction when 1 mole
of copper(II) sulfate reacts with zinc.**

*Step 1 — heat given out.* ∆T = 27.3 − 17.0 = 10.3 °C:

Q = mc∆T = 50 × 4.18 × 10.3 = 2152.7 J = 2.1527 kJ

*Step 2 — which reactant runs out first?* Moles of CuSO₄ = (50 ÷ 1000) ×
0.200 = **0.0100 mol**. Moles of Zn = 1.20 ÷ 65 = **0.0185 mol**. One mole
of Zn reacts with one mole of CuSO₄, so 0.0100 mol of CuSO₄ reacts with
0.0100 mol of Zn — we added 0.0185 mol of zinc, so the zinc is in
[[excess]] and the **copper(II) sulfate is the limiting reactant**.
(Excess means more than enough is present to ensure all the copper(II)
sulfate reacts.)

*Step 3 — per mole.* The heat released when 1 mole of CuSO₄ reacts is:

∆H = Q ÷ n = 2.1527 ÷ 0.0100 = 215.3 ≈ **−215 kJ/mol**

Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)   ∆H = −215 kJ/mol

The negative sign is added because the temperature of the reaction mixture
went up: the displacement is exothermic and heat is released. (Remember the
÷1000 when converting the 50 cm³ to dm³ — the concentration is given in
mol/dm³.)
:::

You could repeat this experiment with metals of different reactivities: the
more reactive the metal, the more heat should be released in the
displacement. Keep everything else the same — the number of moles of metal,
the size of the solid particles, and the volume and concentration of the
copper(II) sulfate solution. **Do not use metals more reactive than
magnesium**: those react with the water itself, and you would be measuring
the heat released by the metal–water reaction instead.

:::key-point
The moles you divide Q by are the moles of the **limiting reactant** — the
chemical that is not in excess. In this experiment the zinc was deliberately
in excess so that all 0.0100 mol of copper(II) sulfate reacted, making the
per-mole division clean.
:::

:::exam-hint
The examiner builds this question in three layers: the Q = mc∆T arithmetic
(2 marks), the moles comparison that identifies the excess reactant (1–2
marks), and the division plus sign (2 marks). The layer candidates drop is
the middle one — *say explicitly* that 0.0185 mol of zinc exceeds the
0.0100 mol of copper(II) sulfate, so copper(II) sulfate is limiting and its
moles go in the denominator of ∆H = Q ÷ n. A final answer without the
negative sign, or divided by the zinc's moles, loses the last two marks.
:::

:::misconception
"Use the moles of the reactant you weighed, because its mass was measured."
The per-mole definition attaches to the balanced equation's reacting ratio,
not to whichever mass was easier to weigh. With zinc in excess, dividing by
0.0185 mol would understate the per-mole value; the reaction's heat belongs
to the 0.0100 mol of copper(II) sulfate that actually reacted.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the salts and their
solutions.
:::
