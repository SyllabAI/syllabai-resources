Many reactions you meet at International GCSE are exothermic — combustion
and neutralisation, for instance. Despite needing a small amount of energy
to get started (the [[activation-energy]]), these reactions release heat
energy overall because the products are more stable than the reactants.
Enthalpy is clearly a driving force: reactions that give out heat happen
readily.

But endothermic processes happen too. Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water
lowers the temperature of the solution — yet it dissolves anyway, pulling
heat out of its surroundings and still going. How? A second factor is at
work: [[entropy]].

Entropy is a measure of **the number of ways of distributing the energy in
a system**. It is greater in a more disordered system, because the energy
can be dispersed more flexibly. The ordering runs solid → liquid → gas:
solids have lower entropy than liquids, and liquids lower entropy than
gases — a gas's particles roam freely, so its energy has the most ways to
be shared out.

If the entropy of a system **increases** during a change, the change is more
likely to happen. When ammonium nitrate dissolves, although the process is
endothermic, the NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions in the giant ionic lattice become
separated from each other and gain freedom of movement in the water — the
entropy of the system increases enough to outweigh the enthalpy
unfavourability. The same logic explains a kitchen classic: the reaction
between baking soda and vinegar (an endothermic reaction that proceeds
spontaneously, fizzing out carbon dioxide gas) — producing a gas increases
the entropy of the system, and the increase carries the change.

You do not need to calculate entropy at this level. What the examiners want
is the concept: enthalpy is not the only driving force, and an increase in
disorder can power changes that absorb heat.

:::exam-hint
This is enrichment, not a numbered specification point — but it earns
extension credit and it future-proofs the explanation questions. If asked
"why does ammonium nitrate dissolve even though the process is endothermic?",
the mature two-part answer is: the dissolving is endothermic, but the ions
leaving the lattice for the solution increases the entropy of the system,
and that increase outweighs the enthalpy change. The same pattern answers
for any spontaneous endothermic change that produces a gas.
:::

:::misconception
"Endothermic changes cannot happen by themselves because they need heat
pumped in." They cannot happen if enthalpy were the only driving force —
but disorder counts too. A change that absorbs heat can still run on its
own when it increases the number of ways the system's energy can be
distributed: ammonium nitrate dissolving in cool water is the standard
proof.
:::
