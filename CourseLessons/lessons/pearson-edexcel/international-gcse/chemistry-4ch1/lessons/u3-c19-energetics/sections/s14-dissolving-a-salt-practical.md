Calorimetry also measures the heat given out or taken in when salts dissolve
in water. The set-up is the same polystyrene cup in a 250 cm³ beaker used
for the displacement experiment. For ammonium chloride:

1. Place a polystyrene cup in a 250 cm³ glass beaker.
2. Transfer **100 cm³ of water** into the cup using a measuring cylinder.
3. Record the **initial temperature** of the water.
4. Weigh **5.20 g of ammonium chloride** using a weighing boat on a balance.
5. Add the ammonium chloride to the water and stir vigorously until it has
   all dissolved.
6. Record the **minimum temperature**.

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Initial temperature of water / °C | 18.3 |
| Minimum temperature of salt solution / °C | 15.1 |

The temperature **decreases**, so the process is endothermic — heat is
absorbed from the surroundings for the dissolving to occur. Note one
difference of kind, not just of sign: [[dissolving]] is a **physical
process** rather than a chemical reaction. When some salts dissolve heat is
given out (exothermic); when others dissolve the process is endothermic —
ammonium chloride belongs to the second group.

The calculation follows the standard assumptions: the diluted solution has
the specific heat capacity of water (it is mostly water), and the mass of
the solution is 100 g — the small mass of ammonium chloride is ignored. Heat
absorbed from the surrounding air is a further source of error, one that
matters much more here than in the hotter experiments.

:::worked-example
**Calculate the molar enthalpy change for dissolving ammonium chloride in
water.**

*Step 1 — heat absorbed.*

Q = mc∆T = 100 × 4.18 × (18.3 − 15.1) = 100 × 4.18 × 3.2 = 1337.6 J =
**1.3376 kJ**

*Step 2 — moles dissolved.* The relative formula mass of ammonium chloride,
NH₄Cl, is 14 + (4 × 1) + 35.5 = **53.5**:

n = mass ÷ Mr = 5.20 ÷ 53.5 = **0.0972 mol**

*Step 3 — per mole.* The [[molar-enthalpy-change]] of solution:

∆H = Q ÷ n = 1.3376 ÷ 0.0972 = **+13.8 kJ/mol**

The **positive** sign shows that heat is absorbed — dissolving ammonium
chloride in water is an endothermic process, which is why the temperature
went down.
:::

:::key-point
Dissolving is a physical process. The direction of its temperature change
depends on the salt: some dissolvings warm the water (exothermic), others
cool it (endothermic). Read the thermometer — do not assume.
:::

:::exam-hint
Two marks hide in the details here. The thermometer reads a **minimum** for
this experiment — "maximum temperature" is copy-paste from the exothermic
experiments and drops the observation mark. And the sign discipline inverts:
temperature **down** → endothermic → ∆H **positive**. If asked why the
assumptions are reasonable, the answer is that the solution is mostly water,
so it inherits water's specific heat capacity; if asked for a major error,
heat absorbed from the surrounding air during the (slow) dissolving is the
honest one.
:::

:::misconception
"Endothermic means the thermometer reads a lower number because the
thermometer is losing heat." The drop is real heat transfer: the dissolving
salt **takes** energy from the water to pull its particles apart and into
solution, so the water's temperature falls. The energy has not disappeared —
it has become chemical energy in the solution, exactly the reverse of the
exothermic bookkeeping.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact with the salts and their
solutions.
:::
