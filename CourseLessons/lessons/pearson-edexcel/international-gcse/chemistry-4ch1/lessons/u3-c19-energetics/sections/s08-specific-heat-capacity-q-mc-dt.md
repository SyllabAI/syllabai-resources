When we heat something up, it gets hotter. The [[specific-heat-capacity]]
tells us how much energy has to be put in to increase the temperature of
something. It is defined as **the amount of heat needed to raise the
temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C**. The unit is J/g/°C (or
J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹).

For water the value is **4.18 J/g/°C** — 4.18 J of heat energy raises the
temperature of 1 g of water by 1 °C. The idea scales in both directions, and
the multiplication is easy to reason through:

- 1 g of water by 2 °C needs 4.18 × 2 = 8.36 J.
- 2 g of water by 2 °C needs 2 × 8.36 = 16.72 J.

The heat energy required is **directly proportional** to the mass (m) and to
the temperature change (∆T), which compresses the whole idea into one
equation — the workhorse of this chapter:

**heat energy change = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change**

**Q = m × c × ∆T**

Each symbol carries a discipline. Q is the heat energy change in joules. m
is the mass **of the substance being heated** (in grams — usually water). c
is that substance's specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g/°C for water). ∆T is
its temperature change in °C. The same equation runs backwards for cooling:
to cool 1 g of water by 1 °C you must remove 4.18 J, so an endothermic
process sitting in water still hands you a usable Q — you just read the
temperature drop.

A [[calorimeter]] is simply the container you run a calorimetry experiment
in: in one experiment it is a copper can, in others a polystyrene cup. What
matters is that the reaction's heat ends up changing the temperature of a
known mass of water inside it.

:::key-point
The unit of specific heat capacity is J/g/°C (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹). Water's value is
4.18 J/g/°C — memorise it, every calculation in this chapter uses it. The
∆ in ∆T is the same "change in" symbol as in ∆H, this time applied to
temperature, and the equation applies to cooling as well as heating.
:::

:::exam-hint
Q = mc∆T questions are won and lost on *which mass* and *which ∆T* you
substitute. m is the mass of the **water (or solution) being heated**, never
the mass of the solid burned or dissolved. ∆T is final minus initial (or the
magnitude of the drop for cooling processes). Carry an extra significant
figure through the calculator and round at the end; divide by 1000 to
convert J to kJ before computing a ∆H in kJ/mol.
:::

:::misconception
"Specific heat capacity is a property of heating only." The quantity
describes energy needed *per degree of temperature change in either
direction* — removing 4.18 J cools 1 g of water by 1 °C just as supplying
4.18 J warms it. Endothermic experiments in this chapter depend on the
cooling direction: the reaction takes heat from the water, ∆T is a drop, and
Q still comes out of the same equation.
:::
