The reaction between an alkali and an acid is essentially the reaction
between hydroxide ions and hydrogen ions to form water:

OH⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) → H₂O(l)

For the potassium hydroxide + hydrochloric acid example, suppose you know
the concentration of the potassium hydroxide but are not so sure of the
dilute hydrochloric acid — the label has gone missing. The following method
finds the unknown concentration **and** the heat released, in one run:

1. Place a polystyrene cup in a 250 cm³ glass beaker.
2. Transfer **25 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ potassium hydroxide** into the cup.
3. Record the initial temperature.
4. Fill a burette with **50.00 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid**.
5. Use the burette to add **5.00 cm³** of the acid to the potassium
   hydroxide.
6. Stir vigorously and record the maximum temperature reached.
7. Keep adding further **5.00 cm³ portions**, stirring and recording the
   maximum temperature each time, until 50.00 cm³ in total has been added.

Plot the temperature of the mixture against the volume of acid added. The
shape tells the story: the temperature **increases at first** because the
acid reacts with the alkali and gives out heat; once all the alkali has been
used up, further additions are just cold acid poured into warm solution —
nothing left to react with — so the temperature **goes down**.

Draw **two lines of best fit**, one through the rising points and one
through the falling points: the point where the lines cross represents
**complete [[neutralisation]]**. Reading from the graph, the maximum temperature
is 31.8 °C, reached when 28.00 cm³ of acid has been added (the initial
temperature for the ∆T is 19.3 °C).

:::worked-example
**Calculate the heat released per mole and the unknown acid concentration.**

*Step 1 — heat given out.* Total volume at neutralisation = 25 + 28 = 53 cm³:

Q = mc∆T = 53 × 4.18 × (31.8 − 19.3) = 2769.3 J = **2.7693 kJ**

(Assumptions as always: density of the mixture = 1 g/cm³, and specific heat
capacity = that of water because the neutralised solution is mostly water.)

*Step 2 — moles.* Moles of KOH = (25 ÷ 1000) × 2.00 = **0.0500 mol**. At the
neutralisation point the moles of HCl are **exactly the same** — that is
what the crossing point guarantees.

*Step 3 — the [[molar-enthalpy-change]].* The heat released when 1 mole of
KOH reacts with 1 mole of HCl:

∆H = 2.7693 ÷ 0.0500 = **−55.4 kJ/mol**

KOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H₂O(l)   ∆H = −55.4 kJ/mol

The negative sign shows heat is released in this exothermic neutralisation.

*Step 4 — the unknown concentration.* 28.00 cm³ of acid contained the
0.0500 mol of HCl:

concentration = moles ÷ volume in dm³ = 0.0500 ÷ (28.00 ÷ 1000) = 0.0500 ÷
0.02800 = **1.79 mol/dm³**

One accuracy footnote: strictly, the water formed would first appear as
steam, and extra heat is given out when steam condenses to liquid water —
the attraction forces between molecules form exothermically. At this level
the value is taken for the liquid product.
:::

:::key-point
The two-lines-of-best-fit construction exists to fix the true neutralisation
point: the crossing volume is where moles of acid = moles of alkali, which
is why the HCl moles can be read off the KOH moles without knowing the acid
concentration in advance.
:::

:::exam-hint
This experiment is the richest mark-scheme target in the chapter. Know the
graph shape and its explanation (rises — exothermic neutralisation; falls —
cold acid diluting warm mixture with nothing left to react), the
two-best-fit-lines crossing construction, and the neat two-in-one finish:
∆H = −55.4 kJ/mol **and** the acid concentration 1.79 mol/dm³ from the same
run. When asked why the temperature falls after neutralisation, the phrase
"no further reaction — only dilution with cold acid" is the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"The maximum temperature must be at the largest volume of acid added."
After the crossing point you are adding cold acid to a warm, spent mixture —
each 5.00 cm³ portion drags the temperature down further. The peak sits at
the **neutralisation volume** (28.00 cm³ here), which is smaller than the
50.00 cm³ delivered in total; that is the entire logic of finding the
crossing point.
:::
