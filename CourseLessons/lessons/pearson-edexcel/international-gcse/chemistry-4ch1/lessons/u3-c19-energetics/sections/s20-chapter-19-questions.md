You have the complete toolkit now: the exothermic/endothermic split, the
∆H sign convention, the Q = mc∆T and ∆H = Q ÷ n pair, four calorimetry
setups with their assumptions and errors, and — for Paper 2C — energy level
diagrams and bond-energy arithmetic. The question bank for this chapter
joins the platform by the specification points below, so every practice
item you are served targets exactly the skills these sections taught.

Before you open the question bank, audit yourself against the chapter's
core moves. Can you define both reaction types with the heat-flow direction?
Can you run a five-step combustion calculation from a burner data table?
Can you state the two solution-calorimetry assumptions and the two errors
that make combustion results less exothermic? On the Paper 2C track: can
you draw both energy level diagrams with every label, and run a bond-energy
sum with correct counts and signs? Anything that wobbled is a section to
revisit, not a reason to start over.

:::summary
- **[[exothermic]]**: heat given out to the surroundings, temperature rises,
  products have less chemical energy (more stable), ∆H negative. Families:
  combustion, metal + acid, neutralisation, displacement.
- **[[endothermic]]**: heat taken in from the surroundings, temperature
  falls, products have more chemical energy (less stable), ∆H positive.
  Example: thermal decomposition of carbonates — heating must continue
  throughout. Not everything that needs a flame to start is endothermic.
- **[[enthalpy-change]] ∆H**: heat taken in or given out per the equation's
  amounts, read from the reactants' point of view, units kJ/mol; you can
  only measure the change, never the absolute enthalpy.
- **Q = mc∆T** converts a temperature change of a known mass (usually
  water, c = 4.18 J/g/°C) into heat energy; ∆H = Q ÷ n converts it to a
  per-mole value with the sign from the temperature trend.
- **Calorimetry families**: combustion (spirit burner + copper can; lid,
  shielding, stir), displacement (polystyrene cup; excess metal; divide by
  the limiting reactant), dissolving (physical process; minimum
  temperature; read the sign carefully), neutralisation (two best-fit
  lines; crossing point = complete neutralisation; doubles as an unknown
  concentration method).
- **Evaluation**: combustion values come out less exothermic than data
  books because of heat loss and incomplete combustion (yellow-orange
  flame, soot); longer alcohol chains burn more exothermically, rising
  regularly with each extra CH₂.
- **Paper 2C**: energy level diagrams (label both axes; products' level
  follows the sign of ∆H); bond-breaking endothermic, bond-making
  exothermic; bond energies are per-mole averages for gaseous molecules —
  count bonds from drawn-out structures, add +in and −out, and the total's
  sign classifies the reaction.
:::

:::can-you
- Define exothermic and endothermic reactions and give two balanced

- Draw and label energy level diagrams for both types (Paper 2C)?
- Calculate Q from mc∆T and quote it with the right sign and units?
- Convert Q into a molar enthalpy change, dividing by the **limiting**

- Describe all four calorimetry procedures with their key controls and
  assumptions?
- Explain why a combustion experiment under-reads, with evidence?
- Calculate an overall enthalpy change from bond energies, including
  double bonds and balancing coefficients (Paper 2C)?
:::

:::exam-hint
Past-paper items for this chapter cluster into three question shapes: a
calculate-from-data Q = mc∆T / ∆H = Q ÷ n chain (always with a sign mark),
a describe-the-method-and-its-errors practical question (assumptions,
improvements, evidence of incomplete combustion), and the Paper 2C
diagram-plus-bond-energy pair. Practise all three shapes against the
clock — and remember the chapter-wide discipline: sign first, then size.
:::

:::misconception
"Revising the formulas is enough for this chapter." The formula questions
are the predictable half. The unpredictable half is the practical
understanding — which mass goes into m, why the lid stays on the burner,
why two lines of best fit, why the zinc is in excess. The examiner tests
the *why* behind every step; the sections in this chapter were written so
each step's reason is one sentence you can quote.
:::
