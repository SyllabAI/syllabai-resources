How accurate was the −1020 kJ/mol obtained for ethanol? Data booklets quote
an accepted value of **−1370 kJ/mol** for the enthalpy change of combustion
of ethanol — 1370 kJ of heat should be given out when one mole burns. The
experimental value is **less exothermic** than expected: the experiment
seemed to give out less heat than it should have. Explaining that gap is a
guaranteed exam question, and the answer has two parts.

**Heat loss dominates.** The warm water gives out heat to the air; heat from
the flame escapes straight into the surroundings rather than into the water;
and heat is used to raise the temperature of the copper can and the
thermometer as well as the water. Every joule that takes those side-routes
is a joule the Q = mc∆T calculation never sees, so the measured temperature
rise is too small and the calculated ∆H is too little exothermic.

**The combustion is incomplete.** [[incomplete-combustion]] happens when
there is not enough oxygen present, and it releases **less** heat than
complete combustion. You can see the evidence directly: the flame of the
wick is often yellow-orange rather than blue, and soot (carbon) collects on
the bottom of the copper can. In complete combustion the flame is blue and
carbon dioxide is produced instead of carbon. Both observations — flame
colour and soot — are acceptable evidence in the exam.

When the same experiment is repeated across the alcohol family, a pattern
emerges. Plotting the molar enthalpy change of combustion against the number
of carbon atoms in the alcohol shows the combustion reaction getting **more
exothermic as the alcohol chain becomes longer** — longer alcohols give out
more heat energy per mole when they burn than shorter ones. The increase is
**regular**: each step from one alcohol to the next adds one extra CH₂ unit,
so the number of extra bonds broken and made increases in a regular way, and
so does the heat evolved.

One plotting discipline belongs with that graph. The points should be joined
as **discrete points, not a smooth curve**: a smooth curve is only correct
for a continuous independent variable — one that can take any value. There
is no such thing as an alcohol with 0.5 or 1.64 carbon atoms; the number of
carbon atoms is non-continuous, so a line through the points can mislead.
The comparison remains valid: repeat with other alcohols under conditions as
similar as possible, and the trend in heat evolved with chain length is
reliable.

:::key-point
Evaluation formula for this experiment: measured ∆H less exothermic than the
data booklet → (1) heat lost to surroundings (air, can, thermometer), (2)
incomplete combustion (yellow-orange flame, soot) because less heat is
released when oxygen is short.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Suggest why the student's value differs from the data book value" wants the
two-part answer above, with **evidence**. State the heat-loss routes
specifically (to the air, from the flame, into the can and thermometer), then
quote an observation for incomplete combustion — soot on the can or a
yellow-orange flame instead of blue. For the improvement question,
"insulate the can / add a lid / shield from draughts" attacks heat loss;
"burn the alcohol in plenty of oxygen" attacks incompleteness. Match each
improvement to the error it fixes.
:::

:::misconception
"A value of −1020 kJ/mol instead of −1370 kJ/mol means the arithmetic was
wrong." The arithmetic is fine; the *method* is systematically imperfect.
Heat escaping to the surroundings and incomplete combustion both push the
measured temperature rise down, and a smaller ∆T produces a smaller — less
exothermic — ∆H. The gap's direction is predictable, which is exactly what
makes it a favourite evaluation question.
:::
