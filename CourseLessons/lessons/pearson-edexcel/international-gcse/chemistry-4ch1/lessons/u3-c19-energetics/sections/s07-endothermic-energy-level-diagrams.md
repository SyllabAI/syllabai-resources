The endothermic [[energy-level-diagram]] is the mirror image of the exothermic
one. In an endothermic change the **products have more energy than the
reactants**, so we say the products are **less stable** than the reactants.
That extra energy has to come from somewhere, and it is taken from the
surroundings — for the thermal decomposition of carbonates in the laboratory,
it comes from the Bunsen burner.

Because the reactants are **gaining** energy, the enthalpy change of the
reaction is given a **positive** sign. The thermal decomposition of calcium
carbonate carries the value you should be able to quote and explain:

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)   ∆H = +178 kJ/mol

This means that 178 kJ of heat energy must be absorbed to convert one mole
of calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. On the diagram,
the arrow from reactants to products points **up**, the products level sits
**above** the reactants level, and ∆H is written beside the arrow with its
plus sign. Label the vertical axis "energy" and the horizontal axis "progress
of the reaction" exactly as before.

:::exam-hint
Paper 2C asks for both diagrams in a single question so the comparison marks
are easy to bank: exothermic = products lower, ∆H negative, arrow down;
endothermic = products higher, ∆H positive, arrow up. For the calcium
carbonate example, quote the value *with* its plus sign and attach the
meaning — "178 kJ absorbed **per mole** of calcium carbonate decomposed."
Omitting "per mole" leaves the answer incomplete; the value is defined for
the equation's amounts.
:::

:::misconception
"The +178 kJ/mol is the total heat the Bunsen burner supplied." The value is
the minimum absorbed **by the reaction** per mole of calcium carbonate
decomposed — the burner also heats the tube, the air and everything else,
which is why a real flame delivers far more than 178 kJ in practice. The
quoted ∆H is the reaction's share, not the fuel bill.
:::
