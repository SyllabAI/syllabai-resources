Very similar methods to the alcohol experiment measure molar enthalpy
changes for three more reaction families:

- **displacement reactions** — for example zinc metal in copper(II) sulfate
  solution;
- **dissolving** — for example ammonium chloride dissolving in water to form
  a solution;
- **neutralisation reactions** — for example potassium hydroxide solution
  with dilute hydrochloric acid.

These experiments also involve "heating water" — but this time the water is
part of the solutions themselves. The reaction happens in (or into) a
polystyrene cup, the solution warms or cools, and the same equation Q = mc∆T
converts the temperature change into heat energy. Two assumptions do the
converting, and you must be able to state both:

1. the **density** of the solution is the same as that of water, so 1 cm³ of
   solution has a mass of 1 g;
2. the **specific heat capacity** of the mixture is the same as that of
   water — reasonable, because the reaction mixture is mostly water.

From there the route to the [[molar-enthalpy-change]] is identical to the
combustion example: Q = mc∆T for the heat, moles of the limiting reactant
for n, then ∆H = Q ÷ n with the sign fixed by whether the temperature rose
(exothermic, negative) or fell (endothermic, positive). The next three
sections run the full procedure and calculation for each family in turn.

:::exam-hint
"Describe how to measure the enthalpy change of neutralisation" and its
siblings for displacement and dissolving are describe-the-method questions:
polystyrene cup in a beaker (insulation), measured volumes, initial
temperature, add and stir, record maximum (or minimum) temperature. Then the
assumption pair — density of solution = density of water, and specific heat
capacity of mixture = 4.18 J/g/°C because the mixture is mostly water —
usually sits on the mark scheme as its own line. Learn it verbatim once and
reuse it for all three families.
:::

:::misconception
"Each reaction family needs a different equation." The equation never
changes: Q = mc∆T, then ∆H = Q ÷ n. What changes is *what reacts* (a metal
and a salt solution, a salt and water, an acid and an alkali) and therefore
*which moles* you divide by. The constant structure is why one worked
example per family is enough preparation for all of them.
:::
