During chemical reactions, bonds in the reactants have to be broken and new
ones formed to make the products. The whole energy story of every reaction
rests on two facts:

**Breaking bonds needs energy — endothermic.**
**Making bonds releases energy — exothermic.**

Think about hydrogen burning in oxygen to make water:

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

Energy has to be supplied to break the bonds in the hydrogen molecules and
in the oxygen molecule. Energy is released when new bonds form between the
hydrogen and oxygen atoms inside the water molecules. In this particular
reaction the products are more stable than the reactants: the total energy
**released** when the covalent bonds form in the water molecules is **more
than** the energy **required** to break the covalent bonds in the hydrogen
and oxygen molecules. Released > absorbed, so the reaction is
[[exothermic]] overall.

Heating calcium carbonate runs the ledger the other way. Breaking up the
original bonds in the compound **uses more energy** than is obtained when
the new ones are made: the reactants are more stable than the products, so
when the reaction finishes more energy has been absorbed than released — an
[[endothermic]] change.

This is also where the small starting push many reactions need comes from:
energy is required to begin breaking the reactant bonds even in a reaction
that releases heat overall (the quantity is called the [[activation-energy]]
and this chapter's bond-energy arithmetic is what lets you account for it).
But the classification of a reaction depends only on the **balance**:
absorbed minus released.

- released **>** absorbed → exothermic, ∆H negative, products more stable.
- absorbed **>** released → endothermic, ∆H positive, products less stable.

:::key-point
The sign of ∆H is decided by competition: energy in to break reactant bonds
versus energy out from making product bonds. Whichever term is bigger wins
the sign — and the winner also decides which side of the energy level
diagram sits higher.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain, in terms of breaking and making bonds, why this reaction is
exothermic" has a three-part mark scheme: (1) bond breaking is endothermic /
needs energy, (2) bond making is exothermic / releases energy, (3) more
energy is released making the new bonds than is needed to break the original
ones. All three sentences, in that order. Omitting the comparison sentence
is the most common way to drop the third mark — naming both processes is not
the same as comparing them.
:::

:::misconception
"Bonds are like springs — breaking them must release the stored energy."
Everyday intuition says breaking things releases energy (snapping a stick,
smashing a plate), but chemical bonds work the opposite way: the bond is the
*low-energy arrangement*, so pulling it apart **costs** energy, and forming
it **pays** energy back. That inversion is the single most examined idea in
the chemistry-only half of this chapter.
:::
