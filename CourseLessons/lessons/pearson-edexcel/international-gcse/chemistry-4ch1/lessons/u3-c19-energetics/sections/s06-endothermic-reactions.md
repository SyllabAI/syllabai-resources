A reaction that **absorbs heat from the surroundings** is called an
[[endothermic]] reaction. Hold a test-tube in which an endothermic reaction
is happening and the tube gets **colder** — heat is flowing the other way,
out of your fingers and the air and into the reaction mixture.

In energy terms the picture mirrors the exothermic case exactly. In an
endothermic reaction the **products have more chemical energy than the
reactants**. To supply the extra energy needed to convert low-energy
reactants into high-energy products, heat energy is absorbed from the
surroundings and converted to chemical energy stored in the new bonds. The
temperature of the reaction mixture and the surroundings goes **down**
because heat energy has been drained and converted into a different form.

Chemical cold packs work on exactly this principle: an endothermic reaction
inside the pack absorbs heat from the surroundings, lowering the temperature
of the injured arm it rests against.

The everyday laboratory example is the [[thermal-decomposition]] of metal
carbonates, which you met with Group 2 chemistry. These are endothermic —
you have to heat a carbonate **constantly** to make it decompose, because
the reaction keeps drawing heat from the flame. Copper(II) carbonate (green)
decomposes on heating to produce copper(II) oxide (black):

CuCO₃(s) → CuO(s) + CO₂(g)

Zinc carbonate decomposes similarly to form zinc oxide when heated.

One warning that examiners turn into questions: **not every reaction that
needs heating is endothermic.** Sometimes we heat a mixture simply to make
the reaction go faster — the burning of magnesium needs a hot flame to start
it, yet the reaction itself is fiercely exothermic. The direction of the
temperature change *during* the reaction tells you which kind you have; the
presence of a Bunsen burner does not.

:::exam-hint
The examiner's favourite endothermic definition question adds a twist:
"explain why the decomposition of copper(II) carbonate is endothermic." The
full-mark chain is: the products have **more** chemical energy than the
reactants → the extra energy must be absorbed from the surroundings as heat
→ so the temperature of the surroundings falls and heating must continue
throughout the reaction. Keep the heating-continuously point — it is what
separates an endothermic decomposition from a merely slow reaction.
:::

:::misconception
"If you have to light a Bunsen burner, the reaction must be endothermic."
Heat applied to *start* or *speed up* a reaction tells you nothing about the
direction of the overall energy change. Magnesium burning needs a flame to
get going and then releases far more heat than it absorbed. Classify by the
temperature change of the mixture itself: drops during the change →
endothermic; rises → exothermic.
:::
