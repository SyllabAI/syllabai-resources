A reaction that **gives out heat energy to the surroundings** is called an
[[exothermic]] reaction. Hold a test-tube in which an exothermic reaction is
happening and the tube gets warmer: heat is flowing out of the reaction
mixture into the glass, your fingers and the air. That is the definition and
the observation in one — heat leaves the reaction for the surroundings, and
the temperature of everything around the mixture goes **up**.

The clearest way to see what is happening energetically is to compare the
chemical energy stored in the bonds of the chemicals before and after. In an
exothermic reaction the **products have less chemical energy than the
reactants**. The difference does not vanish — it is converted to heat energy
and released to the surroundings. Chemical energy stored in bonds becomes
heat energy in the environment, so the reaction mixture and its surroundings
warm up.

The classic demonstration is adding water to solid calcium oxide. The heat
produced is enough to boil the water and raise steam, and calcium hydroxide
forms:

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(s)

The reaction releases so much heat that the water can be seen steaming as the
solid crumbles — a dramatic confirmation that chemical energy has been
converted to heat on a large scale.

:::did-you-know
Calcium oxide is known as [[quicklime]]. Adding water to it is described as
slaking it, and the calcium hydroxide produced is known as slaked lime. The
same chemistry underlies traditional lime mortars, which harden as the
quicklime slakes and then slowly absorbs carbon dioxide from the air.
:::

:::safety
It is less hazardous to use a lump of calcium oxide rather than powdered
calcium oxide, and skin contact must be avoided — the solid is caustic and
the slaking reaction scalds. Wear eye protection.
:::

:::exam-hint
A one-mark "know" question wants exactly this chain: exothermic → heat given
out **to the surroundings** → temperature of the surroundings rises. If the
question then asks you to explain in terms of energy, add the second chain:
products have less chemical energy than reactants → the difference is
released as heat. Saying "it gets hot" without naming the surroundings earns
nothing; the heat flow direction is the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"An exothermic reaction must produce a flame." Flames belong to the small
subset of exothermic reactions that burn — adding water to quicklime, or
dilute acid neutralising dilute alkali, is fiercely exothermic with no flame
at all. The test is the temperature of the surroundings, not light.
:::
