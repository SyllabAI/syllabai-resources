You have already met dozens of [[exothermic]] reactions across this course — this
section gathers the families the examiners expect you to recognise on sight.

**Any reaction that produces a flame is exothermic.** Burning things releases
heat energy. The clearest example is hydrogen burning in oxygen, producing
water and a large amount of heat:

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

The burning of hydrogen is used in oxy-hydrogen cutting equipment
underwater, because the flame is hot enough to cut metal even beneath the
surface. You met the same reaction as the test for hydrogen (the squeaky
pop) and in the reactivity-series work.

**Metals reacting with acids** are exothermic. When magnesium reacts with
dilute sulfuric acid, the mixture gets very warm:

Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

**Neutralisation reactions** are exothermic. About the only interesting thing
you can observe when sodium hydroxide solution reacts with dilute
hydrochloric acid is that the temperature rises:

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Nothing changes colour, no gas bubbles out — but the mixture is measurably
warmer than the two starting solutions. You will measure exactly how much
heat this family releases later in the chapter.

**[[displacement-reaction]]s** are exothermic. The most spectacular example is
the thermite reaction between powdered aluminium and iron(III) oxide, a
displacement (competition) reaction that releases so much heat that the iron
formed is molten — the reaction is used to weld railway lines together:

2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s)

:::exam-hint
"Give two examples of exothermic reactions" is a gift question if you keep a
mental list: combustion (hydrogen, or any fuel), metal + acid,
neutralisation, displacement. Name the chemicals, not just the family —
"magnesium with dilute sulfuric acid" scores; "a reaction that gets hot" does
not. If asked for equations, any balanced equation from this section is safe.
:::

:::misconception
"Neutralisation cannot be exothermic because salt solution does not feel
warm." With dilute solutions the temperature rise is only a few degrees —
real, measurable with a thermometer, but easy to miss with a finger. The
thermometer, not your hand, decides whether a reaction is exothermic; later
in this chapter the measured rise for KOH + HCl works out at ΔH = −55.4
kJ/mol.
:::
