Breaking chemical bonds requires energy, and the stronger the bond, the
more energy is needed to break it. The [[bond-energy]] is defined as **the
amount of energy needed to break 1 mole of covalent bonds in gaseous
molecules**, measured in kJ/mol. The same number works in reverse: the bond
energy is also the amount of energy released when 1 mole of those bonds
**forms**.

Two examples anchor the idea. The Cl–Cl bond energy is **243 kJ/mol**: it
takes 243 kJ to break all the Cl–Cl bonds in 1 mole of chlorine gas,
splitting each molecule into two chlorine atoms — and if those atoms
recombine into their original molecules, exactly 243 kJ is released again.
The H–Cl bond energy is **432 kJ/mol**: if you see 2HCl(g) in an equation,
it takes 2 × 432 kJ to break all the H–Cl bonds in the two moles of hydrogen
chloride — or 2 × 432 kJ will be released when 2HCl(g) is formed from H and
Cl atoms. The coefficient in front multiplies the bond energy; that one
sentence is the whole counting rule of the next section.

| Bond | C–H | C–Cl | I–Cl | Cl–Cl | I–I | H–Cl | H–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bond energy / kJ/mol | 413 | 346 | 234 | 243 | 151 | 432 | 298 |

Some bonds are much stronger than others. A striking comparison from the
table: the bond between iodine and hydrogen (298 kJ/mol) is about **twice as
strong** as the bond between two iodine atoms (151 kJ/mol).

Bond energies can be used to calculate how much heat will be absorbed or
released during reactions of **covalent molecules**. If a reaction involves
ionic compounds, different energy terms are needed — beyond International
GCSE. The method is an estimate by dismantling and rebuilding: calculate how
much energy would be needed to break the reactants up into individual atoms,
then how much would be given out when those atoms recombine into the new
arrangements of the products.

:::exam-hint
Bond-energy questions always supply the table — you never memorise values.
What you must bring is the counting discipline: multiply each bond energy by
**how many moles of that bond** appear in the balanced equation. The 1-mole
definition is also a mark: writing "energy to break a bond" instead of
"energy to break **1 mole** of bonds in gaseous molecules" loses the
definition mark. Note the word *gaseous* — it constrains where the method is
valid (see the next section's worked example).
:::

:::misconception
"A bond energy of 432 kJ/mol for H–Cl means one HCl molecule needs 432 kJ
to break." The value is per **mole** of bonds — one molecule needs 432 kJ
divided by the Avogadro number, an utterly tiny amount. All bond-energy
arithmetic in this course runs in per-mole quantities multiplied by the
equation's coefficients.
:::
