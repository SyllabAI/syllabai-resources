Measuring the heat absorbed or given out by a reaction is
[[calorimetry]], and it is more straightforward than the name suggests. The
idea: let the reaction's heat change the temperature of a known mass of
water, then use Q = mc∆T to calculate the heat released. The mass, specific
heat capacity and temperature change all belong to **the substance heated**
— the water. If you also know how many moles of reactant were used up, you
can divide Q by n to reach the molar enthalpy change ∆H in kJ/mol.

The classic International GCSE experiment measures the heat given off when
small alcohols burn — methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol. The
alcohol burns in a small spirit burner and the heat produced warms water in
a copper can (the calorimeter). The molar enthalpy change of combustion is
how much heat energy is released when **one mole** of the alcohol burns.

The procedure, in the order that earns method marks:

1. Measure **100 cm³ of cold water** with a measuring cylinder and transfer
   it to the copper can.
2. Take the **initial temperature** of the water.
3. Weigh a spirit burner containing the alcohol **with its lid on** — the
   lid stays on whenever the wick is not lit, to stop the alcohol
   evaporating.
4. Arrange the apparatus so the burner can heat the water in the can, and
   shield it as far as possible to prevent draughts.
5. Light the wick. Stop heating when the water has a reasonable temperature
   rise (about 40.0 °C); extinguish the flame by putting the lid back on the
   wick.
6. Stir the water thoroughly and measure the **maximum temperature**.
7. Weigh the spirit burner again with its lid on.
8. Repeat with the same alcohol to check reliability, then repeat with other
   alcohols.

How big a temperature rise should you aim for? There is no ideal answer — it
is a deliberate compromise. The higher the temperature the water reaches,
the greater the heat losses to the surroundings during the experiment; but
if you only warm the water a little, errors in reading the thermometer and
in finding the small mass change of the alcohol become too significant. A
rise of around 40 °C balances the two.

:::safety
Wear eye protection. Never carry a lit spirit burner, and never fill or
re-fill one when there is a naked flame nearby. Keep the lid on the burner
whenever the wick is not lit.
:::

:::key-point
The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance is burned is called the
[[enthalpy-change-of-combustion]]. Every combustion result in this chapter
is quoted per mole of fuel burned.
:::

:::exam-hint
Method questions on this experiment reward the *control* details: lid on
when not lit (stops evaporation changing the mass), shielding from draughts
(cuts heat loss), stir before reading (distributes the heat so the maximum
is true), weigh the burner *with* the lid both times. If asked for an
improvement, "use a lid or shield around the can to reduce heat loss" or
"a wind shield to prevent draughts" are the standard reliable answers.
:::

:::misconception
"The mass to use in Q = mc∆T is the mass of alcohol that burned." The Q
equation describes the **water**: m is the 100 g of water in the can (100
cm³ × density 1 g/cm³). The mass of alcohol burned — the change in the
burner's reading — enters later, when you convert Q into a per-mole value.
Mixing the two masses up is the single most common arithmetic error on this
experiment.
:::
