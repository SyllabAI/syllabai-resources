You can measure the amount of heat energy taken in or released in a chemical
reaction. It is called the [[enthalpy-change]] of the reaction and is given
the symbol ∆H. ∆H is pronounced "delta H" — the Greek letter ∆ is used to
mean "change in", so ∆H means "change in heat". One subtlety matters: it is
not possible to measure how much enthalpy (H) a substance *has* — you can
only measure the **change** in enthalpy when it reacts. The enthalpy change
is the amount of heat energy taken in or given out in a chemical reaction;
it is the difference between the energy of the products and the energy of
the reactants.

∆H is given a minus or a plus sign to show whether heat is being given out
or absorbed, and you always read the sign from the point of view of the
reactants. For an exothermic reaction, ∆H is **negative**, because the
reactants are losing energy as heat — heat is transferred to the
surroundings, which then get warmer. ∆H is measured in units of kJ/mol
(kilojoules per mole); you may also see the units written kJ mol⁻¹.

Written next to an equation, the enthalpy change looks like this:

Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)   ∆H = −466.9 kJ/mol

The ∆H here represents the enthalpy change of the reaction: 466.9 kJ of heat
is given out when one mole of magnesium reacts in this way. You know heat
has been given out because ∆H carries a negative sign.

The word [[stability]] describes the relative energies of reactants and
products: **the more energy a chemical has, the less stable it is.** So when
an exothermic reaction runs, the products (lower energy) are more stable than
the reactants — energy has drained out of the chemicals into the
surroundings. And remember the vocabulary: the reactants are the chemicals
you start with. The per-mole basis is why the [[mole]] matters here — every
∆H you quote is for the amounts shown in the balanced equation.

:::key-point
∆H is the enthalpy change: the heat energy taken in or given out by a
reaction, measured from the reactants' point of view. Negative ∆H = heat
given out (exothermic). Positive ∆H = heat taken in (endothermic). Units:
kJ/mol (equivalently kJ mol⁻¹).
:::

:::exam-hint
Two habits earn the sign-and-units marks every time. First, never quote a
∆H without its sign — an answer of "466.9 kJ/mol" for the magnesium reaction
above loses the mark that "−466.9 kJ/mol" scores. Second, keep the units per
mole: if a question gives you heat in joules for 0.78 g of fuel, dividing by
moles before quoting kJ/mol is part of the calculation, not decoration (the
full method arrives in the worked example later in this chapter).
:::

:::misconception
"A negative ∆H means energy is missing." The minus sign records a **loss
from the reactants**, not a loss from the universe: the heat has been
transferred to the surroundings, where it warms them up. Energy is
conserved — the sign just says which way it flowed relative to the chemicals.
:::
