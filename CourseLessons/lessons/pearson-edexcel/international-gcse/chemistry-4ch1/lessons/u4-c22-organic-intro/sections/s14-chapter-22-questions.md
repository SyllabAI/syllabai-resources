You now hold the complete introductory toolkit for organic chemistry:
the [[hydrocarbon]] definition with its *only* discipline; the five
formula types and the jobs each one does; the [[homologous-series]] and
the [[functional-group]] that power them; the chain-length code and the
full decode procedure for names; structural [[isomerism]] with its
break-a-bond test; a first look at the alcohols; and the three
reaction classes with their classifier instincts. Every later organic
chapter — crude oil, alkanes, alkenes, alcohols, carboxylic acids,
esters, polymers — assumes these skills as standing equipment. The
question bank for this chapter joins the platform by the
specification points below, so every practice item you are served
targets exactly the machinery these sections built.

Before opening the bank, audit yourself against the chapter's core
moves. Can you run the two-element hydrocarbon audit and name the
compounds that stay outside the organic family? Can you move between
empirical, molecular, general, structural and displayed formulae —
including the CnH2n+2 arithmetic that generates a dodecane formula on
demand? Can you decode a six-carbon name into a structure and name a
structure from its code, branches and all? Can you enumerate the
three C₅H₁₂ isomers and explain why the phantom fourth is never
real? Can you classify any reaction the chapter can throw at you as
substitution, addition or combustion? Anything that wobbled is a
section to revisit, not a reason to start over.

The bank itself trains five question shapes. Naming-and-naming-back
items hand you condensed formulae to name and names to draw, from
one-carbon methane up to six-carbon chains and alcohols. Isomer
items ask for the definition, then for drawing and naming the sets —
the two isomers of C₄H₁₀, the five of C₆H₁₄, the double-bond set of
C₄H₈, and the stretch items that look beyond the −OH pattern. Formula
items test the ratio logic (empirical versus molecular) and the
general-formula arithmetic. Classification items present reactions
and demand [[substitution-reaction]], [[addition-reaction]] or combustion — with balanced
equations to write on the spot. And the analysis item pairs a
compound table with dot-and-cross drawing, displayed-formula drawing,
a general-formula recall and a full combustion equation. One warning
carried with the bank itself: some items deliberately reach slightly
beyond exam level — treat them as strength training, not as
evidence you are behind.

:::summary
- **Hydrocarbon (4.1)**: compound of hydrogen and carbon *only*; the
  two-element audit (carbon, hydrogen, nothing else) settles every
  membership question. Saturated = single C–C bonds only;
  unsaturated = contains double or triple C–C bonds.
- **Formula types (4.2)**: empirical = simplest whole-number ratio
  (calculable from data); molecular = actual atom counts (a whole-number
  multiple of the empirical formula; tells you nothing about joining);
  general = the whole family at once (alkanes CnH2n+2, alkenes CnH2n,
  alcohols CnH2n+2O); structural and displayed = the joining (all bonds
  drawn = displayed; not all = condensed structural).
- **Family terms (4.3)**: homologous series = similar chemistry from
  the same functional group, −CH₂− steps between members; functional
  group = the atom/group that determines chemical properties (−OH for
  alcohols, C=C for alkenes); isomerism = same molecular formula,
  different structural formulae.
- **IUPAC naming (4.4)**: names are codes — meth/eth/prop/but/pent/hex
  counts the longest chain; *ane*/*ene*/*ol* endings carry the bond
  and group information; branches are methyl/ethyl with position
  numbers from the smaller-numbers end; *di* marks a doubled branch.
- **Structures from formulae (4.5)**: enumerate isomers by varying
  chain length, branch positions, double-bond positions; discard
  rotations; C₅H₁₂ has exactly three isomers (pentane,
  2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane).
- **Reaction classes (4.6)**: combustion = burn in oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O
  (three equations to know); substitution = replacement (alkane +
  halogen → two products); addition = joining onto C=C (alkene +
  halogen → one product). Mechanisms are not required.
:::

:::can-you
- Define a hydrocarbon with *only* in place, and say which four
  carbon-compound families stay outside organic chemistry?
- Write empirical, molecular and general formulae for the same
  compound, and explain what each one tells you?
- State the three-clause homologous-series definition and give the
  functional group of the alcohols and the alkenes?
- Decode 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene into a structure, and name the
  three C₅H₁₂ isomers from their structures?
- Draw propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol and say why both are C₃H₈O yet
  different compounds?
- Classify ethane-plus-bromine and ethene-plus-bromine by product
  count, and write the ethanol combustion equation?
:::

:::misconception
"Isomer enumeration is guesswork — either you spot them or you don't."
Enumeration is a procedure: fix the molecular formula; walk chain
lengths from longest to shortest; at each length place branches (and
double bonds) at every distinct position; strike out anything
convertible by rotation; audit every survivor against the four-bond
rule. Run it on C₄H₁₀ and you get two; on C₅H₁₂, three. The students
who "spot" isomers quickly are running the same procedure silently —
make it explicit and you match them every time.
:::
