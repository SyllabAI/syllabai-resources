The chapter closes its teaching with the third term of specification
4.6's toolkit in action: every organic reaction you meet at
International GCSE belongs to one of three classes — **combustion**,
**substitution** or **addition**. All organic compounds you will study
undergo combustion: it is just another word for burning, and it means
reaction with oxygen. Burn a [[hydrocarbon]] in **excess** oxygen and
the products are always carbon dioxide and water, together with a
large release of heat energy. Propane burning — the flame of a
camping stove, which is where propane is so often used — follows the
pattern:

C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)

Butene burning shows the same shape with a different chain:

C₄H₈(g) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)

Alcohols undergo similar reactions, and ethanol's equation is the one
to memorise:

C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

Balancing these is ordinary equation bookkeeping with one trap — the
oxygen atom already inside the alcohol (see the exam hint). The
products never change: complete combustion of anything organic in
excess oxygen gives CO₂ and H₂O, and the equations differ only in the
coefficients you need to balance carbon and hydrogen.

The other two classes are distinguished by what happens to the
reacting molecule. A **[[substitution-reaction]]** occurs when an atom
or group of atoms is **replaced** by a different atom or group —
something swaps in, something swaps out, and the molecule keeps its
overall shape. The standard example is a hydrogen atom in an alkane
being replaced by a halogen atom: ethane reacting with bromine gas
swaps one H for one Br,

CH₃CH₃ + Br₂ → CH₃CH₂Br + HBr

— bromoethane and hydrogen bromide as the two products. The detail
behind this reaction belongs to the alkanes chapter (u4:c24), but the
classification logic is complete here: count what entered and what
came out, and a replacement has happened.

An **[[addition-reaction]]** is the opposite gesture: something is
**added** to a molecule without taking anything away. Only unsaturated
molecules can do this, because the double bond carries spare bonding
capacity — and alkenes are the workhorses. Ethene reacting with
bromine takes the whole Br₂ molecule onto the two carbons:

CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br

One reactant set, **one** product — nothing is ejected, because
nothing needed replacing; the double bond simply opened and each
carbon took a bromine. Compare the two bromine reactions side by side
and the classification writes itself: ethane + bromine gives **two**
products (a substitution — the swapped-out hydrogen had to go
somewhere), ethene + bromine gives **one** (an addition). That
product-count contrast is the fastest classifier you own.

:::exam-hint
Specification wording for this section: *understand how to classify
reactions of organic compounds as substitution, addition and
combustion* — and the statement ends with a gift: **knowledge of
reaction mechanisms is not required**. You classify by observing what
happens (something burned? something swapped? something joined?),
never by explaining the electron-level how. Learn the three
one-sentence definitions, the product-count contrast for the two
bromine cases, and the three combustion equations, and 4.6 is fully
covered.
:::

:::misconception
"Ethane with bromine and ethene with bromine are the same reaction
because both use bromine." Same reactant fragment, different class.
Ethane is saturated — every bond is spoken for — so bromine can only
**replace** a hydrogen, and the displaced hydrogen leaves as HBr
(two products; substitution). Ethene is unsaturated — the C=C has
spare capacity — so the whole Br₂ **adds on** and nothing leaves (one
product; addition). Watch the products, not the reactants: what the
molecule does with the incoming atoms is the classification.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the three combustion equations (propane, butene,
ethanol) and name the products of complete combustion in excess
oxygen? Can you define substitution and addition in one sentence
each, and classify the two ethane/ethene-plus-bromine reactions by
product count? Can you explain why alkenes but not alkanes undergo
addition? Those are the last teaching marks of the chapter — the

:::
