Enrichment section: the isomerism in specification 4.3 is the
structural kind you met in s09, and that is the kind the papers test.
But the word *isomerism* hides a second world, and one glance at it
will make the structural version feel like solid ground. To be
precise, what you have studied should be called **structural
isomerism** — same molecular formula, different structural formulae,
atoms joined in a different order. The other important kind of [[isomerism]] is
**optical isomerism**, and it inverts the premise: optical isomers
have their atoms joined in the **same** order (identical structural
formulae) but arranged differently in three-dimensional space.

They are mirror images of each other — and, like your two hands, they
cannot be superimposed. Rotate one as you like, and it never lies
flat on top of the other: every alignment that matches one part
misaligns another. Optical isomerism appears when a molecule contains
a **chiral centre** — a carbon atom connected to four *different*
atoms or groups. The tetrahedral shape around that carbon (the
molecular geometry you met with diamond in u1:c08) is what locks the
mirror-image arrangement in place: rotate one molecule 180° in space
and it still refuses to stack on its twin.

Here is the strange part: optical isomers have identical chemical
properties — **except when they interact with something else that is
chiral**. Biology is chiral through and through. Apart from glycine,
every naturally occurring amino acid exists in both optical forms,
which means every protein in your body is built from one-handed
building blocks — so your body's receptors can tell the two versions
of a molecule apart. The same compound can smell of spearmint in one
form and of caraway seed in the other, purely because the two mirror
images fit differently into the chiral protein receptors in your
nose.

That asymmetry is not a curiosity — it is a safety-critical fact of
pharmaceutical chemistry. In the 1960s, a drug molecule was given to
pregnant women to treat morning sickness because one of its optical
isomers had anti-nausea effects; unknown to everyone at the time, the
manufacture also produced the mirror-image isomer, and that other
form caused severe limb deformities in thousands of children. The
thalidomide tragedy rewrote the industry's rulebook: drug companies
now spend heavily on separating the optical isomers of every new
chiral drug, in case one form carries side effects the other does
not. One molecule, two mirror images, two biological fates — the
cleanest possible demonstration that in chemistry, structure is
meaning. (Extension, beyond even the main line here: the two forms
of a chiral molecule are labelled with the prefixes R and S to record
the configuration of atoms around the chiral centre.)

:::exam-hint
Keep the two isomerisms straight by their one-line signatures:
structural isomers differ in **bonding order**; optical isomers
differ in **three-dimensional arrangement** only. For International
GCSE, "isomerism" in specification 4.3 means the structural kind —
same formula, different structures, break-a-bond test (s09). Optical
isomerism is beyond-the-paper enrichment, but the chiral-centre idea
— a carbon with four different groups — is a beautiful application of
the tetrahedral carbon geometry you already own.
:::

:::misconception
"Mirror images are just the same molecule seen from the other side."
For a chiral molecule, no rotation ever superimposes the two forms —
that refusal is the definition. The consequences are chemical, not
philosophical: one form of carvone smells of spearmint, its mirror
image of caraway; one form of a drug treats nausea, its mirror image
caused a tragedy. Same connections, different space — different
compound in every way that biology can detect.
:::
