For anything larger than the smallest molecules, a fully displayed
formula is time-consuming to draw, so chemistry uses a set of
simplifications — all of them still [[structural-formula]]e, all of
them still showing exactly how the atoms join. You can write a chain
using grouped labels: CH₃ or CH₂ standing in for a carbon with its
attached hydrogens, instead of drawing every carbon-hydrogen bond.
The butane molecule (molecular formula C₄H₁₀ — a [[molecular-formula]]
that alone tells you nothing about joining) can be written as
CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ or as CH₃−CH₂−CH₂−CH₃. Both condensed forms show exactly
how the four carbons link in a chain; the molecular formula does not.

The displayed [[displayed-formula]] draws every bond as a line, and
several different-looking drawings can all be correct. Twist a butane
chain in the middle and you have not made a new molecule — the chain
has simply rotated, because rotation around single carbon-carbon
bonds happens freely in real molecules too. Molecular models make
this vivid: blobs of modelling clay joined by matchstick "bonds" will
twist into every drawn arrangement without any bond breaking. By
convention you draw the structure with all carbon atoms in a
straight line, which is why textbook drawings look flatter than the
molecules they describe.

Double bonds follow the same logic with one extra line. Propene,
C₃H₆, contains a carbon-carbon double bond, drawn as **two lines**
between the two carbon atoms — two pairs of shared electrons, the
C=C you met as the alkenes' functional group in s04. You would
normally write propene in a simplified condensed form as CH₃CH=CH₂,
with the double bond shown. More advanced shorthand sometimes hides
the C=C bond inside an abbreviated written formula; at International
GCSE you should always show it. (The same display discipline applies
to writing structure in equations: any unambiguous structural version
is acceptable — but in the drawn-answer cases below, the safest habit
is the full displayed formula.)

:::key-point
If you are asked to **draw** the structure of a molecule in an
International GCSE exam, always draw it as a displayed formula. You
cannot lose marks for showing every bond, whereas a condensed
shorthand can cost you. When you are simply writing a structure
inside an equation, either version is fine — choose whichever you
write fastest without errors.
:::

:::key-point
The boundary between the two structural forms is a bond count. If
**all** the bonds are shown as lines, it is a displayed formula. If
**not all** of them are shown — but the joining is still
unambiguous — it is a (condensed) structural formula. Some condensed
forms show some bonds (CH₃−CH₂−CH₃), others none (CH₃CH₂CH₃); both
are structural, neither is displayed.
:::

:::exam-hint
Models are not exam furniture, they are thinking tools — and rotation
is the idea they teach best. If two drawn structures differ only by a
twist of the chain, they are the same molecule, because single bonds
rotate. Test any "are these the same or different?" question by
asking: could I turn the first drawing into the second without
breaking a bond? If yes, same molecule; if no, different molecule —
and that question is exactly what the isomerism section formalises.
:::

:::worked-example
Draw the displayed formula of propene, CH₃CH=CH₂ (4.5: write the
displayed formula given the molecular formula).

1. Place the three carbons in a line, in the order the condensed
   formula reads: C1, C2, C3.
2. Draw the double bond (two lines) between C1 and C2 — the formula
   says the C=C sits at the start of the chain.
3. Add hydrogens until every carbon has exactly four bonds: C1 takes
   two H atoms (double bond uses two of its four bonds), C2 takes
   one H, C3 takes three H.
4. Audit the count: three carbons, and 2 + 1 + 3 = 6 hydrogens —
   matching C₃H₆. The two-lines rule and the four-bond rule between
   them guarantee the drawing is correct.
:::

:::misconception
"Two structures that look different must be different molecules."
Appearance is not identity in organic chemistry: chains twist, and
single-bond rotation changes the drawing without changing the
molecule. The test is bonding, not layout — same atoms, same
connections, same molecule. Only when bonds would have to **break**
to convert one drawing into the other are you looking at genuinely
different structures (the isomerism idea, two sections ahead).
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write butane in condensed form two different ways and say why
both mean the same molecule? Can you state the all-bonds rule that
separates displayed from condensed structural formulae? Can you draw
propene's displayed formula from its condensed form, using the
four-bond audit to check your hydrogen count? And can you explain
why drawing a twisted chain does not create a new substance? If all
four are secure, naming is next.
:::
