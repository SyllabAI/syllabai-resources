Organic chemistry needs more than one way to write a molecule down,
because different jobs need different information. The five
representations in specification 4.2 — empirical, molecular, general,
structural and displayed formulae — are not five levels of difficulty;
they are five lenses, and this section installs the first two. An
[[empirical-formula]] shows the **simplest whole-number ratio** of the
atoms in a compound, and it is the version you can calculate from
experimental data (the Chapter 5, u1:c05 toolkit). On its own it
cannot identify the molecule: without more information you cannot
recover the true formula from the ratio alone. Methane's empirical
formula is CH₄ — which happens to be its molecular formula too. But
ethane's empirical formula is CH₃, while its molecular formula is
C₂H₆.

A [[molecular-formula]] counts the **actual number** of each type of
atom in one molecule. Butane is C₄H₁₀; ethene is C₂H₄. Every molecular
formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula: a
compound with empirical formula CH₂ could have molecular formula
C₂H₄, C₃H₆ or C₄H₈ — the carbon-to-hydrogen ratio must always match
the ratio in the empirical formula. That multiplicity is exactly why
the empirical formula alone cannot pin a molecule down.

Here is the limitation that shapes everything else in this chapter: a
molecular formula tells you **nothing** about how the atoms are joined
together. Two completely different compounds can share one molecular
formula — the isomerism section (s09) makes that idea concrete. In
practice, molecular formulae are used rarely in organic chemistry,
precisely because they carry no bonding information. The one place
they earn their keep is combustion equations for simple hydrocarbons,
where the internal structure genuinely does not matter:

2C₄H₁₀(g) + 13O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 10H₂O(l)

In almost every other situation — naming, drawing, predicting
reactions — you will reach for a structural or a displayed formula
instead. Those two, plus the general formula that unites whole
families, are the subjects of the next two sections.

:::exam-hint
The formula-type definitions are quotable, and the ratio check is a
one-mark staple: given molecular formula C₄H₈, the empirical formula
is CH₂ (divide through by the largest whole number possible). More
features of homologous series — the family vocabulary these formulae
feed into — are collected in the alkanes chapter (u4:c24). If a
question asks *which* formula tells you how atoms are joined, the
answer is never the molecular formula: it is the structural or
displayed formula.
:::

:::misconception
"The empirical formula and molecular formula are always different
formulae." Methane breaks that rule on sight: its simplest ratio CH₄
is already the true formula, so the two coincide. The general law is
the multiple — the molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the
empirical formula, and that multiple can be 1. Check by dividing the
subscripts by their largest common factor; if nothing divides, the
formulae are identical.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define empirical formula (ratio) and molecular formula
(actual counts) in one sentence each? Given CH₂ as an empirical
formula, can you write three valid molecular formulae whose
carbon-to-hydrogen ratio matches it? Can you name the one reaction
type where molecular formulae are actually convenient, and explain
why structure-free formulae are fine there? If yes, the family
vocabulary is next.
:::
