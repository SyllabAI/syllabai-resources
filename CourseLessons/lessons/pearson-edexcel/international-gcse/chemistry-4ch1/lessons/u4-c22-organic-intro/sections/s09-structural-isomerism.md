A straight chain is an unbranched chain — that vocabulary point clears
the way for the third big term of specification 4.3. **Structural
[[isomerism]]** exists because one [[molecular-formula]] can clothe
itself in more than one structure: structural isomers are molecules
with the **same molecular formula but different
[[structural-formula]]e**. The atoms in different isomers are joined in a different
order. Examples make it real.

Pick up five carbon atoms and twelve hydrogen atoms — physically, as
molecular models — and try to join them. You will find more than one
way to do it, and every arrangement you build is a legitimate
molecule. All of them have the molecular formula C₅H₁₂; each has a
different structure. There are **three** isomers in the set:

- **pentane** — the straight-chain (unbranched) isomer, five carbons
  in a row;
- **2-methylbutane** — a four-carbon chain (*butane*) with one methyl
  group on the second carbon;
- **2,2-dimethylpropane** — a three-carbon chain (*propane*) with two
  methyl groups on the second carbon, the *di* marking the pair.

The naming you built in s08 now does real work: each isomer's name is
a complete structural report. And there is a physical test hiding in
the model exercise: try converting one isomer into another by bending
or twisting. It cannot be done. Bending and rotating never convert an
isomer — you would have to **break bonds** and reconnect the atoms.
That break-a-bond test is the simplest isomer check you own: same
formula, no rotation route, different connection pattern — isomers.

Students hunting a fourth C₅H₁₂ isomer frequently produce one, and it
is always the same disappointment: the "new" drawing is 2-methylbutane
rotated in space. Guard against the illusion by always drawing isomers
with the longest carbon chain horizontal (or by redrawing the set
without hydrogen atoms first) — then the duplicates expose themselves.
In an exam, show the hydrogens even though the carbon-skeleton sketch
is the faster comparison tool.

:::key-point
Warning: **isomer** and **isotope** are different words for unrelated
ideas. Isotopes are different atoms of the *same element* — same
atomic number, different mass numbers. Isomers are *different
compounds* — same molecular formula, different structural formulae.
One is a contrast within an element; the other is a contrast between
whole molecules. Keep them apart in every answer.
:::

:::exam-hint
Isomer-count questions reward method over inspiration: fix the
molecular formula, then vary chain length, then vary branch positions
(and for alkenes, double-bond position — next section). To compare
candidates quickly, redraw each as a carbon skeleton only — hydrogens
omitted — so duplicates and rotations become visible at a glance.
Then, before writing the final answer, redraw with all hydrogens
shown: the exam wants complete structures, and the four-bond audit is
your self-check.
:::

:::misconception
"I found a fourth isomer of C₅H₁₂." You found a rotation. Every
apparent fourth member of the set is 2-methylbutane seen from a new
angle — same connections, same molecule. The break-a-bond test
settles it: if no bond must break to superimpose the drawings, they
are one compound, not two. Draw the longest chain horizontal every
time and the phantom isomer never appears again.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the structural-isomerism definition in its two halves
(same formula, different structure)? Can you draw and name all three
C₅H₁₂ isomers, identifying the longest chain each name is built on?
Can you run the break-a-bond test and explain why rotation never
produces a new isomer? And can you say precisely how an isomer
differs from an isotope? If those four hold, the enrichment section
on mirror-image isomerism is next.
:::
