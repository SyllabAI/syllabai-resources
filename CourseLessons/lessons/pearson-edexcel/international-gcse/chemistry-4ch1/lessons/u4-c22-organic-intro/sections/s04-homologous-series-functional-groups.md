Before the third and fourth formula types, you need the family idea
that organises the whole of organic chemistry. Carbon always forms
four bonds and hydrogen always forms one; oxygen forms two and a
halogen atom forms one (that is covalent-bonding bookkeeping from
Chapter 8, u1:c08, not a new rule). With those counts fixed, organic
compounds fall into families — many different families, called
**homologous series**. The alkanes, alkenes, alcohols, carboxylic
acids and esters are all homologous series, each developed in its own
later chapter; the family idea itself is one of the three terms in
specification 4.3.

A [[homologous-series]] is a series of compounds with **similar
chemical properties because they share the same functional group**,
and consecutive members differ from the next by exactly one −CH₂−
unit. That single sentence packs three membership tests: similar
chemistry (same group), the same functional group throughout, and the
−CH₂− step between neighbours. The third test is what makes a
**general formula** possible — a [[general-formula]] writes every
member of the family at once using n for the carbon count. For the
alkanes, a molecule with n carbons always carries 2n + 2 hydrogens,
so the general formula is CnH2n+2. Test it: methane, n = 1, gives
2 × 1 + 2 = 4 hydrogens (CH₄); propane, n = 3, gives 8 (C₃H₈); and
dodecane, n = 12, must carry 2 × 12 + 2 = 26 hydrogens, giving
C₁₂H₂₆ — no experiment needed, just the formula. Different families
have different general formulae: CnH2n for the alkenes, CnH2n+2O for
the alcohols. The first few members sit side by side in the table
below, and you should read each column top-to-bottom watching the
−CH₂− steps happen.

| Alkanes | Alkenes | Alcohols |
|---------|---------|----------|
| CH₄ | — | CH₄O |
| C₂H₆ | C₂H₄ | C₂H₆O |
| C₃H₈ | C₃H₆ | C₃H₈O |
| C₄H₁₀ | C₄H₈ | C₄H₁₀O |

(Read the alkene column's empty first cell as chemistry, not damage: a
double bond needs *two* carbon atoms, so no one-carbon alkene exists.
Every alkane and alcohol count is valid from one carbon upward.)

A [[functional-group]] is an atom or a group of atoms that **determines
the chemical properties** of a compound. All compounds in the same
homologous series carry the same functional group — the −OH group for
alcohols, the C=C double bond for alkenes. One way to think about it:
the functional group is what makes a particular compound behave
differently from an alkane of similar size. The hydrocarbon skeleton
is inert scenery; the group is where the reactions happen.

The last two formula types appear here and get their full treatment in
the next section. A **structural formula** shows how the atoms in a
molecule are joined together, and it comes in two forms: the
**displayed formula** (sometimes called the full structural formula),
which draws every bond as an individual line, and the **condensed
structural formula**, which writes out the chain without bond lines —
propane as CH₃CH₂CH₃. Each line in a displayed formula represents a
pair of shared electrons: a covalent bond, exactly as in Chapter 8.
Displayed formulae are always drawn with the molecule straightened out
and flattened; they do not show the real three-dimensional angles, but
they show exactly how every atom is joined. For butane, the drawn
right-angle appearance has nothing to do with the true shape — in
reality the bonds are arranged tetrahedrally at about 109.5°, the same
geometry that builds the diamond structure you met in u1:c08.

:::maths-skills
The general formula is an arithmetic machine. Given CnH2n+2: n = 1 →
CH₄ (2 × 1 + 2 = 4); n = 3 → C₃H₈ (2 × 3 + 2 = 8); n = 12 → C₁₂H₂₆
(2 × 12 + 2 = 26). The same substitution works for alkenes (CnH2n:
n = 2 → C₂H₄) and alcohols (CnH2n+2O: n = 2 → C₂H₆O). Exam questions
love the reverse direction — "dodecane has 12 carbons; write its
formula" — and the whole trick is trusting the algebra over the
temptation to count hydrogens one by one.
:::

:::exam-hint
The homologous-series definition earns marks in three clauses, so
quote all three: similar chemical properties, *because* they share
the same functional group, differing by −CH₂− between consecutive
members. A question asking "why do all alcohols react similarly?"
wants the functional-group clause, not a list of alcohols. And if you
are asked what makes alcohols different from alkanes, the answer is
the group — −OH — not a property.
:::

:::misconception
"A general formula is just a vague molecular formula." The two answer
different questions. A molecular formula describes *one* molecule —
C₃H₈ is propane, full stop. A general formula describes *every member
of a family at once* — CnH2n+2 is methane, ethane, propane and every
other alkane, with n as the free variable. Confusing them inverts the
whole family idea: the general formula is the pattern, the molecular
formula is one instantiation of it.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you state the three membership tests for a homologous series? Can
you use CnH2n+2 to write the formula for a 12-carbon alkane without
counting hydrogens individually? Can you name the functional group of
the alcohols and of the alkenes, and say in one sentence what a
functional group does? Can you explain why the alkene column of the
table starts at two carbons? Those four checks are the section — pass
them and the drawing section is pure technique.
:::
