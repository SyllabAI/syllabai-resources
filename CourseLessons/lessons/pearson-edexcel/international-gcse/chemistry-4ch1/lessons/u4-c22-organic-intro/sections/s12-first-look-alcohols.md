One more family gets a first look before this chapter closes — the
alcohols, because they will meet you again in the reactions section
and at full length in a later chapter. **Alcohols** are a
[[homologous-series]] of compounds which all contain an **−OH
[[functional-group]]** attached to a hydrocarbon chain. The group is
coded in the name by the ending **−ol**, the third name-ending in
your [[iupac-nomenclature]] decoder (after *ane* for all-single bonds and *ene* for a double
bond). Methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol — the four small
members line up with the chain-length code counting carbons exactly
as before: *meth*, *eth*, *prop*. Ethanol is what everyday speech
just calls "alcohol" — the compound in alcoholic drinks — but in
chemistry the word names the whole family.

Position matters the moment chains reach three carbons. There are
**two different forms of propanol**: the −OH group can sit on the end
carbon of the chain (**propan-1-ol**) or on the second carbon
(**propan-2-ol**). The number works exactly like the alkene position
code from s07 — it names which carbon carries the group. And the two
forms are genuine **structural isomers**: there is no way to bend or
twist one molecule into the other, because moving an −OH group from
carbon 1 to carbon 2 means breaking bonds. Same molecular formula
(C₃H₈O), different structural formula — the s09 definition, live.

Extension, one step beyond the −OH pattern: a structural isomer of
propanol exists that contains **no −OH group at all** — CH₃OCH₂CH₃,
in which the oxygen atom sits *between* two carbon atoms instead of
being joined to a hydrogen. This compound is named methoxyethane and
belongs to a different homologous series, the **ethers**. It is a
quiet preview of the functional-group idea's power: one oxygen atom,
two different positions, two different families with different
chemistries.

:::key-point
Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are structural isomers: the −OH group on
the end carbon versus the middle carbon. The position number in the
name does real structural work — it is the difference between two
compounds, not a stylistic variation, and the break-a-bond test
applies exactly as it did in s09.
:::

:::exam-hint
When you balance combustion equations for alcohols in the next
section, take care with the oxygen inside the molecule: the −OH
contributes an oxygen atom to the formula, and forgetting it is the
standard balancing error. Write the alcohol's formula first (ethanol
is C₂H₅OH, not C₂H₆), then balance — the extra O is inside the
reactant, and the equation arithmetic will expose it if you let it.
:::

:::misconception
"Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are the same compound — both are just
'propanol'." The name without the number is incomplete: the two
numbered forms place the −OH group on different carbons, which makes
them structural isomers with different structures. If an exam asks
you to draw "the two alcohols with formula C₃H₈O", the marking point
is the position of the group — end carbon versus middle carbon — and
a single unnumbered "propanol" answers neither.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define the alcohols as a homologous series — functional
group, attachment point, name ending? Can you draw both propanols and
say why they are structural isomers rather than drawings of one
compound? Can you describe what makes methoxyethane different from
propan-1-ol despite the same molecular formula? Then you are ready
for the reactions that close the chapter.
:::
