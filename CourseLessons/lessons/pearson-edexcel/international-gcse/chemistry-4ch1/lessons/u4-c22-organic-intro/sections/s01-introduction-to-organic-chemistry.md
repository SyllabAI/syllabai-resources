Chemistry has two great territories. Everything you studied in the first
three units — metals, salts, acids, the air itself — belongs to the
inorganic half. This chapter opens the other half: the chemistry of
carbon, where sugars, fats and proteins live alongside fuels, plastics,
medicines and dyes. One element builds all of it. Carbon atoms bond to
each other in chains, branched chains and rings, with hydrogen atoms
attached, and the sheer variety of those arrangements is why carbon
chemistry has its own name: **organic chemistry**. Caffeine from a
coffee bean, aspirin from a laboratory bench and the dyes that colour
clothing are all [[organic-compound]]s — carbon compounds with wildly
different sizes and shapes, built from the same bonding rules.

The name has a history worth knowing. *Organic* was originally used
because chemists believed these compounds could only come from living
things. That belief is long gone: organic compounds are made in
factories every day. The modern meaning is precise — an organic
compound is any carbon compound **except the very simplest ones**:
carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, the carbonates and the
hydrogencarbonates stay in the inorganic camp. Within organic
chemistry, the simplest family of all is the [[hydrocarbon]]: a
compound made up of hydrogen and carbon only, and it is where this
chapter's specification statements begin.

Expect a period of adjustment. Organic chemistry greets you with a
crowd of new compounds, unfamiliar names and new ways of drawing
structures — that first impression can be genuinely intimidating, and
this chapter exists to dismantle it. The whole route runs through six
core specification statements, assessed in **both** Paper 1C and Paper
2C, with no chemistry-only band anywhere in Unit 4: what a hydrocarbon
is; the five formula types for representing organic molecules; the
terms homologous series, functional group and isomerism; IUPAC naming
of compounds with up to six carbon atoms; writing possible structures
from a molecular formula; and classifying reactions as substitution,
addition or combustion. Each statement gets its own section or two,
and every skill reappears in later chapters — crude oil, alkanes,
alkenes, alcohols and beyond all run on the machinery you build here.

One practical habit carries more weight than any single fact: get
your hands on molecular models. A school lab will usually have a set;
otherwise modelling clay and matchsticks (or small nails) make
perfectly workable bonds. Physical models let you rotate, twist and
rebuild molecules until the two-dimensional drawings stop looking
arbitrary — and by the time you reach the isomerism sections you will
find the models have quietly done half the teaching. Spend time
exploring: names, structures, models. That is the honest route into
organic chemistry, and every later chapter assumes you took it.

:::cross-curricular
Organic chemistry is the chemistry of life and the chemistry of
industry at once. Proteins, sugars and fats — the molecules your body
runs on — are organic. So are the fuels in a camping stove, the
pharmaceuticals in a pharmacy, the dyes in clothing and the plastics
in every device you own. The naming and structure rules in this
chapter are the shared language of medicine, food science, materials
engineering and fuel chemistry; the six-carbon naming limit you will
practise is exactly the vocabulary those industries use daily.
:::

:::misconception
"Organic means natural, so organic compounds must come from living
things." That was the historical belief, and it is the trap. The modern
definition is structural, not biological: organic compounds are carbon
compounds apart from the very simplest (carbon dioxide, carbon
monoxide, carbonates, hydrogencarbonates). Fertiliser factories,
refineries and pharmaceutical plants synthesise organic compounds on
tonne and milligram scales alike. What excludes a compound from
organic chemistry is its formula — not where it was made.
:::
