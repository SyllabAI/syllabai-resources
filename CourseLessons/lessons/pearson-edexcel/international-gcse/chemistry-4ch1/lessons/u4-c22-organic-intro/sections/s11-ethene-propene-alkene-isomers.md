Apply the isomer machinery to the smallest alkenes and the pattern
snaps into focus. Ethene, CH₂=CH₂, has **no isomers** — with only two
carbons there is nowhere else for anything to go. Propene,
CH₃CH=CH₂, almost has one: you can move atoms around, but there is no
rearrangement of C₃H₆ that still contains a carbon-carbon double bond
in a chain. So propene has no structural isomer that is still an
alkene.

The [[molecular-formula]], though, keeps its options open. There is a
structural isomer of C₃H₆ that contains **no** carbon-carbon double
bond at all: three carbon atoms joined in a **ring**. It is called
cyclopropane, and the name is itself a decode — a ring of three
carbons with only single bonds between them. Each ring carbon bonds
to its two ring neighbours, using two of its four bonds, and to two
hydrogens for the rest: C₃H₆ exactly. Cyclopropane and propene are
genuine structural [[isomerism]] cases — same molecular formula, different
structural formulae (ring versus chain), and the break-a-bond test
from s09 confirms no rotation converts one into the other. (This ring
versus chain contrast is extension material: the examinable skill is
the isomer definition, and cyclopropane is its most surprising
demonstration.)

Move up to C₄H₈ and the double-bond set grows to **three** structural
isomers that each contain a carbon-carbon double bond: but-1-ene,
but-2-ene, and 2-methylpropene — a branched three-carbon chain with
the double bond in the middle. Notice the two independent dials you
are turning: the **position** of the double bond along the chain
(but-1-ene versus but-2-ene) and the **branching** of the chain
(2-methylpropene). Isomer enumeration at this level is just running
those dials systematically — fix the formula, walk through chain
lengths, branch positions and double-bond positions, and discard
rotations along the way.

:::exam-hint
"What isomers exist with molecular formula X?" is a specification 4.5
question, and the method is dial-walking: every alkene isomer must
contain C=C, so enumerate chain lengths, branch patterns and
double-bond positions, then strike out duplicates that differ only by
rotation. For C₃H₆ the examinable answer inside the alkene camp is
"propene only" — but knowing cyclopropane exists shows the examiner
you understand that the formula, not the functional group, defines
the isomer set.
:::

:::misconception
"A ring molecule can't be an isomer of a chain molecule — one has a
double bond and the other doesn't." The isomer test never mentions
functional groups: same molecular formula, different structural
formula, different compound. Propene (C₃H₆, chain, C=C) and
cyclopropane (C₃H₆, ring, all single bonds) pass the test exactly.
The formula is the identity card; where the bonds go is the
difference.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you explain why ethene has no isomers and propene has none within
the alkene family? Can you decode the name cyclopropane into a ring
structure and show it is C₃H₆? Can you name the three alkene isomers
of C₄H₈, saying which dial each one turns? With the isomer toolkit
complete, one more family gets a first look before the chapter closes
— the alcohols.
:::
