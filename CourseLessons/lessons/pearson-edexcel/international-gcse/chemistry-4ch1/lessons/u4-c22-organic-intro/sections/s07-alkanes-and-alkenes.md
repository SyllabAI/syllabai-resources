The two simplest [[homologous-series]] of hydrocarbons — and the two
endings that will meet you in every organic name — are the alkanes and
the alkenes. **Alkanes** are a homologous series of similar
[[hydrocarbon]]s (compounds of carbon and hydrogen only) in which
every carbon is joined to the next by **single covalent bonds**. The
ending *ane* codes that all-single-bond structure: ethane is a
two-carbon chain (*eth*) with carbon-carbon single bonds, CH₃CH₃ —
the s02 saturated camp, family-sized.

**Alkenes** are a homologous series of hydrocarbons that contain a
**carbon-carbon double bond**, coded in the name by the ending *ene*.
Ethene is a two-carbon chain containing C=C, CH₂=CH₂ — the
unsaturated camp. With longer chains a new question appears: the
double bond could sit anywhere along the chain, and *ene* alone no
longer pins the structure down. The fix is a position number. Number
the chain and state which carbon atom the double bond **starts** at,
written between hyphens in the name:

| Formula | Name | Description |
|---------|------|-------------|
| CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ | but-1-ene | a four-carbon chain with the double bond starting on the first carbon |
| CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | but-2-ene | a four-carbon chain with the double bond starting on the second carbon |

Both molecules are C₄H₈ — the code number, not the formula, is what
distinguishes them. But-1-ene and but-2-ene are genuinely different
compounds: converting one into the other would mean breaking bonds
and rebuilding the chain, not bending it.

One rule fixes the numbering direction: **number from the end that
produces the smaller numbers in the name**. Counting from the left of
CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ puts the double bond at carbon 1; counting from the
right would put it at carbon 3 — and 1 beats 3, so the name is
but-1-ene, not but-3-ene. The same smaller-number principle returns
in the next section for branch positions, so install it once here
and reuse it everywhere.

:::exam-hint
Two names with the same molecular formula are a classic contrast
question: but-1-ene and but-2-ene share C₄H₈ but differ in double-bond
position, and the mark sits on saying the position — first carbon
versus second carbon — precisely. When you write position-coded names
yourself, show the hyphens (but-1-ene, never but1ene): the number is
a separate code element, and its placement carries the information.
:::

:::misconception
"But-1-ene and but-2-ene are the same molecule drawn from different
ends." Flipping a molecule is rotation (s06) — but moving the double
bond is reconstruction. The two alkenes differ in which carbons share
the double bond; no rotation converts one into the other, so they are
distinct compounds. The decoder that separates them is the number in
the name: *-1-* versus *-2-*. Position codes carry structural truth;
orientation carries none.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define alkane and alkene in one sentence each — series
membership, bond type, and name ending? Can you write ethane and
ethene in condensed form and point at the bond difference? Given
CH₃CH=CHCH₃, can you name it with the correct position number and
justify the numbering direction with the smaller-numbers rule? Those
are the family basics; the next section adds branches to the chain.
:::
