Start with the smallest possible organic molecule family. A
[[hydrocarbon]] is a compound that contains carbon and hydrogen
**only** — no oxygen, no halogens, nothing else. The word *only* is
doing real work in that definition, and examiners know it. Virtually
every organic compound contains carbon and hydrogen; what makes a
hydrocarbon special is that carbon and hydrogen are all it contains.
Methane, propane, butane, ethene — the fuels that dominate later
chapters are all hydrocarbons, ranging from single carbon atoms up to
long chains and ring structures, drawn in every arrangement carbon's
four bonds allow.

The carbon atoms in a hydrocarbon chain are joined by single, double
or triple covalent bonds, while every carbon-to-hydrogen bond is a
single bond. That distinction between the carbon-carbon bond types is
not decoration — it splits the whole hydrocarbon family into two
camps. A [[saturated]] hydrocarbon contains only single carbon-carbon
bonds: every carbon uses all four of its bonds on separate partners,
so no more atoms can be added. An [[unsaturated]] hydrocarbon contains
at least one double or triple carbon-carbon bond, and those spare
bonding capacity points are exactly where later reactions happen.
The alkane family (Chapter 24, u4:c24) and the alkene family
(Chapter 25, u4:c25) give each camp its full treatment; here you only
need the vocabulary and the bond-counting logic behind it.

Keep the definition in its examinable shape. If a question shows you a
molecule's structure and asks whether it is a hydrocarbon, the check
is a two-element audit: carbon present, hydrogen present, and
**nothing else**. A molecule like ethanol — which contains an oxygen
atom in its −OH group — fails that audit no matter how carbon-rich it
looks. That audit is also your first classification tool: the next
sections build the formula vocabulary that lets you record what a
molecule contains, and the camp it belongs to.

:::key-point
Organic compounds can be described as **saturated** (containing only
single C–C bonds) or **unsaturated** (containing double or triple C–C
bonds). The two words describe bonding capacity, not reactivity in
general — and the alkane and alkene chapters (u4:c24, u4:c25) develop
exactly how each camp behaves.
:::

:::exam-hint
The definition has a habit of being tested with a near-miss. Carbon
dioxide contains carbon — but no hydrogen, so it is not a hydrocarbon.
Ethanol contains both carbon and hydrogen — but also oxygen, so it is
not a hydrocarbon either. When the specification says *a compound of
hydrogen and carbon only*, the *only* is the mark. Write the full
definition, never "a compound containing hydrogen and carbon", and the
near-misses cannot touch you.
:::

:::misconception
"Organic compound and hydrocarbon mean the same thing." They do not —
hydrocarbon is the strict subset. Every hydrocarbon is an organic
compound, but the reverse fails the moment any third element appears:
alcohols, carboxylic acids and esters all contain oxygen and all sit
outside the hydrocarbon family. If a question asks whether a molecule
is a hydrocarbon, run the two-element audit — carbon, hydrogen,
nothing else — and answer from the audit, not from vibes.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define a hydrocarbon with the word *only* in place, and
explain why ethanol fails the definition while propane passes it? Can
you say what bond type separates saturated from unsaturated
hydrocarbons, and name the two families (met later) that embody each
camp? If the audit habit — carbon, hydrogen, nothing else — feels
automatic, you are ready for the formula section.
:::
