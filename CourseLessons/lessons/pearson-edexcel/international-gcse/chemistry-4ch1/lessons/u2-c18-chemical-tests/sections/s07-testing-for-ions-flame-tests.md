Every salt contains at least one cation and one anion, and the two ion classes
are tested separately. The [[flame-test]] is the first of the cation tests: it
shows the presence of certain metal ions in a compound, because those ions
colour a flame in ways the naked eye can tell apart.

The method has four moves. First, a **platinum or nichrome wire** is dipped
into **concentrated hydrochloric acid**. Second, the wire is dipped into the
salt you want to test, so that some salt sticks to the end. Third, the wire
and its salt are held just within a [[non-luminous-flame]] — the
non-luminous (roaring) Bunsen burner flame. Fourth, the colour is observed and matched to the ion it proves.

The concentrated hydrochloric acid is not decoration — it cleans the wire.
A new wire, or one reused from an earlier test, is cleaned by dipping it into
the concentrated acid and then holding it in the non-luminous flame, repeated
until the wire gives no colour to the flame at all. Only a colour-clean wire
gives a result you can trust: any leftover sodium from a previous test, for
example, would paint its own yellow over the truth. The acid converts surface
contamination into volatile chlorides that the flame strips away, which is
why the cleaning dip and the test dip use the same reagent.

:::key-point
If a new piece of wire is not being used, it is usually cleaned by dipping it
into concentrated hydrochloric acid and then holding it in a non-luminous
Bunsen flame. This is repeated until the wire does not give any colour to the
flame.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection and take great care with the concentrated acid. Do not
get it on your skin, and watch out for "spitting" when heating it on the wire
— hold the wire at arm's length in the flame.
:::

:::exam-hint
Method questions on this test are scored on the *sequence*, so rehearse it as
four numbered moves: acid, salt, non-luminous flame, colour. The two details
examiners most want are the **concentrated hydrochloric acid** (name it fully
— "acid" alone is loose) and the **non-luminous** flame (the blue roaring
flame, not the yellow safety flame — the yellow flame's own colour would
drown the result). A common follow-up asks *why* the wire is cleaned first:
to remove ions from earlier tests that would give their own flame colours.
That "why" is one mark that costs one sentence.
:::

:::misconception
"You dip the wire in the acid to make the salt stick better." The acid dip
has two jobs and adhesion is the lesser one. Its primary job is cleaning:
concentrated hydrochloric acid converts contaminating ions on the wire into
volatile chlorides that burn away in the flame, leaving no colour of their
own. Without the clean, a wire that once touched sodium chloride would report
yellow for every sample afterwards — the previous test contaminating the next
one.
:::
