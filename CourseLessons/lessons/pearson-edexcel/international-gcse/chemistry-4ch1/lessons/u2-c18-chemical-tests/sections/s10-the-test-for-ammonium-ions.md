Sodium hydroxide solution reacts with **ammonium** salts — either solid or in
solution — to produce **ammonia gas**. This is the fourth cation test of
objective 2.47, and it breaks the pattern of the other three: there is no
precipitate to look for, because ammonium hydroxide does not form a solid.
The evidence is a gas with a smell and an alkaline reaction.

In the cold, just enough ammonia is produced for the test to work by smell —
the sharp, familiar odour of the reagent bottle. If you **warm** the mixture,
ammonia comes off steadily and can be tested properly with a piece of **damp
red litmus paper or universal indicator paper**: ammonia is alkaline and turns
the paper **blue** — the gas test from earlier in this chapter, reused as the
confirmation step here.

The ionic equation is:

NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)

A full equation for adding sodium hydroxide solution to solid ammonium
chloride is:

NH₄Cl(s) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)

This test works on a sample of a solid or a solution, because you are not
looking for a precipitate — the solid reacts just as well as its solution.

:::key-point
Recognising gases by smelling them has to be done with great care. In this
case there is usually so little ammonia gas present in the cold that it is
safe to smell if you take the normal precautions. You should not, however,
make any attempt to smell the mixture when it is warm.
:::

:::safety
Do not attempt to smell the ammonia gas if you have a breathing problem such
as asthma. Waft gently towards the nose if a smell is required at all, and
carry out the warming step in a fume cupboard or well-ventilated space.
:::

The reactions of the four cations with sodium hydroxide solution compress
into one summary table:

| Ion | Result of adding NaOH(aq) | Name of precipitate formed | Formula of precipitate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cu²⁺ | blue precipitate | copper(II) hydroxide | Cu(OH)₂ |
| Fe²⁺ | green precipitate | iron(II) hydroxide | Fe(OH)₂ |
| Fe³⁺ | orange-brown precipitate | iron(III) hydroxide | Fe(OH)₃ |
| NH₄⁺ | ammonia gas produced (damp red litmus turns blue) | no precipitate | — |

:::exam-hint
The ammonium test is the only 2.47 test whose evidence is a gas, and the
mark scheme wants the whole chain: add sodium hydroxide and warm; ammonia gas
evolved; identified by damp red litmus turning blue. Expect the ionic equation
(NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O) as a one-mark rider — note that the ammonium ion
*is* the acid-side partner here, handing a proton to the hydroxide base. The
summary table above is the single most examinable artefact of the cation
section: cover the right-hand columns and reconstruct all four rows until you
can do it cold.
:::

:::misconception
"The ammonium test works like the copper test — add sodium hydroxide and
watch for a coloured solid." There is no coloured solid: [[ammonium-ion]]
hydroxide does not precipitate, it decomposes to ammonia and water. That is
also why this test uniquely accepts a solid sample — with the metal hydroxide
tests, a solid sample would hide its precipitate; here the ammonia gas escapes
whether the sample is solid or dissolved. Write "no precipitate; ammonia
evolved" in that order and the mark scheme finds you.
:::
