The halide test closes the anion family: chloride, bromide and iodide ions are
caught with silver nitrate, and the **colour of the silver halide precipitate**
says which one. The [[halide-ion]]s — the −1 ions formed by the halogens of
Group 7 — are collectively named by this test's logic: one reagent, three
colours, three conclusions.

Make a solution of your suspected chloride, bromide or iodide and add enough
dilute nitric acid to make it [[acidified]]. Then add some **silver nitrate
solution**.

A **white** precipitate (of silver chloride) shows the presence of **chloride
ions**:

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

A **cream** precipitate (of silver bromide) shows the presence of **bromide
ions**:

Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s)

A **yellow** precipitate (of silver iodide) shows the presence of **iodide
ions**:

Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s)

The nitric acid is doing the same bodyguard work as in the sulfate test: it
reacts with and removes other anions that would also form precipitates with
silver nitrate — carbonate and hydroxide ions are the named offenders. But the
acid choice is even tighter here. You must **not** use hydrochloric acid (nor
hydrobromic nor hydroiodic acid), because you would be adding halide ions of
your own and a precipitate is then certain — the test would report the
reagent. Nitric acid contributes only nitrate ions, whose silver salt stays
dissolved.

:::key-point
All of these silver halide precipitates tend to discolour to greys and pale
purples on exposure to light.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection for all these tests and avoid skin contact with the
reactants and products. Silver nitrate stains skin black on contact — handle
the solution, not the drops.
:::

:::exam-hint
The three colours in specification language: chloride **white**, bromide
**cream**, iodide **yellow**. "Off-white" for chloride loses to "white"; the
white-cream-yellow sequence darkens down the group, which is a memory hook
the examiner rewards when you volunteer it. The equation set is three ionic
equations differing only in the halide — learn one and you know all three.
Two follow-ups recur: *why nitric acid?* (removes carbonate/hydroxide
interference without adding halide ions) and *why not hydrochloric acid?*
(its chloride ions would precipitate silver chloride regardless of the
sample). Both are one-sentence answers worth preparing verbatim.
:::

:::misconception
"Any dilute acid will do for the acidified step — acid is acid." In the
halide test the acid's own anion joins the reaction mixture, so the wrong acid
fakes the result: hydrochloric acid adds chloride ions, hydrobromic adds
bromide, hydroiodic adds iodide — each certain to precipitate with silver
nitrate. Compare the sulfate test, where the forbidden acid was sulfuric for
the same structural reason (its anion was the target ion). The rule generalises:
never acidify with an acid whose anion is the ion you are testing for.
:::
