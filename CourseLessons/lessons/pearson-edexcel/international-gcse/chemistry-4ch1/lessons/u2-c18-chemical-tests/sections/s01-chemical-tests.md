A colourless gas pops when you hold a flame to it. Another colourless gas turns
cloudy water milky. A clear solution turns a dull green solid a brilliant blue.
None of these substances can be identified by looking at them — but each one
answers a [[chemical-test]], a procedure whose observable result has exactly one
cause. This chapter is the analytical toolkit of International GCSE chemistry:
five gas tests, the flame test and its five colours, the sodium hydroxide tests
for metal [[cation]]s and the ammonium ion, the silver nitrate, barium chloride
and acid tests for three families of [[anion]]s, and the two tests for water —
one chemical, one physical. Every test follows the same logic: add the reagent,
watch for the positive result, and name the ion or gas it proves.

Seven objectives organise the chapter, and every one of them is examined in
both papers. The gas tests (2.44) are pure observation questions. The flame
test (2.45) and its colour table (2.46) are method-and-recall questions. The
cation tests (2.47) and anion tests (2.48) are the descriptive essay questions
of this chapter — full marks live in the sequence of additions and the colour
of each result. The two water tests (2.49 and 2.50) close the chapter by
distinguishing *presence* of water from *purity* of water, a distinction the
examiner likes to probe. Learn each test as a three-line script — reagent,
result, conclusion — and the whole chapter compresses into one page you can
reproduce under exam conditions.

:::prior-knowledge
Four earlier chapters load this one. From Chapter 12 (u2:c12) you need the
halogens and their coloured silver salts in embryo; the halide tests here are
the payoff of Group 7 chemistry. From Chapter 11 (u2:c11) you need the alkaline
metals — lithium, sodium and potassium — whose flame colours and hydroxide
solubility decide two of the tests. From Chapter 17 (u2:c17) you need the
solubility rules, because every positive result in the cation and anion tests
is an insoluble salt precipitating out of solution; the term precipitate was
defined there. From Chapter 5 (u1:c05) you need balanced equations
with state symbols — (s), (l), (g), (aq) — because writing the ionic equation
for a precipitate is a standard follow-up mark here.
:::

:::exam-hint
All seven objectives are assessed in both Paper 1C and Paper 2C — nothing in
this chapter is chemistry-only. The recurring question shapes are: name the
gas from a described observation ("a green gas that bleaches damp litmus");
describe a test in full, including the reagent, the observation and the
positive result; interpret a table of test results to identify an unknown
compound; and write the ionic equation for a precipitate. Watch the command
words: *describe* wants the procedure in order, *state* wants the single
observation, *explain* wants the chemistry behind the result — for example why
the acid must be added *before* the barium chloride in the sulfate test.
:::

:::misconception
"'Chemical tests' are separate facts to memorise, unrelated to the reactions
of earlier chapters." Every test in this chapter is a reaction you already
know, pointed at a question. The limewater test for carbon dioxide *is* the
calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide reaction; the cation tests *are* the
insoluble-hydroxide entries of the solubility rules; the halide tests *are*
precipitation reactions between silver ions and Group 7 ions. Learn the
reaction and the test is the reaction plus one observation.
:::
