A clean [[flame-test]] reports its cation by colour. Five cations are
examined, and the five colours are pure recall — the specification words are
the mark scheme words:

| Cation | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Li⁺ | red |
| Na⁺ | yellow |
| K⁺ | lilac |
| Ca²⁺ | orange-red |
| Cu²⁺ | blue-green |

Two colour pairs need guarding. **Red versus orange-red**: lithium burns red,
calcium burns orange-red — the "orange" half of calcium's description is what
separates it from lithium's clean red. **Yellow versus everything yellow-ish**:
sodium's yellow is famously intrusive, and that is precisely why the wire
cleaning of the previous section matters — a contaminated wire reports sodium
for every sample it meets.

The colours come from the electrons of the metal ions: the flame's energy
promotes electrons to higher levels, and the light they emit falling back has
wavelengths — colours — fixed by the ion's identity. That is why the test
identifies the *ion*: the same element carries the same electron
arrangement whatever compound it sits in.

:::key-point
Lithium is not the only ion to give a red flame colour. Strontium gives a very
similar colour. The only way to be sure is to compare the flame colour
alongside that of a known compound of lithium or strontium.
:::

:::exam-hint
The colour for sodium is described as yellow in the specification, but you
will also see it described as orange — both are usually accepted. For every
other colour, stay with the specification's exact word: red, lilac,
orange-red, blue-green. "Purple" for potassium loses the mark that "lilac"
earns. Expect two question shapes: a table to complete (ion to colour or
colour to ion) and a describe-the-test question that wants the method *plus*
the colour word *plus* the conclusion. Drill the five pairs until the table
rebuilds itself on paper in under thirty seconds.
:::

:::misconception
"The flame colour tells you the compound, so lithium chloride and lithium
sulfate give different colours." The colour belongs to the **cation**, not the
compound: every lithium salt burns red, every copper(II) salt burns
blue-green, because the emitting electrons belong to the metal ion. That is
what makes the test a cation test. The anion is invisible to the flame —
which is exactly why the anion tests later in this chapter exist.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection for all these tests and avoid skin contact with the
reactants and products. Carry out flame tests in a well-ventilated space and
never re-heat a wire whose acid drop has not burned off.
:::
