The flame test covers five cations; sodium hydroxide solution covers the
rest. The idea rests on one solubility fact: of the common hydroxides, only
those of sodium, potassium and ammonium dissolve in water to any extent. Most
metal hydroxides are [[insoluble]]. So when you add sodium hydroxide solution
to a solution containing metal ions, the metal's hydroxide forms and drops out
of solution as a solid — a [[precipitate]] — and the **colour** of that
precipitate names the metal.

To carry out the test, dissolve the salt in distilled (pure) water and put
about 1 cm³ in a test-tube. Add about the same volume of dilute sodium
hydroxide solution. What you are looking for in most of these tests is the
formation of a precipitate; the solution goes cloudy as the solid forms.

**A blue precipitate** shows the presence of **copper(II) ions**. The
precipitate is copper(II) hydroxide. Any copper(II) salt in solution reacts
this way — with copper(II) sulfate solution, the full equation is:

CuSO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq)

**An orange-brown precipitate** shows the presence of **iron(III) ions
(Fe³⁺)**. The precipitate is iron(III) hydroxide. Any iron(III) compound in
solution gives this precipitate — for example:

FeCl₃(aq) + 3NaOH(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s) + 3NaCl(aq)

**A green precipitate** shows the presence of **iron(II) ions (Fe²⁺)**. The
precipitate is iron(II) hydroxide — say, from iron(II) sulfate solution:

FeSO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq)

Notice how much more complicated the full equations are than the story they
tell — they also hide what is happening. The [[ionic-equation]] shows only the
things that actually react, and for precipitation reactions it is the better
tool:

Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)  ·  Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)

:::key-point
The green precipitate darkens on standing and turns orange-brown around the
top of the tube. This is because the iron(II) hydroxide is oxidised to
iron(III) hydroxide by the air.
:::

:::key-point
You must carry out these three tests on a **solution** of the salt you are
testing. If you tested the solid you would not be able to see whether a
precipitate had formed, because there is already solid present.
:::

:::exam-hint
The three coloured precipitates are the heart of 2.47: blue = Cu²⁺, green =
Fe²⁺, orange-brown = Fe³⁺. Two follow-up marks recur. First, the *why-a-
solution* point: the test needs a solution because a solid sample would hide
its own precipitate — ammonium is the exception examined next section, where
the evidence is a gas, not a colour. Second, the ionic equation: for any
named metal hydroxide precipitate, write the metal ion and hydroxide ions as
aqueous and the hydroxide as solid, balanced — Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂,
Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂, Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃. Learn the three charge-to-
hydroxide ratios (1:2, 2:2, 3:3) and the balancing is automatic.
:::

:::misconception
"A green precipitate could be iron(II) or iron(III) — they sound similar." The
colours are different and the difference is the whole test: iron(II) hydroxide
is **green**; iron(III) hydroxide is **orange-brown**. There is a bridge
between them, though — the green precipitate darkens on standing and turns
orange-brown from the top of the tube downwards, because air oxidises Fe²⁺ to
Fe³⁺. If the question shows that colour change over time, it is telling you
the sample was iron(II), slowly rusting in the test-tube.
:::
