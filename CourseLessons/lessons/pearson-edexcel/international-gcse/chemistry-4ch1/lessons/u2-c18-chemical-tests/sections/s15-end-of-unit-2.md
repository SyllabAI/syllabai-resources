This section closes the chapter — and with it, Unit 2. Everything the chapter
taught now compresses into seven abilities, one per objective, and the unit's
question bank above (page anchor below the fold) is where each ability gets
its drills. The chapter's own questions do the same job at chapter scale: gas
identifications from single observations, full method descriptions for named
tests, and an unknown-compound interpretation that chains a precipitate
colour with an anion result — the exact shape the papers use.

:::summary
A [[chemical-test]] is one reagent, one observation, one conclusion. The five
gas tests: hydrogen — lighted splint, squeaky pop (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O); oxygen —
glowing splint, relights; carbon dioxide — limewater turns milky
(Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O, dissolving again to soluble calcium
hydrogencarbonate if bubbling continues); ammonia — damp red litmus turns
blue, the only alkaline gas; chlorine — damp blue litmus goes red then white
(acidic, then bleached). The [[flame-test]] cleans a platinum/nichrome wire
with concentrated hydrochloric acid, holds it in a non-luminous flame, and
reads five colours: Li⁺ red, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺
blue-green. Sodium hydroxide solution precipitates the metal hydroxides as coloured [[precipitate]]s —
Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green (darkening to orange-brown on standing as air oxidises
it), Fe³⁺ orange-brown — and releases ammonia from ammonium salts
(NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O), confirmed on damp red litmus. The anion scripts:
carbonate — dilute hydrochloric acid fizzes, gas turns limewater milky
(CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O); sulfate — dilute hydrochloric acid then barium
chloride, white BaSO₄ (never sulfuric acid, nitric/barium nitrate as an
alternative pair); halides — acidify with nitric acid then silver nitrate,
AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow. Water: white anhydrous copper(II)
sulfate turns blue (presence); freezing at exactly 0 °C or boiling at exactly
100 °C proves purity — impure water freezes lower and boils higher.
:::

:::can-you
Keyed to the seven objectives, in order: give all five gas tests as
reagent-observation-conclusion scripts (2.44); describe the flame test method
in four moves with the cleaning rationale (2.45); reproduce the five-colour
table ion-to-colour and colour-to-ion (2.46); run all four sodium hydroxide
tests — three precipitate colours with ionic equations and the ammonium gas
test with its equation (2.47); execute all three anion scripts with their
acid-first rationales and the five-row summary table (2.48); state the
copper(II) sulfate water test and explain why it shows presence, not purity
(2.49); and give the physical purity test with both fixed points and both
drift directions (2.50).
:::

:::exam-hint
Every objective in this chapter is examined in both papers — no chemistry-only
content remains in Unit 2 after this chapter. The highest-yield revision
artefacts, in order: the cation summary table (four rows), the anion summary
table (five rows), and the five-colour flame table. The question types to
drill: single-observation gas identification; full method description with
state-symbol equations; and the multi-result unknown-compound interpretation,
where the marks go to row-by-row elimination. When describing any method,
number the steps — sequence marks are awarded in order and a scrambled
description strands them.
:::

:::misconception
"Each test is an isolated fact, so I revise them as twenty separate cards."
The tests interlock, and the interlocks are the exam questions. The ammonia
gas test *is* the confirmation step of the ammonium-ion test; the limewater
test *is* the confirmation step of the carbonate test; the acid-first habit
in the sulfate and halide scripts is one principle applied twice (destroy the
interfering anions before the specific reagent arrives); and the purity trap
binds the two water tests into one mark-scheme pair. Revise the tests as one
network with shared nodes and the unknown-compound questions become reading
comprehension instead of recall.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The chapter's opening idea — that routine analysis now belongs to machines —
describes real laboratories. Instrumental methods (flame photometry and its
descendants) do the flame test's job with detectors that read emission lines
the eye cannot separate, and they measure *how much* as well as *which*.
Yet the classical tests here remain the first line of field chemistry:
water-quality kits still carry the precipitation reagents, environmental
screening still begins with the colour of a precipitate, and the chlorine
bleach reaction you scripted is the same chemistry that makes chlorine the
workhorse of water treatment. Fast, cheap, interpretable by eye — the
twenty-first-century lab carries both toolkits and knows when each is the
right one.
:::
