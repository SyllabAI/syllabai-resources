Water has two tests, and they answer two different questions. The first asks
*is water present?*; the second asks *is this sample pure water?* Mixing them
up is the single most-tested trap in this section.

**The chemical test for the presence of water (2.49):** add the sample to
white [[anhydrous]] copper(II) sulfate. Water turns it **blue**. Anhydrous
copper(II) sulfate lacks its [[water-of-crystallisation]] and is white;
dropping water onto it replaces the water of crystallisation and the solid
turns blue as the [[hydrated]] compound CuSO₄·5H₂O forms. The colour flip is
fast, cheap and unmistakable — white to blue means water.

The same test, however, works for anything that contains water. Sodium
chloride solution turns it blue; dilute sulfuric acid turns it blue; damp
sand turns it blue. A positive result proves the presence of water — it does
**not** show that the water is pure, because the test never looks at what
*else* is dissolved in the sample.

**The physical test for purity (2.50):** measure the **freezing (melting)
point or the boiling point**. Pure water freezes at exactly 0 °C and boils at
exactly 100 °C at 1 atmosphere pressure. If the water is impure, it will
usually freeze at a **lower** temperature and boil at a **higher** temperature
— the dissolved substances spread the phase changes away from the fixed
points. This is a physical test: nothing is identified chemically, the
temperatures themselves are the evidence.

:::safety
Avoid skin contact with the copper salts — anhydrous copper(II) sulfate is
irritant as well as an indicator of water. Wear eye protection throughout.
:::

:::exam-hint
The examiner pairs these tests precisely to punish the impure/pure confusion.
A typical question: "A liquid turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue. Does
this prove the liquid is pure water? Explain." The full-mark answer is no —
the blue flip shows water is present, and impure water would turn it blue just
as happily; purity needs the melting/boiling-point measurement. Quote both
fixed numbers (0 °C and 100 °C at 1 atmosphere) and both drift directions
(impure water freezes lower, boils higher). If you also explain *why* —
dissolved substances lower the freezing point and raise the boiling point —
you have the whole mark scheme in four sentences.
:::

:::misconception
"The copper(II) sulfate test proves water is pure because it gives such a
clear result." Clarity of result is not purity of conclusion. The white-to-blue
flip is a test for the **presence** of water molecules, full stop — it fires
identically for seawater, acid solution and rainwater. Only the physical
measurement (exact freezing or boiling point) speaks about purity. The two
objectives are numbered separately in the specification for exactly this
reason.
:::
