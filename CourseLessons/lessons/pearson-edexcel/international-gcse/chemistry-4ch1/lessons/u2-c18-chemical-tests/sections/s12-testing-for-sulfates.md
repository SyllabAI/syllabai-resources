Make a solution of your suspected [[sulfate]] in distilled (pure) water, add
some **dilute hydrochloric acid**, and then add some **barium chloride
solution**. A sulfate produces a **white precipitate** of barium sulfate:

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

The ionic equation is one line, but the *order* of the additions is the
examined thinking. The acid goes in first to react with and remove other
anions that might also produce white precipitates with the barium chloride.
The classic interferer is the carbonate: barium carbonate is white and
insoluble, so without the acid step, carbonate ions in the sample would fake
a positive sulfate result. The acid reacts with the carbonate ions and
removes them — the fizz of carbon dioxide escaping is the interference being
destroyed — and only then does the barium chloride report on sulfate alone.
The acid must be added **before** the barium chloride solution; reversed, the
false precipitate has already formed.

One acid is forbidden in this test. You must never acidify the solution with
**sulfuric acid**, because sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions — add those and
a barium sulfate precipitate is guaranteed regardless of the sample. The test
would be reporting on the reagent, not the unknown.

:::key-point
You could also use nitric acid and barium nitrate solution in the same way.
:::

:::exam-hint
The script: dilute hydrochloric acid first, then barium chloride solution;
white precipitate; sulfate present. The two "why" follow-ups are where the
marks live: *why acid first?* — to remove carbonate (and other interfering)
ions that would give a white barium precipitate of their own; *why not
sulfuric acid?* — it would add sulfate ions and force a positive result.
Both answers are one sentence each and both have appeared in real papers.
Name the white precipitate (barium sulfate) and quote its ionic equation with
state symbols; the nitric acid + barium nitrate variant earns credit when
offered as an alternative pair.
:::

:::misconception
"The acid step is just to help the salt dissolve — any acid will do." The acid
is not a solvent aid; it is a *bodyguard* that destroys interfering anions
before the barium ion arrives. And the acid choice is not free: sulfuric acid
would introduce the very ion being tested for, guaranteeing a white
precipitate even in a sulfate-free sample. Hydrochloric acid (chloride salts
of barium stay soluble) or nitric acid (nitrate salts all soluble) are safe;
sulfuric acid never is.
:::
