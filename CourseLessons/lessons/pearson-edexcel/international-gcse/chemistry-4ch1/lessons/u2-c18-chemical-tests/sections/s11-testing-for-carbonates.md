The anion tests begin with the easiest: add dilute hydrochloric acid to your
sample and look for **fizzing/bubbling**. A [[carbonate]] releases carbon
dioxide gas when it meets an acid, and the identity of the gas is confirmed by
bubbling it through limewater, which turns milky — the gas test from earlier
in this chapter closing the loop on the anion side.

Most carbonates are insoluble in water, so you will usually be doing this test
on a sample of a **solid**. The soluble carbonates — sodium carbonate,
potassium carbonate and ammonium carbonate — are the exception, and the test
works equally well on solutions of these. Either way the reaction is the same:
carbonate plus acid gives salt plus water plus carbon dioxide. For example,
with zinc carbonate and dilute nitric acid:

ZnCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

The ionic equation shows any carbonate reacting with any acid:

CO₃²⁻(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

That generality is the point of the test: every carbonate, solid or dissolved,
does the same thing to an acid, so one fizz-and-confirm script covers them all.

:::exam-hint
The full script is: add dilute hydrochloric acid; fizzing/bubbling observed;
bubble the gas through limewater; limewater turns milky; therefore a carbonate
is present. Both halves matter — fizzing alone is not proof (other chemistry
can make bubbles), and the limewater confirmation is the mark that converts
"gas evolved" into "carbon dioxide identified". The ionic equation
CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O is the extension-level mark: quote it when asked how
*all* carbonates behave. And mind the state symbols — the carbonate is solid
here, the hydrogen ions aqueous.
:::

:::misconception
"Fizzing with an acid proves carbonate, full stop." Fizzing proves a *gas* is
evolved; the limewater step proves it is *carbon dioxide*. The two-step
structure is not bureaucracy — metals above hydrogen also fizz with acids
(hydrogen, which pops, not milks). If a question hands you an unknown white
powder that fizzes with acid, the carbonate conclusion is only earned when the
gas turns limewater milky. Quote both observations and the conclusion writes
itself.
:::

:::safety
Wear eye protection for all these tests and avoid skin contact with the
reactants and products. Point the mouth of the tube away from you when adding
acid to a solid.
:::
