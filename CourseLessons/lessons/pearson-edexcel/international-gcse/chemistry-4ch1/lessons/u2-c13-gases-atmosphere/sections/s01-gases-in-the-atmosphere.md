The atmosphere is the blanket of gas held around the Earth by gravity, and
you are breathing a sample of it right now. That sample is a **mixture**:
several different gases jumbled together, each keeping its own properties,
none of them chemically joined to the others. This chapter takes that
mixture apart. You will learn exactly what dry air is made of and in what
proportions, then meet the experiments that measure one particular
component — oxygen — directly, and finally follow the two gases that
matter most for chemical reactions and for the climate: **oxygen** and
**carbon dioxide**.

The chapter runs on one measuring idea, stated here so everything
afterwards has a home: a gas's share of a mixture is quoted as a
**[[percentage-by-volume]]** — of every 100 cm³ of [[dry-air]], about
78 cm³ is nitrogen and about 21 cm³ is oxygen. Three different
experiments (heated copper, rusting iron, burning phosphorus) all attack
the same question from different angles: remove the oxygen from a known
volume of air, watch the volume shrink, and do one subtraction and one
division. Whichever reagent you use, the arithmetic and the error
analysis are the same shape — and that shape is where the exam marks
live.

From measurement the chapter moves to reaction. **[[combustion]]** —
burning elements in oxygen — produces a family of compounds called
**oxides**, and comparing the oxides of metals with the oxides of
non-metals reveals a clean, examinable split in their properties. The
same chemistry explains where atmospheric carbon dioxide comes from, and
why that particular gas, despite being only about 0.04% of the air,
punches far above its concentration: it is a **[[greenhouse-gas]]**, and
the final sections of this chapter make you explain — at the level of
molecular vibrations — why carbon dioxide absorbs infrared radiation
while nitrogen and oxygen, 99% of the air between them, do not.

Every objective in this chapter is **core content: it can be examined in
both Paper 1C and Paper 2C**. By the end you will be able to recall the
composition of dry air; describe and calculate the oxygen-percentage
experiments using metals or non-metals (the listed practical); describe
the combustion of magnesium, hydrogen and sulfur in oxygen, with
equations; describe carbon dioxide forming from heated metal carbonates,
including copper(II) carbonate; and explain the greenhouse effect in
infrared terms.

:::prior-knowledge
Three earlier chapters load this one. From Chapter 1 (u1:c01) you need
the particle model — gases fill their container, particles are far apart,
and a gas sample's volume is really the volume of its container. From
Chapter 5 (u1:c05) you need word and balanced equations with state
symbols, because every reaction in this chapter is written out in full.
From Chapters 7 and 8 (u1:c07, u1:c08) you need the ionic/covalent split:
metal oxides are ionic compounds, non-metal oxides are covalent molecules,
and that distinction drives the property split later in the chapter.
Chapter 14 (u2:c14) develops rusting properly — here you *use* rusting
as a measuring tool — and Chapter 18 (u2:c18) owns the gas tests,
including the hydrogen "squeaky pop" this chapter mentions.
:::

:::exam-hint
Nothing in this chapter is chemistry-only, so every fact and every
explanation here can appear on Paper 1C as well as Paper 2C. The
oxygen-percentage practical is one of the most reliably examined
practicals in the whole specification: expect both a
*describe-the-method* question and a *calculate-the-percentage* question,
and expect a follow-up demanding the error analysis ("give one reason the
answer came out too low"). The composition figures themselves are pure
recall — four numbers that cost nothing to learn and pay every year.
:::

:::misconception
"Air is mostly oxygen — that's what we breathe." Breathing *feels* like
the reason air exists, but it is not the reason air is mostly what it
is. Nitrogen makes up about four-fifths of the air and oxygen only about
one-fifth; every breath takes in far more nitrogen than oxygen. Students
who guess "oxygen" on the composition question hand the mark away — the
examiner is testing whether you learned the table or trusted your gut.
:::
