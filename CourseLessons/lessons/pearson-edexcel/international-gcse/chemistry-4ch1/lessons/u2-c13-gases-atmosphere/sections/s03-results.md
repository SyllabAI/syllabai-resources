Every oxygen-percentage experiment ends the same way: the volume drop is
the volume of oxygen that reacted, and one division turns that into a
percentage. For the copper experiment, the volume fell from 100 cm³ to
79 cm³ because the oxygen was removed as it reacted:

2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)

copper + oxygen → copper(II) oxide

The volume of oxygen that reacted was 100 − 79 = **21 cm³**. The
original air sample was 100 cm³, so:

percentage of oxygen = 21/100 × 100 = **21%**

That is the whole calculation — subtraction, then a percentage. The
iron-rusting version of the experiment asks exactly the same question,
but the arithmetic has one extra step because the apparatus holds air in
three separate places, and the examiner expects you to handle all three.

:::worked-example
**Worked example: the percentage of oxygen in air from the rusting of
iron.** Wet iron filings sit in a conical flask joined to a gas syringe;
the air's volume is measured in three parts. Use this data:

| Measurement | Volume |
|---|---|
| volume of air in conical flask / cm³ | 130 |
| volume of air in connecting tube / cm³ | 12 |
| initial reading on gas syringe / cm³ | 92 |
| final reading on gas syringe / cm³ | 43 |

**Step 1 — total volume of air at the start.** Add all three volumes:
130 + 12 + 92 = **234 cm³**.

**Step 2 — total volume of 'air' at the end.** The oxygen is gone but
the leftover gas still fills flask, tube and syringe: 130 + 12 + 43 =
**185 cm³**.

**Step 3 — volume of oxygen used up.** 234 − 185 = **49 cm³**.

**Step 4 — percentage of oxygen.** 49/234 × 100 = 20.9 ≈ **21%**.

The three-volume bookkeeping is the part students drop marks on: the
oxygen was spread through flask, tube *and* syringe, so the percentage
is 49 out of 234, never 49 out of 92.
:::

The **[[rusting]]** of iron is the slow chemistry doing the same job —
iron rusts in damp air, using up oxygen as it does so, and that reaction
is the measuring tool:

- Put wet iron filings in a conical flask connected to a gas syringe.
- Before starting, measure the volume of air inside the apparatus:
  fill the flask and connecting tube with water, then transfer the
  water to a measuring cylinder. (The small volume occupied by the iron
  filings itself is negligible.)
- Record the initial gas-syringe reading.
- Leave the apparatus for about a **week**, until the reading stops
  changing — rusting is slow.
- Record the final reading, then run the four-step calculation above.

:::key-point
Iron needs **both oxygen and water** to rust — dry iron in dry air does
not rust, and iron underwater with no dissolved oxygen does not either.
That is why the filings are *wet*: the water lets the rusting reaction
proceed, and the reaction consumes the oxygen. Rusting is treated fully
as a corrosion problem in Chapter 14 (u2:c14); here it is your oxygen
removal mechanism.
:::

:::safety
Iron filings can irritate the skin and are a particular hazard if they
get into the eyes — handle them with a spatula, not fingers, and wear
eye protection.
:::

Sometimes the iron experiment comes out **below 21%**. Two standard
reasons, and the mark scheme wants them in this form:

- **Not left long enough** — the iron has not had the chance to react
  with all the oxygen in the apparatus, so some oxygen survives and the
  measured drop is too small.
- **Not enough iron at the start** — the iron must be in [[excess]],
  enough to react with every trace of oxygen; if the iron runs out
  first, the leftover oxygen never gets counted.

Notice both errors push the answer the same way — down. A well-run
experiment with excess iron and enough time converges on 21% from
either direction.

The third method is a demonstration rather than a class experiment,
because it uses white phosphorus, but it is the fastest of the three.
Phosphorus is so reactive that it burns in the oxygen of ordinary air,
forming a phosphorus oxide that dissolves readily in water — and the
water's job in the apparatus matters twice over.

- Set up a bell jar standing in water, with a small evaporating basin
  floating on the water carrying a piece of phosphorus.
- Mark the initial water level on the outside of the jar.
- Remove the bung, touch the phosphorus with a hot metal wire to ignite
  it, and replace the bung quickly.
- The phosphorus burns inside the jar, filling it with white smoke; the
  water level **rises** as oxygen is consumed. The smoke then clears as
  the oxide dissolves in the water.
- When the water level stops rising, mark the final level.
- Measure the change by turning the jar upside down, filling it with
  water to each mark in turn, and pouring into a large measuring
  cylinder.

:::key-point
Phosphorus does not react with water — that is what lets it sit on a
floating basin inside a jar whose lower half is underwater. It reacts
with the *oxygen* above the waterline instead, which is precisely the
gas you are measuring.
:::

:::safety
This is a teacher demonstration. The demonstrator wears goggles or a
face shield and works in a well-ventilated room. The bell jar must be
held down firmly at the start, because the pressure rises as burning
begins. And once the fumes have cleared, the evaporating basin is
sunk with a glass rod — unreacted phosphorus left in warm, damp air can
re-ignite.
:::

The experiments above share one idea with everything that follows:
oxygen is reactive, and its reactions with elements are worth naming.
Elements that burn in oxygen take part in **[[combustion]]** reactions,
and an element burns more brightly and more rapidly in pure oxygen than
in air — because air is only 21% oxygen, while a jar of pure oxygen is
100% of it. Sulfur, one of the elements you will burn in the next
section, gives a product with a name pair worth fixing now.

:::key-point
Sulfur dioxide is also called **sulfur(IV) oxide**. And when it
dissolves in water the product is **[[sulfurous-acid]]** (H₂SO₃) —
**not** sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). The "-ous" acid carries the "- dioxide"
gas; keep the pair matched and the naming marks are safe.
:::

:::exam-hint
All three methods get asked as "why must the [copper / iron /
phosphorus] be in excess?" and the answer is always the same sentence
with the reagent swapped in: **so that all the oxygen in the sample
reacts** — otherwise some oxygen survives and the calculated percentage
is too low. Learn that sentence once and it answers three experiments.
Also expect the reverse question: "give one reason the student's answer
was below 21%" — the two iron-method reasons (insufficient time,
insufficient iron) are the mark-scheme bank.
:::

:::misconception
"A result below 21% means the air that day really contained less
oxygen." The composition of the atmosphere does not drift by several
percent between lessons — a low result is an **experimental error**, not
an atmospheric fact. Trace the error to its mechanism: unreacted oxygen
left behind (too little time or too little iron), or volume readings
taken at the wrong temperature. Examiners reward answers that name the
mechanism, not answers that shrug at the number.
:::
