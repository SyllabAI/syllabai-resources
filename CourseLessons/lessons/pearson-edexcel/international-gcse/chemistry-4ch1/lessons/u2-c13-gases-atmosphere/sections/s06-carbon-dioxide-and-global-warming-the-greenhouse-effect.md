Carbon dioxide enters the atmosphere whenever fossil fuels burn — and
fossil fuels burn everywhere in a modern economy. Coal is mostly carbon,
and burning it in excess oxygen is the simplest combustion there is:

C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)

Petrol is a mixture containing many different **[[hydrocarbon]]**s
(compounds of carbon and hydrogen only — they are treated fully in
Unit 4, starting at Chapter 22 (u4:c22)). One typical component, octane
(C₈H₁₈), burns to give carbon dioxide and water in a ratio worth
checking yourself: the equation is balanced atom for atom.

2C₈H₁₈(l) + 25O₂(g) → 16CO₂(g) + 18H₂O(l)

Every hydrocarbon molecule that burns adds its carbon to the atmosphere
as carbon dioxide, and for roughly the last 200 years — since the
industrial revolution — the level of CO₂ in the atmosphere has been
**increasing**, driven by the burning of fossil fuels and by
deforestation (cutting down trees for agriculture, which removes the
plants that absorb CO₂).

Why should a gas present at only 0.04% of the atmosphere carry so much
weight? Because carbon dioxide is a **[[greenhouse-gas]]** — one of a
select group of gases that interact with the radiation the Earth sends
back out into space. The mechanism runs in four steps:

1. High-energy **ultraviolet and visible light** from the Sun pass
   through the atmosphere and warm the Earth's surface.
2. The warm surface — like any warm surface — radiates energy back out
   as **[[infrared-radiation]]**.
3. Molecules such as CO₂ in the atmosphere **absorb** that infrared
   radiation.
4. The molecules then **re-emit the energy in all directions**,
   including back towards the ground — so the atmosphere, and the
   surface beneath it, end up warmer than they would otherwise be.

That is the greenhouse effect: not a glass roof, but infrared radiation
absorbed and re-emitted by molecules that the rest of the air ignores.
Scientists believe that the rising concentration of carbon dioxide is
intensifying this effect, and that the increase **may contribute to
climate change**. The exact pattern of change is difficult to predict,
but the effects scientists are most concerned about include: polar ice
caps melting, sea levels rising, and more extreme weather such as
floods, droughts and heatwaves.

:::did-you-know
Science is not a chorus, and this topic is a rare case where a
specification acknowledges the debate. Most scientists believe that
increases in carbon dioxide will cause devastating climate change. But
others reject this: they argue that the increases in average world
temperatures during the twentieth century were part of natural heating
and cooling cycles driven by the Sun and by changes in ocean currents,
and that the effect of carbon dioxide is small by comparison. The
scientific majority position is the one carried by the mark schemes —
but knowing the debate exists is part of knowing how evidence and
consensus work.
:::

:::did-you-know
Water vapour is a greenhouse gas too — an important one, as you will
see next section — but its concentration in the air swings between 0 and
4% with the weather. Carbon dioxide's share is far smaller and far
steadier, which is exactly what makes its *trend* so telling: a slow,
ratcheting rise with one chemical cause (combustion) and one biological
counterweight (photosynthesis) that deforestation weakens.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The greenhouse effect is where chemistry meets geography, economics and
politics head-on. The combustion equations in this section are the
chemical end of the energy debate: every energy choice a country makes
— coal, oil, gas, renewables, nuclear — is in part a decision about how
much CO₂ those equations release into the 0.04%. The consequences list
(melting ice caps, rising seas, extreme weather) is written in
geography; the response, in international treaties and carbon pricing,
is written in politics. Chemistry supplies the mechanism that all three
subjects argue about.
:::

:::exam-hint
Spec 2.13 says "know", but the know is precise: a greenhouse gas is one
that **absorbs infrared radiation** — the radiation emitted by the
Earth's surface — **and re-emits it in all directions**. Answers built
on "traps heat like the glass in a greenhouse" or "blocks the Sun's
rays" drop the mark, because both miss the actual mechanism: the Sun's
incoming light passes *through*, and it is the *outgoing infrared* that
the molecules catch. Name the radiation (infrared), name the source
(the Earth's surface), and name the re-emission (all directions).
:::

:::misconception
"The greenhouse effect is a bad thing we should eliminate." Read step
four again before agreeing. The greenhouse effect is what keeps the
Earth warm enough for water to be mostly liquid — without any
greenhouse gases the surface would be far too cold for life as we know
it. The problem is not the effect but its **enhancement**: extra carbon
dioxide from human activity strengthens an essential natural warming
into a destabilising one. Say "enhanced greenhouse effect" for the
problem and you have the concept exactly.
:::
