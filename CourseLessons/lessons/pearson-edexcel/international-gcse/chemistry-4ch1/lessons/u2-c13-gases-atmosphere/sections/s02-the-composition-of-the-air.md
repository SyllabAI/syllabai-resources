Learn the composition of unpolluted, [[dry-air]] as a table first and a
story second. The four most abundant gases, quoted as approximate
**[[percentage-by-volume]]** figures:

| Gas | Amount in air (%) | Amount in air (fraction) |
|---|---|---|
| nitrogen | 78.1 | about 4/5 |
| oxygen | 21.0 | about 1/5 |
| argon | 0.9 | — |
| carbon dioxide | 0.04 | — |

Nitrogen and oxygen between them account for about 99% of the air, with
argon — one of the noble gases — making up most of the remainder. The
other noble gases (helium, neon, krypton, xenon) are present too, but in
amounts so small they round to nothing at this level of precision. Read
the fraction column as the examiner's shorthand: "about one-fifth oxygen"
is the sentence the mark scheme rewards, and it is the sentence the
experiments in this section are designed to confirm.

:::key-point
These figures apply **only to dry, unpolluted air**. Ordinary air can
carry anywhere between 0 and 4% water vapour on top of everything in the
table, which is why the table is always stated for dry air. And although
carbon dioxide's percentage is tiny, it is **rising steadily** because of
human activity — a fact this chapter returns to in the greenhouse
sections.
:::

One measurement dominates this chapter: the percentage of oxygen. The
strategy is always the same — react something with the oxygen in a known
volume of air and watch how much the volume decreases as the oxygen is
removed. The first method uses copper metal, and it is worth mastering
the apparatus carefully because every other method in this chapter is a
variation on its logic.

The apparatus is a pair of **[[gas-syringe]]**s joined through a silica
tube packed with copper turnings. The procedure:

- Push one plunger fully in and set the other at 100 cm³. The system now
  contains exactly 100 cm³ of air.
- Heat the silica tube strongly with a roaring Bunsen flame.
- Push the left plunger in: air is forced over the heated copper and the
  right plunger moves out to receive it.
- Push the plungers in sequence, back and forth, so the air keeps
  passing over the hot copper. The pink-brown copper turns black as
  copper(II) oxide forms on it.
- As the copper reacts, slide the Bunsen burner along the tube so the
  flame is always heating fresh copper.
- Watch the total gas volume fall as oxygen is consumed. Keep sweeping
  the air until the volume **no longer changes**.
- Let the apparatus cool back to room temperature, then take the final
  reading — gases expand when heated, so a hot reading would be too high.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and heat carefully: a flame applied clumsily can
crack the glass syringe. Take care not to push the opposite plunger
completely out of its barrel during the sweeping.
:::

A typical result set:

| Measurement | Volume |
|---|---|
| initial volume of air in the system / cm³ | 100 |
| final volume of 'air' in the system / cm³ | 79 |

The volume fell by 21 cm³, and the next section converts that fall into
the percentage of oxygen. Two facts make the method work, and both are
examinable in their own right.

:::key-point
The copper must be **in [[excess]]** — more than enough to react with
all the oxygen in the air sample. You will see unreacted copper left at
the end, and that is deliberate: if the copper ran out first, some
oxygen would survive unreacted and the percentage you calculate would be
**too low**.
:::

:::key-point
The final 79 cm³ is called 'air' in inverted commas because it is no
longer really air — the oxygen has gone and what remains is mostly
nitrogen. Nitrogen is very unreactive: put a lighted splint into this
leftover gas and the splint goes out, because **oxygen is needed for
things to burn**. That observation is itself evidence the experiment did
its job.
:::

:::exam-hint
"State why the apparatus is allowed to cool before the final volume is
recorded" is a one-mark gift if you know the particle model: gases
expand when heated, so a reading taken hot overstates the final volume
and understates the calculated oxygen percentage. And in
describe-the-method questions, the phrase the mark scheme wants for the
end-point is **"until there is no further change in volume"** — not
"until it looks finished".
:::

:::misconception
"The volume goes down because the copper takes up space / the copper is
lost." The volume falls because **oxygen gas is removed from the gas
mixture** — it reacts with the copper and becomes part of a solid
(copper(II) oxide) sitting on the tube's surface. Solids contribute
essentially nothing to the gas volume. The copper itself is in excess:
whatever volume change you measure belongs to the oxygen, which is
exactly why the method measures oxygen.
:::
