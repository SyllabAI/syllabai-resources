Magnesium oxide dissolving to give an alkaline solution and sulfur
dioxide dissolving to give an acidic one are not accidents — they are
two halves of a pattern that runs through the whole of chemistry. When
elements burn in oxygen, you can generalise about the **[[oxide]]**s
they form, and the generalisation follows the metal/non-metal line you
already know from the Periodic Table (u1:c04).

**Metal oxides** are ionic compounds, built from metal ions and oxide
ions (O²⁻):

- they are usually **basic oxides**, which means they react with acids
  to form salts;
- they are usually **insoluble** in water;
- the ones that *are* soluble react with the water to form **alkaline**
  solutions containing hydroxide (OH⁻) ions — magnesium hydroxide from
  magnesium oxide is the worked case from the previous section.

**Non-metal oxides** are covalent molecular compounds:

- they are usually **acidic oxides**, which react with alkalis and bases
  to form salts;
- they are **often soluble** in water, reacting with it to form
  **acidic** solutions containing hydrogen (H⁺) ions — sulfur dioxide's
  sulfurous acid is the example already on your page, and carbon
  dioxide behaves the same way.

Learn the two lists as mirror images: ionic vs covalent, basic vs
acidic, insoluble vs often soluble, alkaline vs acidic solutions. One
element, one side of the divide — that is the entire rule set.

Carbon dioxide deserves its own introduction, because the next two
sections follow it into the atmosphere. It is a colourless gas, most
easily made in the laboratory by reacting dilute hydrochloric acid with
calcium carbonate in the form of marble chips:

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

But there is a second route to carbon dioxide that matters for this
chapter: heat most metal **carbonates** strongly and they split apart,
giving the metal oxide and carbon dioxide. Breaking a compound up by
heating it is called **[[thermal-decomposition]]**:

- copper(II) carbonate is a **green** powder that decomposes on heating
  to give **black** copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide:

  CuCO₃(s) → CuO(s) + CO₂(g)

- calcium carbonate needs a much higher temperature before it
  decomposes — and the reaction is commercially important, because it
  converts limestone (calcium carbonate) into quicklime (calcium
  oxide):

  CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

:::exam-hint
Fix the two colours now and keep them for the rest of the course:
copper(II) **carbonate is green**, copper(II) **oxide is black**. The
green-to-black colour change on heating is the observation question for
copper(II) carbonate's thermal decomposition, and it also fingerprints
the black coating formed in the copper-oxygen experiment back in
Section 2 — same black compound, copper(II) oxide, made two different
ways.
:::

:::misconception
"Heating copper(II) carbonate is combustion — both need a Bunsen
burner." The Bunsen burner supplies heat in both cases, but the
chemistry is different. **Combustion** is an element or compound
*reacting with oxygen* — oxygen is a reactant and appears on the left of
the equation. **Thermal decomposition** is *one compound splitting
into two or more products* using heat alone — no oxygen is consumed, and
CO₂ appears on the right as a *product*. If oxygen were a reactant,
copper(II) carbonate could not decompose in a sealed jar; it can, and
that is the test of the distinction.
:::
