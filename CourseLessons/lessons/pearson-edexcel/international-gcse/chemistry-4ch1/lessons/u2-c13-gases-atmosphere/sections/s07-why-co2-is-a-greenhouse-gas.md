The presence of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere is essential to life
on Earth: they keep the planet at a temperature at which water is
mostly liquid. But it is just as important that the main constituents
of the air — nitrogen and oxygen — are *not* greenhouse gases. If they
were, the atmosphere would absorb outgoing infrared at every wavelength
and the Earth would be too hot to support life as we know it. So what
qualifies carbon dioxide and water vapour for a job that 99% of the air
cannot do?

The qualification rule first: a **[[greenhouse-gas]]** must be able to
**absorb infrared radiation** emitted by the Earth's surface and then
**emit it again in all directions**, warming the atmosphere. All
molecules vibrate — their bonds stretch and bend constantly. A molecule
can absorb infrared radiation when those vibrations involve a **change
in the polarity of the molecule**. Polarity is therefore the whole
story, and it is worth building the idea carefully.

**What is polarity?** A **[[polar-molecule]]** has one end slightly
negative and the other end slightly positive. Polarity arises because
different atoms attract the electrons in a covalent bond to different
extents — a property called **[[electronegativity]]** — and it also
depends on the shape of the molecule. Water is the classic example: the oxygen atom attracts the
shared electrons in both O–H bonds more strongly than the hydrogen
atoms do, so the oxygen end of the molecule carries a small negative
charge (δ−) and the hydrogen ends carry small positive charges (δ+).
The polarity of a water molecule changes as it vibrates, and that is
why **water vapour is an important greenhouse gas**.

Now apply the rule to the three gases that matter.

**Carbon dioxide** is a *non-polar* molecule as it stands: it is linear
— the three atoms sit in a straight line, O=C=O — and symmetrical, so
the bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel. There is no
positive end and no negative end. But the molecule does not stand
still. As a CO₂ molecule **vibrates**, its geometry momentarily distorts
— the symmetric stretch and bend destroy the perfect symmetry for an
instant — and in those moments the molecule becomes non-symmetrical and
polar. Because its polarity **changes as it vibrates**, carbon dioxide
can absorb infrared radiation, and it is a greenhouse gas.

**Oxygen and nitrogen** are the opposite case in every particular. Each
molecule contains two *identical* atoms, so the electrons in the bond
are shared equally and there are no partial charges to begin with. When
the molecules vibrate, they have only one way of vibrating — the bond
simply stretches and compresses — and the molecule **remains
non-polar throughout**. No change in polarity means no ability to absorb
infrared radiation, so N₂ and O₂ are **not greenhouse gases**, whatever
their concentration.

That is the complete answer to the section's question, and it is a
genuine explanation — the kind that turns a "know" statement into
understanding. The atmosphere warms itself through the small fraction
of molecules whose vibrations keep changing their polarity, and the two
gases that dominate the air are transparent to infrared precisely
because identical atoms cannot build a dipole, vibrating or not.

:::summary
Dry air is approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and 0.04%
carbon dioxide (figures for dry, unpolluted air; water vapour adds up to
4% more). The percentage of oxygen can be measured by reacting a
reagent — heated copper, damp iron filings or burning phosphorus — with
the oxygen in a known volume of air and measuring the volume decrease:
copper gives 100 → 79 cm³ for 21%, iron gives the three-volume
calculation 234 → 185 cm³ for 49 cm³ used up ≈ 21%, and in every case
the reagent must be in **excess** so all the oxygen reacts; low results
come from too little time or too little reagent. Elements burn in
oxygen in **combustion** reactions: magnesium (bright white flame →
white MgO, slightly soluble to alkaline solution), sulfur (blue flame →
poisonous colourless SO₂, dissolving to **sulfurous** acid H₂SO₃) and
hydrogen (pale blue flame → water; H₂/O₂ mixtures explode — the squeaky
pop). Metal oxides are ionic and usually **basic**; non-metal oxides
are covalent and usually **acidic**. Carbon dioxide forms in the lab
from marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid, and by **thermal
decomposition** of metal carbonates (green copper(II) carbonate → black
copper(II) oxide + CO₂; limestone → quicklime at high temperature). It
reaches the atmosphere from fossil-fuel combustion and it is a
**greenhouse gas**: it absorbs the infrared radiation the warm surface
emits and re-emits it in all directions, because its polarity changes
as it vibrates — while N₂ and O₂, with equally shared electrons and no
polarity change, absorb nothing.
:::

:::exam-hint
The polarity explanation is a four-link chain, and mark schemes pay the
links separately: (1) all molecules vibrate; (2) a molecule absorbs IR
only if its vibration changes its polarity; (3) CO₂ is symmetric and
non-polar at rest, but vibration makes it momentarily polar — so it
absorbs; (4) O₂ and N₂ have two identical atoms, share electrons
equally and stay non-polar however they vibrate — so they cannot
absorb. Answers that just say "CO₂ absorbs IR but oxygen doesn't" earn
the first and last marks and lose the two reasoning links in between.
:::

:::misconception
"CO₂ is non-polar, so it cannot interact with infrared radiation at
all." Non-polar describes the molecule's *resting* shape — a straight,
symmetrical O=C=O line whose bond dipoles cancel. But the molecule is
vibrating, and the vibration repeatedly breaks the symmetry, creating a
momentary polarity that lets it absorb IR. The correct statement is
"non-polar at rest, polar in vibration" — the vibration *is* the
mechanism, not an obstacle to it.
:::
