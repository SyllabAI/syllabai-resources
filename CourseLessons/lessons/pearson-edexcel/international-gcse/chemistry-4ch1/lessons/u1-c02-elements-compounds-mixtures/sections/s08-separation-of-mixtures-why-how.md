Separating mixtures is not a school-only exercise — it is one of the
load-bearing walls of the whole chemical industry. Crude oil arriving from
a well is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons and is split by
distillation before anything useful can be made from it. Fresh water is
produced from salt water by distillation on an industrial scale. The
enrichment of uranium — separating two forms of the same element — is a
separation problem. And in forensic science, the components of a mixture
usually have to be **separated before they can be analysed**: an unseparated
sample tells you almost nothing.

The common thread: because mixtures were made by mixing (no reaction),
they can be undone by **physical means**. Which physical method you choose
depends on what is mixed with what — an insoluble solid in a liquid needs
**filtration** (sand from water), a dissolved solid needs
**crystallisation** or **simple distillation**, two miscible liquids need
**fractional distillation**, and dissolved dyes yield to **paper
chromatography**. Spec point 1.10 asks you to *describe* each of the first
four techniques; §s09 builds every one of them, and chromatography follows
in §s10.

:::key-point
Mixtures are separated by **physical means** because no chemical reaction
holds them together. The method is chosen by *what is mixed with what*:
insoluble solid → filtration; dissolved solid → crystallisation or simple
distillation; miscible liquids → fractional distillation; dyes → paper
chromatography.
:::

:::misconception
"Separating a mixture needs a chemical reaction to pull the substances
apart." No — that is compound thinking. Physical changes (boiling,
condensing, filtering, evaporating, dissolving) are enough for mixtures;
it is **compounds** that need chemical reactions such as electrolysis to
break them up.
:::

:::exam-hint
"State which separation method you would use to …" (1 mark each): pick the
method from the *mixture type*, not from habit. Mark schemes award the
method name only — "filtration", "crystallisation", "simple distillation",
"fractional distillation", "paper chromatography" — so spell out the full
name; "distillation" alone is ambiguous when fractional is intended.
:::
