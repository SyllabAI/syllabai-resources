Pure substances — elements and pure compounds alike — melt and boil at
**fixed temperatures**. Water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C; every
clean sample of water does exactly the same. **Mixtures usually melt and
boil over a range of temperatures** instead. That single contrast is spec
point 1.9, and it hands you a practical purity test.

Impurities push both numbers the "wrong" way: they **lower the melting
point** and **raise the boiling point**. Dissolve 10 g of common salt
(sodium chloride) in 1 litre of water and the melting point drops to about
−0.6 °C while the boiling point climbs to about 100.2 °C. (This is why salt
gritters scatter salt on winter roads — the salty water is harder to freeze
than pure water.)

The melting point gives chemists a working purity test. Suppose you make
aspirin in a sixth-form practical. Aspirin is a white powder that melts at
138 °C. You record the temperature at which your sample *starts* to melt
and the temperature at which it has *fully* melted. If your sample melts
anywhere in the range 128–134 °C you know it is quite impure — it melts
over a wide range, and below the melting point of the pure substance.

Note how this reuses Lesson u1:c01: melting point and boiling point are the
same numbers you used to predict the state of a substance — but here they
become evidence about **what is dissolved in it**.

:::key-point
Pure substance → **fixed** melting and boiling point. Mixture → melts and
boils over a **range**. Impurities **lower** the melting point and
**raise** the boiling point. Melting point is the classic lab test for
purity.
:::

:::maths-skills
Reading a purity test is comparison logic, not calculation: (i) does the
sample melt at a single temperature or across a range? (ii) is that range
below the pure substance's melting point? Sample at 128–134 °C vs pure at
138 °C fails both tests at once — range *and* depression — so the verdict
is "impure" with two pieces of evidence.
:::

:::misconception
"Impurities raise the melting point, because there is more stuff to melt."
No — the opposite: dissolving an impurity **lowers** the melting point
(salt water freezes at about −0.6 °C, not above 0 °C) and raises the
boiling point (about 100.2 °C, not below 100 °C).
:::

:::exam-hint
"Substance X melts at 122 °C; substance Y melts between 87 and 93 °C.
Explain which is the pure substance" (2–3 marks): name **X**, then give the
reason in mark-scheme language — a pure substance has a **fixed/sharp
melting point**, while Y melts **over a range of temperatures**, so Y
contains an impurity. Both halves are needed: the verdict *and* the
fixed-vs-range rule.
:::
