A **[[compound]]** is formed when two or more elements **chemically
combine** — and the moment that reaction happens, the proportions lock. The
elements always combine in **fixed proportions**: the same compound, made
in London or on Mars, always contains exactly the same ratio of its
elements. Hydrogen and fluorine always combine to form hydrogen fluoride,
[[formula]] HF; magnesium and fluorine always form magnesium fluoride,
MgF₂; carbon dioxide is always CO₂ and methane always CH₄. You cannot make
a "stronger" or "weaker" carbon dioxide by using more carbon or more oxygen
— chemistry does not negotiate on ratios.

In diagrams, a compound shows **more than one type of atom bonded
together**. That picture is worth pausing on: the atoms are
not just mixed side by side as in a mixture — they are *bonded*, which is
precisely why undoing a compound needs a chemical reaction (§s06).

:::key-point
Compounds = two or more elements chemically combined in **fixed
proportions**. Hydrogen fluoride is always HF; magnesium fluoride is always
MgF₂; carbon dioxide is always CO₂. Diagrams show two or more atom types
bonded together.
:::

:::misconception
"You can make carbon dioxide with different proportions of carbon and
oxygen, like mixing more or less squash in water." No — that is mixture
thinking. In a compound the ratio is fixed by the bonding: CO₂ is always
two oxygen atoms per carbon atom, never more, never fewer. Change the
ratio and you are either making a different compound or leaving unreacted
excess behind.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why magnesium oxide is a compound" (2 marks): mark schemes want
both halves — (i) it contains two (or more) elements **chemically
combined**, and (ii) the elements are present in **fixed proportion**.
Writing only "it has different types of atom" describes a mixture of atoms
just as well — the "chemically combined" phrase is what scores.
:::
