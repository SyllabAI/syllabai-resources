**Filtration** separates a solid from a liquid. Pour the mixture through
filter paper: the solid left behind on the paper is the **[[residue]]**, the
liquid that comes through is the **[[filtrate]]** (Figure 2.6 — sand from
water). Filtration also separates **two solids**, provided only one of them
is soluble in water — which is exactly the trick used for rock salt below.

**Crystallisation** separates a dissolved solid (a solute) from its
solution. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to boil off some water
until an almost **[[saturated-solution]]** solution forms — you
can test this by dipping a glass rod in and seeing if small crystals form
on its surface. Then turn the Bunsen burner off and let the crystals grow
as more water evaporates and the solution cools; filter them off at the end
(Figure 2.7).

**Rock salt** is salt contaminated with earthy, rocky impurities that are
*not* soluble in water. Crush it and mix with hot water: the salt
dissolves, the impurities do not. Filter — the insoluble impurities stay on
the filter paper as residue, and the filtrate is a clean salt solution.
Crystallise the filtrate and pure salt drops out. The same three moves
(dissolve, filter, crystallise) separate *any* pair of solids where one is
soluble and the other is not.

**Simple distillation** gets the *solvent* back instead. Boil the solution:
the water evaporates, travels into the condenser, and is condensed back to
a pure liquid to collect — while the salt stays in the flask. (Run it long
enough and the solution becomes so concentrated that the salt crystallises
out too.) One detail examiners love: water always enters the condenser at
the **lower** end, so the jacket fills completely and stays full even if
the water flow stops.

**Fractional distillation** separates two **[[miscible]]** liquids — ones
that mix in any proportion to form a single layer — using their different
boiling points: ethanol boils at 78 °C, water at 100 °C. With careful
heating the column stays at the lower temperature, so all the water
condenses inside the column and trickles back to the flask while only
ethanol vapour reaches the top and passes into the condenser. (The column
is often packed with glass beads to help the separation; the ethanol that
comes out is about 96% pure, and the last 4% of water cannot be removed by
distillation — the book files both details under "beyond International
GCSE".)

**Paper chromatography** — the technique that finishes this lesson —
separates mixtures of dyes. Most inks and food colourings are not single
colours but mixtures of dyes; chromatography splits them up the paper, and
it even works for colourless substances like sugars if a way is found to
make the spots visible. The method (spec 1.13):

- draw the **[[baseline]]** in **pencil**, about 1 cm from the bottom of the
  paper — never pen, because pen ink would move up the paper with the
  solvent;
- spot the dye mixture on the line and let it dry;
- stand the paper in a beaker with a small amount of solvent, **below the
  pencil line** — or the inks would simply dissolve into the solvent;
- put a lid (a watch glass) on the beaker so the atmosphere saturates with
  solvent vapour, which stops the solvent evaporating off the paper;
- when the solvent has risen to about 1 cm from the top, mark the highest
  level it reached — the **[[solvent-front]]** — then leave the paper to dry.

The pattern left on the paper is the **chromatogram**; reading it is §s10.

:::safety
Avoid skin contact with the solvents and the dyes, especially if you have
sensitive skin. (Book safety note for the chromatography work.)
:::

:::key-point
Water feeds into the condenser at the **lower** end: the jacket fills
better, and if the flow stops the condenser stays full of water.
:::

:::key-point
If a dye does not move from the pencil line, it is not soluble in that
solvent — try another. If it moves with the solvent front, it is *too*
soluble — try another. Finding the right solvent is done by experimenting.
:::

:::worked-example
The rock-salt run is the exam's favourite separation story, so rehearse it
as a chain: **crush + hot water** (salt dissolves, rock does not) →
**filter** (residue: insoluble impurities; filtrate: salt solution) →
**crystallise** (heat to near-saturation, rod test, Bunsen off, cool,
filter the crystals). Each arrow is a mark: method + what it removes.
:::

:::misconception
"You can get salt back from salt water by filtering the solution." No —
filtration only catches solids that are *insoluble*. Dissolved salt passes
straight through filter paper with the water; recovering it needs
crystallisation (or simple distillation, which collects the water instead).
:::

:::exam-hint
Method-choice questions follow the mixture type: insoluble solid + liquid →
**filtration**; dissolved solid from solution → **crystallisation** (want
the solid) or **simple distillation** (want the solvent); two miscible
liquids → **fractional distillation**; two solids, one soluble → **dissolve
and filter**. Describing the rock-salt method earns marks in order —
crush/dissolve, filter, crystallise — so narrate it stepwise, naming
residue and filtrate.
:::
