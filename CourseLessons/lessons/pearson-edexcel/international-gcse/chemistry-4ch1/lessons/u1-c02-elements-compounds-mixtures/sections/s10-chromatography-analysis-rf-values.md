Chromatography does more than split a mixture — it tells you **what is in
it**. Suppose your unknown mixture (m) might contain dyes d1, d2, d3 and
d4. Draw a pencil line on a larger sheet, spot the mixture at one mark and
small spots of the four *known* dyes at the others, then develop the
[[chromatogram]] as in §s09. Then compare — and measure
carefully: always measure to the **centre of the spot**.

The comparison rule: if a spot in the mixture has the **same colour** and
travelled the **same distance** as a known dye, they match. Here, mixture
m produces spots corresponding to d1, d3 and d4. Dye
d2 is the trap: its colour matches a spot in the mixture, but it travelled
a *different* distance — so it must be a different compound.

Raw distances are unreliable, because each run's solvent moves a different
distance up the paper. So we report a ratio instead — the
**[[rf-value]]**:

> Rf = distance moved by the spot (from the pencil line) ÷ distance moved
> by the solvent front (from the pencil line)

In the chromatogram just described, the solvent front moved 3.6 cm, so for
dye d3: Rf =
2.9 ÷ 3.6 = **0.81**. The mixture's three spots give blue 0.9 ÷ 3.6 =
0.25, orange 2.0 ÷ 3.6 = 0.56, green 2.9 ÷ 3.6 = 0.81 — and the known dyes
give d1 = 0.56, d2 = 0.36, d3 = 0.81, d4 = 0.25. Matching values identify
the mixture's contents: **d1, d3 and d4**.

Two honesty rules about Rf: it must fall **between 0 and 1** (a value
bigger than 1 means you divided the wrong way round), and it has **no
units**. And Rf values only compare fairly on the **same paper and same
solvent** — here every spot shared one sheet, so the comparison was safe;
separate sheets are fine too, as long as paper type and solvent match.

This lesson's practice is wired to the question bank below.

:::key-point
Rf value = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front,
measured to the centre of each spot. Rf always lies between **0 and 1**,
has **no units**, and only compares fairly on the same paper with the same
solvent.
:::

:::worked-example
Mixture m vs dyes d1–d4 (solvent front 3.6 cm): m's spots —
blue 0.25, orange 0.56, green 0.81. Known dyes — d1 0.56, d2 0.36,
d3 0.81, d4 0.25. Match: 0.25 = d4, 0.56 = d1, 0.81 = d3 → **m contains
d1, d3 and d4**. d2's colour matches but its Rf (0.36) appears nowhere in
m, so d2 is absent — colour alone never decides; distance (Rf) does.
:::

:::maths-skills
Rf is a ratio of two lengths, so the skill is division with sense-checking:
spot distance (smaller) on top, solvent-front distance (larger) below.
Sanity-check the answer — if Rf > 1 you inverted the fraction. Quote Rf to
2 decimal places (0.25, 0.56, 0.81 in the example above).
:::

:::cross-curricular
**Maths:** an Rf value is a scale-invariant ratio — the same idea as a
scale factor on a map, which is exactly why it works when raw distances
change between runs.
**Forensics:** the anonymous-letter problem is real forensic science —
comparing an unknown ink against a library of known inks by matching Rf
values on the same chromatogram.
:::

:::misconception
"The dye that moved furthest up the paper is simply 'the best' dye — and a
spot can travel further than the solvent." No — a spot can never overtake
the solvent front, so Rf can never exceed 1. And distance moved is not a
property to quote: Rf (on the same paper and solvent) is, because raw
distances change from run to run.
:::

:::exam-hint
Chromatogram questions have three stock mark lines: (1) same dye ⇔ same
colour **and** same distance/Rf; (2) a "minimum of N dyes" answer — count
the spots, but note two dyes could coincide in one spot (re-run with a
different solvent to check); (3) always measure to the **centre of the
spot**. And keep Rf unitless — writing "0.81 cm" throws the mark away.
:::

:::summary
- Every substance is an [[element]], a [[compound]] or a [[mixture]];
  diagrams show it: one atom type (element), two or more atom types
  **bonded in fixed proportions** (compound), different substances not
  chemically joined in **any proportion** (mixture).
- Mixtures keep their components' properties (iron stays grey, sulfur
  yellow) and separate by **physical means**; compounds have their own new
  properties and need **chemical reactions** (e.g. electrolysis) to undo.
- A pure substance melts and boils at **fixed temperatures**; a mixture
  melts and boils over a **range** — impurities lower the melting point
  and raise the boiling point.
- Separation methods: **filtration** (insoluble solid; residue vs
  filtrate), **crystallisation** (dissolved solid; evaporate to near
  saturation, cool), **simple distillation** (collect the solvent;
  condenser water enters at the bottom), **fractional distillation**
  (miscible liquids by boiling point: ethanol 78 °C vs water 100 °C).
- **Paper chromatography**: pencil baseline, solvent below the line, lid
  on, mark the solvent front; a [[chromatogram]] identifies dyes by
  matching spots; **Rf = spot distance ÷ solvent-front distance**, always
  0–1, no units, same paper and solvent to compare.
:::
