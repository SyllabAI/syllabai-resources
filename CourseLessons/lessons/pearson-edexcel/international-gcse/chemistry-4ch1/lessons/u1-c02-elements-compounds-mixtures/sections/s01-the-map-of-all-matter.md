Look around: nearly everything you meet in everyday life is a **mixture**.
The air you breathe contains elements such as nitrogen and oxygen along
with compounds such as carbon dioxide and nitrogen oxides. The food you eat
and the drinks you drink are mixtures too. Even a gold ring is not pure gold —
an 18-carat ring is only 75% gold, with the rest usually copper. Only a few
things — a nugget of pure metal, a diamond, a bottle of distilled water — come
close to being the single, uncontaminated substances chemists call **pure**.

Chemistry sorts every substance into one of three boxes: an [[element]], a
[[compound]] or a [[mixture]]. Getting that classification right is the
business of this lesson, and it matters far beyond the classroom. Most
forensic work starts by **separating** a mixture into its components — you
cannot analyse a sample until the things mixed into it are apart. By the end
of this lesson you will be able to:

- classify any substance as an element, compound or mixture;
- say why a pure substance melts and boils at a **fixed temperature** while a
  mixture melts and boils over a **range**;
- describe the four separation techniques — simple distillation, fractional
  distillation, filtration and crystallisation — plus paper chromatography;
- use a **chromatogram** and **Rf values** to work out what a mixture
  contains.

:::key-point
Every substance is an element, a compound or a mixture. Elements and
compounds are **pure**; mixtures are not, and their components can be
separated by physical means such as filtration or distillation.
:::

:::prior-knowledge
From Lesson u1:c01 (States of matter) you already know the terms **melting
point**, **boiling point**, **solute** and **solvent** — we use all of them
here, especially in the purity test (§s07) and the separation methods (§s09).
From Key Stage 3 you should remember that everything is made of atoms; if
the word *atom* is hazy for you, Chapter 3 (u1:c03) is where it is properly
defined.
:::

:::cross-curricular
**Forensics:** identifying an unknown substance — ink from a ransom letter,
a powder at a crime scene — always begins by separating the mixture and ends
with chromatography (§s10).
**Geography/industry:** producing fresh water from salt water (desalination)
is the simple distillation of §s09 at industrial scale.
:::

:::misconception
"The water we drink is pure water." No — pure water is a compound (H₂O
only). Drinking water is a **mixture**: water with dissolved substances in
it. That is exactly why it boils over a range, not at exactly 100 °C.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Classify" and "state whether … is an element, compound or mixture" are
1-mark command words: give the box, then (for 2 marks) the reason — e.g.
"*mixture* because the components are not chemically joined and are present
in no fixed proportion". Reasons score; vibes do not.
:::
