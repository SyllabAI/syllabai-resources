A **[[mixture]]** is what you get when substances are simply put together —
**no chemical reaction occurs**. The components can be elements, compounds,
or both at once, and (unlike a compound) they can be present in **any
proportion**: you can put in as much or as little of each component as you
like, and it is still the same mixture. Nothing locks the ratio in, because
nothing has reacted.

The book's Figure 2.5 gives the three possible recipes, and they are worth
memorising as a classification toolkit:

- a **mixture of elements** — nitrogen and oxygen (this is essentially the
  air);
- a **mixture of compounds** — carbon dioxide and water vapour;
- a **mixture of an element with a compound** — carbon dioxide and nitrogen.

Because no reaction happens, the components keep their own properties and
can be got back apart by physical means — the theme of §s06 and §s09.

:::key-point
In a mixture no chemical reaction occurs; the components (elements
and/or compounds) can be present in **any proportion**. Mixtures of
elements, of compounds, and of an element with a compound are all possible.
:::

:::misconception
"A mixture has to be made of either all elements or all compounds." No —
the third combination is just as legal: an element mixed with a compound
(carbon dioxide and nitrogen, Figure 2.5). Classification asks *what is in
it and has it reacted*, not which single category the contents belong to.
:::

:::exam-hint
2-mark "explain why X is a mixture" answers score with: (i) the substances
are **not chemically joined** (no reaction has occurred) and (ii) they are
**not in fixed proportions** / can be mixed in any ratio. Either half alone
usually banks 1 mark; both halves bank both.
:::
