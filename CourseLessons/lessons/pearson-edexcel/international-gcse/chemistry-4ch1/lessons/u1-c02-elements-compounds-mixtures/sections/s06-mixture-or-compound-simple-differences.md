Water is the book's cleanest contrast. In **water** (a compound), every
single water molecule has two hydrogen atoms combined with one oxygen atom
— it never varies. In a **mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases**, the two
can be present in any proportion you fancy. The same story with iron and
sulfur: as a mixture you can combine them in any ratio, but iron sulfide
(FeS) — the compound — always contains iron and sulfur in exactly the same
proportion.

Proportions are only half the story. The other half is **properties**. In a
mixture of iron and sulfur, each element keeps its own properties: the iron
stays grey, the sulfur stays yellow, and when you add dilute hydrochloric
acid the iron fizzes to produce hydrogen while the sulfur does not react at
all. The compound iron sulfide behaves completely differently — it reacts
with acids in its own way, unlike either element. Compare a mixture of
hydrogen and oxygen with the compound water: put a flame to the mixture and
it explodes; the compound, water, is a colourless liquid that simply puts
the flame out.

**Separation follows the same divide.** Mixtures can be separated by
**physical means** — methods like changing the temperature or dissolving
part of the mixture, which involve no chemical reactions. A magnet pulls
the iron out of an iron–sulfur mixture while the sulfur stays behind. The
elements in a compound, though, cannot be separated by physical means:
turning water back into hydrogen and oxygen requires electrolysis (a
chemical process — Chapter 10). You *can* separate a mixture of hydrogen
and oxygen physically, though — oxygen condenses to a liquid at a much
higher temperature than hydrogen (−183 °C as opposed to −253 °C), so
cooling far enough leaves the oxygen liquid while hydrogen stays gaseous.

Finally, the vocabulary of purity: a mixture is not a pure substance. If a
sample contains only a small amount of an unwanted substance, that
unwanted substance is called an **[[impurity]]**.

:::key-point
Mixture: components in **any proportion**, each **keeping its own
properties**, separable by **physical means**. Compound: **fixed
proportion**, its **own new properties**, separable only by **chemical
reactions** (e.g. electrolysis of water).
:::

:::misconception
"Iron sulfide is just iron and sulfur sitting next to each other, so it
still reacts with acid like iron does." No — once iron and sulfur are
chemically combined, the individual properties are gone. Iron sulfide
reacts with acids differently from iron; sulfur's reaction with the acid
disappears too. New bonds mean new substance.
:::

:::exam-hint
The 4–6 mark "compare a mixture of iron and sulfur with the compound iron
sulfide" answer earns marks per difference stated: composition (any ratio
vs fixed), properties (kept vs new — use the acid test), separation
(physical vs chemical). Mark schemes reward precise comparisons, so write
"the **mixture** keeps the properties of iron and sulfur" — not just
"they are different".
:::
