The second reaction family of Statement 4.36C is the carbonates:
a [[metal-carbonate]] reacts with an acid to give a [[salt]],
carbon dioxide and water — the Unit 2 rule again (u2:c16), and
dilute ethanoic acid obeys it like any other acid. What changes
is only the salt, and the same ethanoate arithmetic as the
metals section runs underneath it.

With sodium carbonate the reaction fizzes vigorously and leaves
a colourless solution of **sodium ethanoate**:

Na₂CO₃(s) + 2CH₃COOH(aq) → 2CH₃COONa(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

With calcium carbonate the story repeats: lots of fizzing and a
colourless solution — this time containing **calcium ethanoate**:

2CH₃COOH(aq) + CaCO₃(s) → (CH₃COO)₂Ca(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

Notice that both equations need **two** acid molecules per
carbonate unit. The arithmetic is the metals section's again,
viewed from the other side: the carbonate ion is doubly charged
(CO₃²⁻), so it needs either two singly-charged metal ions or one
doubly-charged one — and it needs two protons to become water
and carbon dioxide. Each proton leaves behind one [[ethanoate-ion]]
in solution, so two protons means two CH₃COOH molecules on the
left. The salt side then just counts charges: two Na⁺ (each
with one ethanoate) or one Ca²⁺ (with two ethanoates).

This is also the reaction that makes the family's fizz a
diagnostic: any solution that fizzes on a carbonate and turns
limewater milky with the gas produced is behaving as an acid —
and for the carboxylic acids, gently. Ethanoic acid plus calcium
carbonate is, in fact, a household-scale example of acid-on-
limescale chemistry, run at vinegar strength rather than
laboratory strength.

:::key-point
Both of this chapter's salt families come from one move: the
ethanoate ion is 1−, so count the positive charge it must
neutralise. Metals: Mg²⁺ → (CH₃COO)₂Mg. Carbonates: CO₃²⁻ →
two protons, two ethanoates, and the salt holds whichever metal
the carbonate brought — CH₃COONa from sodium carbonate,
(CH₃COO)₂Ca from calcium carbonate. One counting rule, every
formula.
:::

:::exam-hint
The observation trio for a carbonate item is fizzing (carbon
dioxide gas), a colourless solution forming (the ethanoate salt
is soluble), and the limewater test turning milky if you route
the gas through it. Naming the salt scores separately from
describing the observation — write both. And the 2 in front of
CH₃COOH is not decorative: it is the carbonate's two negative
charges demanding two protons, and items ask for balanced
equations with the coefficient in place.
:::

:::misconception
"The carbon dioxide is part of the salt." The CO₂ escapes as a
gas — the fizz is the visual proof. The salt is only the ions
left dissolved in the solution: metal cation plus ethanoate
anion. Writing CO₂ into a salt formula (CH₃COONa·CO₂ and the
like) double-counts the carbonate's atoms; the carbonate ion
split into CO₂ and H₂O, and its metal joined the ethanoate.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write both carbonate equations — sodium and calcium —
balanced, with state symbols? Can you explain why two acid
molecules are needed per carbonate either through the CO₃²⁻
charge or through the two protons? Can you name both salts from
their ion pairs? Then 4.36C is fully delivered and only the
question bank remains.
:::
