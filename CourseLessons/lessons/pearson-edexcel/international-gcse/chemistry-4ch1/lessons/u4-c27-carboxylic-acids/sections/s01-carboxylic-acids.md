The alcohols lesson ended with a sour note: oxidise ethanol —
microbially in open air or sharply in the lab with dichromate(VI)
— and the product is **ethanoic acid**. That product is not a
one-off. It is the representative of a whole family of organic
acids, the **[[carboxylic-acid]]s**, and this lesson gives that
family the same treatment the alcohols received: structure first,
then names, then reactions (u4:c26).

Every member of the family carries the same [[functional-group]]:
the **carboxyl group, –COOH**. Read the arrangement carefully,
because it is two groups welded onto one carbon — a carbonyl
(C=O) and a hydroxyl (O–H) sharing the same end carbon atom:

```
      O
      ‖
—C—O—H
```

That shared carbon is what makes the chemistry of this family
different from both the alcohols (O–H alone) and the aldehydes
(C=O alone). It is also why the group is always found at the end
of a chain: the bonding arrangement needs a carbon with a spare
bond for the rest of the molecule.

The most familiar member is [[ethanoic-acid]], which carried the
old name *acetic acid* — and the everyday face of the family is
**[[vinegar]]**: an aqueous solution containing ethanoic acid
(4.37C). The sour taste of vinegar and the sharpness of the
family are the same chemistry at different dilutions.

The family is built by oxidation. A carboxylic acid forms when
its corresponding alcohol is oxidised — leave ethanol exposed to
air and its resident microorganisms, or oxidise it deliberately
with potassium dichromate(VI) in dilute sulfuric acid, and the
–OH-bearing carbon climbs one rung up the oxidation ladder to
–COOH (Sections 4–5 of the alcohols lesson). The rung above it
on that ladder does not exist at IGCSE: the carboxylic acids
are where the oxidation story of the alcohols stops.

:::prior-knowledge
Before this lesson you should be fluent with three ideas from
the alcohols lesson (u4:c26): the –OH functional group and how
it is drawn; the two oxidation routes to ethanoic acid — microbial
(slow, in the air) and laboratory (dichromate(VI), orange to
green); and the naming habit of counting carbons in the chain.
If any of those wobble, repair them first — this family is the
alcohols' oxidation product, and every name here is the alcohol
name with its ending swapped.
:::

:::did-you-know
The family's one-carbon member, methanoic acid, is old enough to
have its own delivery system: the sting of a stinging nettle is
a dose of a dilute methanoic-acid solution injected through the
plant's hollow hairs. The traditional remedy — rubbing a leaf of
the dock plant that tends to grow nearby — is folk chemistry,
but the acid in the sting is this lesson's smallest acid.
:::

:::cross-curricular
Vinegar is economics and history in a bottle. Before
refrigeration, the acetic acid in vinegar was the world's main
food preservative — pickling works because bacteria cannot
survive the low pH, so harvests survived winters. Different
civilisations fermented whatever they grew: wine into wine
vinegar, malt into malt vinegar, rice into rice vinegar. One
molecule — ethanoic acid — supporting food security across
every grain-growing culture on Earth.
:::

:::exam-hint
Statement 4.34C says *know* the functional group — this is a
recall item, not a derivation. Reproduce the arrangement from
memory: carbonyl C=O **and** hydroxyl O–H on the same carbon,
written –COOH. The classic lost mark is writing –OH and calling
it the carboxylic-acid group; that is the alcohol group. The
carboxyl group has the double-bonded oxygen, and drawing or
writing it earns the mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Ethanoic acid contains –OH, so it is an alcohol." The hydroxyl
part of –COOH is bonded to a carbon that also carries the C=O —
and that second oxygen changes the family, the name and the
reactions entirely. Functional groups are judged by their whole
arrangement, not by spotting one fragment inside them. The same
O–H appears in both families; the C=O is what makes this one
an acid.
:::

:::
