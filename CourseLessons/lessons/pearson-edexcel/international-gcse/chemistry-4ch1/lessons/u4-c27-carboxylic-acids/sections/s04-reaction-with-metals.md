The Unit 2 rule still stands: a metal above hydrogen in the
[[reactivity-series]] reacts with a dilute acid to give a
[[salt]] and hydrogen (u2:c14, u2:c16). Dilute ethanoic acid
obeys it exactly as hydrochloric acid does — only more slowly,
which is the [[weak-acid]] behaviour of the last section showing
through. The new thing is not the reaction type; it is the salt,
because every carboxylic acid has its own family of salts built
on the **ethanoate** ion.

Drop magnesium ribbon into dilute ethanoic acid and it fizzes
vigorously: hydrogen gas streams off and the solid disappears
into a colourless solution of **magnesium ethanoate**:

Mg(s) + 2CH₃COOH(aq) → (CH₃COO)₂Mg(aq) + H₂(g)

That formula deserves a slow read, because the salt formulae are
where students reliably stumble. Magnesium ethanoate is an
[[ionic-compound]]: it contains magnesium ions, Mg²⁺, and
ethanoate ions, CH₃COO⁻. Each [[ethanoate-ion]] carries a single
negative charge, so a doubly-charged magnesium ion needs **two**
of them to balance the charges — hence the subscript 2 and hence
the 2 in front of the acid in the equation. The metal is written
after the ethanoate groups because that order reflects the
structure: the positive magnesium ion is attracted to the
negative charge sitting on the oxygen atoms of the ethanoate
ions.

The ethanoate ion itself is worth a picture in words: a
CH₃–C(=O) unit with the spare oxygen carrying the negative
charge — written CH₃COO⁻. It is the carboxylic-acid molecule
with its acidic hydrogen removed; the O–H proton is what the
metal takes away, and the electron it leaves behind becomes the
ion's charge. Draw it as the carboxyl group minus one H, with
a minus sign on the single-bonded oxygen.

Other metals behave exactly as the reactivity-series predicts.
Sodium is dangerously reactive with the acid — do not try it
outside a demonstration. Zinc produces bubbles of hydrogen
noticeably more slowly than it does with hydrochloric acid, a
direct visual of the weak-acid speed difference. Metals below
hydrogen in the series — copper, silver — do not react at all.
Wherever a reaction does take place, a metal ethanoate forms
alongside the hydrogen: zinc ethanoate is (CH₃COO)₂Zn, built by
the same two-ethanoates-per-ion arithmetic.

:::key-point
Every metal-ethanoate formula is a charge-balance exercise, and
the ethanoate ion is always 1−. So the metal's ion charge sets
the subscript: Mg²⁺ takes two ethanoates, (CH₃COO)₂Mg; Na⁺ takes
one, CH₃COONa; Al³⁺ takes three, (CH₃COO)₃Al. Work the charges
first and the formula writes itself — never balance metal
ethanoates by trial and error.
:::

:::exam-hint
Salt-formula items are marked on the ion arithmetic, so show it:
Mg²⁺ + 2CH₃COO⁻ → (CH₃COO)₂Mg. The equation mark for
2CH₃COOH(aq) in front of the arrow is earned by the same
arithmetic — two acid molecules donate two protons to build the
two ethanoate ions. State symbols complete the mark: (s), (aq),
(g) as in the magnesium equation above.
:::

:::misconception
"Ethanoic acid plus magnesium gives CH₃COOMg." That formula
balances nothing — one 1− ion cannot neutralise one 2+ ion. The
error is forgetting that the ethanoate ion is a single-charge
unit; the fix is writing the two ions first and subscripting
after. The same discipline separates zinc ethanoate
((CH₃COO)₂Zn, correct) from ZnCOO (not a formula at all).
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the magnesium-plus-ethanoic equation with state
symbols and explain where the 2s come from? Can you build the
formula of sodium ethanoate, zinc ethanoate and magnesium
ethanoate from ion charges? Can you predict what copper does in
dilute ethanoic acid and why? Charges first, formula second —
and this section is yours.
:::
