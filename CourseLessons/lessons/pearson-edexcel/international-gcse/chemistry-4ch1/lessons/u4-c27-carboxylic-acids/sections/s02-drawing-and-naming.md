Statement 4.35C narrows the task to the four simplest members of
the [[homologous-series]] — [[methanoic-acid]], ethanoic,
propanoic and butanoic acid — and asks for the three levels of
formula plus the name of each. The naming engine is the same one the alkanes,
alkenes and alcohols used: meth- means one carbon, eth- two,
prop- three, but- four, and the acid ending *-anoic acid* replaces
the alcohol's *-anol*.

| acid | molecular formula | structural formula |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|
| methanoic acid | CH₂O₂ | HCOOH |
| ethanoic acid | C₂H₄O₂ | CH₃COOH |
| propanoic acid | C₃H₆O₂ | CH₃CH₂COOH |
| butanoic acid | C₄H₈O₂ | CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH |

The [[molecular-formula]] counts each element's atoms; the
[[structural-formula]] shows the order of the atoms unambiguously
— every member ends in COOH with the hydrogen-bearing chain
before it. The molecular formulae share the pattern CnH2nO2:
each member is its corresponding alcohol with one extra oxygen
atom — the oxidation that builds the carboxyl group. Methanol
CH₄O oxidised gives methanoic acid CH₂O₂; ethanol C₂H₆O gives
ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂. The carbon count never changes on the way
up.

The [[displayed-formula]] level is where the discipline lives.
Every bond must be drawn — the chain as C–C and C–H lines, and
the carboxyl end with the C=O double bond and the O–H single
bond shown explicitly. Methanoic acid's displayed formula is
H–C(=O)–O–H with both bonds on the same carbon visible. The
condensed COOH is a convenience for equations; the drawn-out
arrangement is what *displayed* means.

:::key-point
When naming a carboxylic acid, count the **total** number of
carbon atoms — including the one inside the –COOH group. Propanoic
acid has three carbons in total, which is why it starts with
*prop*: the two chain carbons plus the carboxyl carbon. Because
the –COOH group is always on the end carbon, there is no need
to insert a number into the name to locate the functional group
— no locant exists anywhere in this family.
:::

:::exam-hint
If an item asks for a displayed formula, draw all the bonding in
the COOH group — the C=O and the O–H both explicit. If an item
asks for the formula inside an *equation*, the condensed form is
normal and correct: CH₃COOH. Reading the demand curve of the
question — drawing item versus equation item — tells you which
level to produce, and mixing them up is the standard lost mark
here.
:::

:::did-you-know
The old name of ethanoic acid, *acetic*, comes from *acetum*,
Latin for vinegar — the Romans named the family's famous member
after its most famous solution. The name survives in English
words like *acetate* (the ethanoate ion's old name) and in
*nail-varnish remover*, whose solvent was once called acetone
for the same reasons.
:::

:::misconception
"Butanoic acid has four carbons in its chain." No — butanoic
acid has four carbons **in total**, three in the chain plus the
carboxyl carbon. Students who count only the CH₃CH₂CH₂– part
write butanoic acid as having three carbons and then mis-draw
the formula. The carboxyl carbon is a real carbon atom: it sits
in the count, it sits in the molecular formula (the C of COOH),
and it sits in the displayed drawing with its C=O bond.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write molecular, structural and displayed formulae for
all four members up to four carbons, with the COOH end drawn
out? Can you explain why propanoic acid carries *prop* and why
no locant number is ever needed? Can you state how many carbons
each molecular formula's n counts? Three yeses and 4.35C is
banked.
:::
