This section is the *why* behind the [[weak-acid]] label — it runs
one step beyond the statement list, which is exactly why the
family's acid behaviour has been described so far only in
outcomes. The distinction is about **[[dissociation]]**: how
completely an acid splits into ions when it dissolves in water.

Ethanoic acid is a weak acid. The acids met earlier in the
course — hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid — are **[[strong-acid]]s**.
The word measures ionisation, and nothing else. A strong acid
dissociates completely in water: a solution of hydrochloric acid
contains mostly H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions and hardly any intact HCl
molecules at all. A weak acid dissociates only partially: for
ethanoic acid, only about 1 molecule in every 75 splits into H⁺
and CH₃COO⁻ — the rest stay whole, sitting in the solution
dissolved but not ionised.

That single sentence explains every observation in this chapter.
Same concentration of ethanoic acid and hydrochloric acid: the
ethanoic solution holds far fewer H⁺ ions per litre, so it reacts
with metals or metal carbonates much more slowly (fewer protons
available to collide per second), and its pH sits higher. The
acid still reacts — the reactions are just protracted, because
each dissociated H⁺ that reacts nudges the equilibrium of the
remaining molecules to dissociate a little more. Weak acids do
the whole of an acid's job; they do it at a lower H⁺ concentration.

:::key-point
Strength and concentration are independent variables. Strength
(dissociation: completely vs partially into ions) is about the
molecules themselves; concentration (dilute vs concentrated) is
about how much acid is dissolved per unit volume. A dilute
solution of a strong acid and a concentrated solution of a weak
acid can hold similar H⁺ concentrations — the words cross-cut,
and conflating them is the single most common misconception in
acids chemistry.
:::

:::exam-hint
"Explain why ethanoic acid reacts more slowly with magnesium
than hydrochloric acid of the same concentration" is a two-mark
classic. Mark 1: ethanoic acid is weak / partially dissociated /
only about 1 in 75 molecules ionise. Mark 2: so the H⁺(aq)
concentration is lower / fewer hydrogen ions per unit volume.
Answers through concentration ("it is more dilute") score nothing
— the question fixes the concentration identical; the answer
must route through dissociation.
:::

:::misconception
"Strong acids are concentrated acids, weak acids are dilute
acids." Whether an acid is strong or weak has nothing to do with
its concentration. Concentrated hydrochloric acid and dilute
hydrochloric acid are both strong acids — both dissociate
completely; they differ only in how much of them is dissolved.
Concentrated ethanoic acid (glacial acetic acid) is still a weak
acid — still only partially ionised, whatever its concentration.
Four combinations exist, and every one of them is real.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you define the strong/weak distinction through dissociation
and quote the 1-in-75 picture for ethanoic acid? Can you explain
the slower reaction rate through H⁺ concentration? Can you pair
the four combinations (strong/weak × concentrated/dilute) with a
real example of each? Then the label "weak" is fully earned and
the carbonates are next.
:::
