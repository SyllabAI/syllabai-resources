Iron is the world's most important alloy base. There are many alloys
of iron containing various proportions of carbon and sometimes other
metals, and an alloy of iron with carbon is called **[[steel]]**. The
course needs three kinds, distinguished almost entirely by carbon
percentage — a perfect demonstration that alloy recipes are tunable,
not fixed.

**Mild steel** — low-carbon steel — contains up to about 0.25%
carbon. That small addition increases the hardness and strength of
the iron usefully while leaving the metal workable: **[[mild-steel]]**
is strong, and can be easily hammered into various shapes (**[[malleable]]**)
and drawn into wires (**[[ductile]]**). Those properties buy it a
huge workload — nails, car bodies, ship building, girders and bridges
among many others. Its weaknesses are equally specific: it rusts when
exposed to oxygen and water (the enemy from Chapter 14, u2:c14), and
it is about three times denser than aluminium. That density figure is
why some car bodies are made from aluminium instead: the body will
not rust, and because the car is lighter, less fuel has to be used —
two advantages against a cost, which is exactly how materials
engineers argue.

**High-carbon steel** contains about 0.6–1.2% carbon (the amounts
are variable), and usually small amounts of manganese as well. More
carbon makes the steel harder and more resistant to wear than mild
steel — but also more **brittle**: not as malleable and ductile. Hard
and wear-resistant is the spec sheet of a cutting tool, and that is
its home: **[[high-carbon-steel]]** is used for cutting tools. The
malleability that mild steel spends on girders is traded away for an
edge that stays sharp.

**Stainless steel** is iron alloyed with chromium and often
nickel, and its magic is borrowed chemistry: chromium forms a strong
oxide layer in the same way aluminium does, and that oxide layer
protects the iron underneath too, so **[[stainless-steel]]** is very
resistant to corrosion — it stays "stainless" by self-coating,
exactly like the aluminium of the previous section. Uses follow
corrosion resistance: kitchen sinks, saucepans, knives and forks and
gardening tools, plus major industrial service in brewing, dairy and
chemical work, where corrosion-resistant vessels are essential.
Significantly more expensive than mild steel, it is bought only where
the corrosion bill justifies it.

| Type of steel | Iron mixed with | Some uses |
|---|---|---|
| mild steel | up to 0.25% carbon | nails, car bodies, ship building, girders |
| high-carbon steel | 0.6–1.2% carbon | cutting tools, masonry nails |
| stainless steel | chromium (and nickel) | cutlery, cooking utensils, kitchen sinks |

Copper completes the property-to-use catalogue, and every entry is an
examination favourite:

| Use | Property |
|---|---|
| electrical wires | very good conductor of electricity and ductile |
| pots and pans | very good conductor of heat, very unreactive and malleable |
| water pipes | unreactive — does not react with hot or cold water — and malleable |
| surfaces in hospitals | antimicrobial properties and malleable |

:::cross-curricular
This section is engineering economics in miniature. The car-body
decision between mild steel and aluminium weighs rust resistance,
density (fuel burn over the vehicle's life) and unit cost — a
three-factor trade-off argued exactly as a design engineer would.
The stainless-steel industry case (brewing, dairy, chemical plant)
shows corrosion chemistry setting capital budgets: vessels that
survive their contents are worth a premium alloy. Even the coinage
choice of cupronickel is monetary policy meeting metallurgy —
durability and cost per coin, struck billions of times.
:::

:::exam-hint
Steel-comparison questions are answered with *paired* properties:
mild steel for car bodies because strong, malleable and cheaper than
the alternatives; high-carbon for tools because harder and
wear-resistant, accepting brittleness; stainless where corrosion
resistance is worth the premium, with the chromium-oxide mechanism
stated. Two further marks hide in the details: density (mild steel
about three times denser than aluminium — the fuel argument) and
rusting (mild steel's disadvantage that stainless answers). Quote the
carbon percentages when asked to identify a steel — 0.25% versus
0.6–1.2% separates mild from high-carbon on sight.
:::

:::misconception
"Stainless steel never rusts because chromium is unreactive." The
chromium is *very* reactive with oxygen — that is the entire trick. It
reacts instantly to build the same kind of thin, strong oxide layer
that aluminium builds, and the layer seals the iron away from oxygen
and water. Unreactive metals resist corrosion by not reacting;
stainless steel resists corrosion by reacting first, once, at the
surface. Two different mechanisms with the same visible result —
confusing them loses the mechanism mark.
:::

:::summary
In one page: most metals are extracted from ores in the Earth's
crust; only unreactive metals occur uncombined (2.22C). Extraction
method follows the reactivity series — carbon reduction below carbon
(iron, with carbon monoxide as the furnace's working reducing agent),
electrolysis above zinc (aluminium in molten cryolite at around
1000 °C, cathode Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al, anode 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻), reactive-metal
reduction for the awkward few such as titanium (2.23C) — and you
reason through any process from the two costs (energy, reducing
agent) plus the redox labels (2.24C). Uses follow properties:
aluminium (low density, conducts, self-protecting oxide layer) in
planes, cables and pans; copper (conducts, unreactive, malleable,
antimicrobial) in wires, pipes, pans and hospital surfaces; the three
steels — mild (0.25% C, strong and workable, rusts), high-carbon
(0.6–1.2% C, hard and brittle, tools), stainless (chromium, nickel;
self-protecting) (2.25C). An alloy is a metal mixed with one or more
other elements, usually metals or carbon (2.26C), and alloys are
harder because differently sized atoms break the regular lattice so
the layers of ions cannot slide (2.27C).
:::
