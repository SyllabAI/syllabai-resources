Pure metals are rarely what products are made of — most metal objects
around you are mixtures, and the mixture is the point. An
**[[alloy]]** is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements,
usually other metals or carbon. Two names carry the definition: **[[brass]]**
is a mixture of copper and zinc, and steel is an alloy of iron with
carbon. Both are made the same way — the elements are melted together
and mixed — and both behave differently from their pure parents.

The difference shows up as hardness, and the reason is pure lattice
geometry. Recall the metallic model from Chapter 9 (u1:c09): a pure
metal is a regular lattice of identical positive ions surrounded by
delocalised electrons, and that regularity is exactly what makes pure
metals soft. When a force is applied, whole planes of ions can slide
over one another and settle into new positions — the metal bends or
dents rather than shatters. Picture a neatly stacked rack of
identical balls: rows slide easily because every ball lines up with
the next.

Now stir differently sized atoms into the lattice, which is what
making an alloy does. In an alloy, the different metals or elements
have slightly differently sized atoms, so the regular arrangement
breaks up. The rows no longer line up; a sliding plane hits a
misfit atom almost immediately and jams. It becomes much more
difficult for the layers of ions to slide over each other — and that
is the whole answer to why alloys are harder than the pure metals
they are made from. The delocalised electrons are still there (the
alloy still conducts, still shines), but the slip-planes are wrecked.

Keep the definition tight as well as the explanation. "A mixture of a
metal and one or more elements, usually other metals or carbon" is
the exact wording the mark scheme wants: the base must be a *metal*,
the added elements are *usually* other metals or carbon — carbon in
the steels is the standard example of the exception — and "mixture"
matters because no chemical bonds rearrange; it is a blended
structure, not a compound.

:::exam-hint
The alloy-hardness question is marked in a fixed three-step chain:
(1) an alloy contains atoms of different sizes; (2) the regular
lattice arrangement is disrupted, so the layers cannot slide over
each other easily; (3) therefore the alloy is harder than the pure
metal. Write all three steps — answers that jump from "different
atoms" straight to "harder" drop the middle, mechanism mark every
time. And never explain hardness with the electrons: the sliding
layers of *ions* are the moving parts.
:::

:::misconception
"Alloys are compounds, because the elements are chemically bonded in a
fixed ratio." An alloy is a *mixture* — the added atoms sit in the
metal's lattice without forming new compounds, and their proportions
can be varied continuously (mild and high-carbon steels differ only in
carbon percentage, as the next sections show). Compounds have fixed
formulae and rearranged bonding; alloys have tunable recipes and an
unchanged metallic lattice with some atoms swapped in.
:::
