Brass and steel opened the alloy story; three more named alloys
complete the catalogue the exam expects. **Bronze** is a mixture
of copper and tin — humanity's oldest engineered alloy, and still the
name given to that copper-tin family today. **Stainless steel**
is an alloy of iron with chromium and usually nickel: the chromium
contribution is what makes it "stainless", a story told properly in
the steel section. And **[[cupronickel]]** — the mixture of copper
and nickel — is usually used to make "silver" coins: the coinage in
your pocket that looks like silver contains no silver at all, just a
copper-nickel blend tuned to look and wear like it.

Each of these earns its place by combining its parents' virtues. The
copper in bronze and cupronickel contributes workability and
corrosion resistance; the tin hardens the **[[bronze]]**, the nickel toughens
and silvers the coinage. Stainless steel is the same design logic
applied to iron, whose rusting habit is exactly the weakness
**[[stainless-steel]]** patches. Notice the pattern across every alloy met
so far — brass, bronze, cupronickel, the steels: take a useful but
flawed pure metal, dissolve in a second element, and repair a specific
weakness. That design pattern is the "explain the use" objective in
miniature, and the steel kinds are where it is examined hardest.

:::exam-hint
Alloy-composition questions are recall-for-one-mark items: bronze is
copper *and tin*; stainless steel is iron *with chromium (and usually
nickel)*; cupronickel is copper *and nickel* and is the coinage
alloy; brass is copper *and zinc*. The commonest lost mark is writing
"bronze is a mixture of metals" — true but unscored. Name the
elements, and for stainless steel remember the chromium is the
anti-rust ingredient the examiner asks about next.
:::

:::misconception
"'Silver' coins contain silver, because that is what they are called."
The silvery colour comes from the cupronickel blend — copper and
nickel are both silvery-grey metals, and mixed they mimic silver
closely enough for everyday coinage. Coinage metals are chosen for
durability, wear resistance and modest cost, not for precious-metal
content. Treat the name as appearance, never as composition.
:::

:::quick-check
Question-bank join: spec 2.25C/2.26C items. From memory, give the
composition of bronze, stainless steel and cupronickel, and say which
one lives in your pocket and why it is there. Then check the design
pattern: for each alloy, name the weakness of the base metal that the
added element repairs. If stainless steel's answer is "rusting", you
are ready for the steel section.
:::
