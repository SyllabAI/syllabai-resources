Extraction always ends the same way: the metal's compound is turned
into the metal itself. In the oxide ores that dominate this chapter,
that means removing oxygen — and removing oxygen is
**[[reduction]]**. In the electron picture it means the same thing
from the other side: the metal in an ore exists as positive ions, and
to produce the neutral metal those ions must gain electrons. Reduction,
twice over, in both vocabularies of Chapter 14 (u2:c14).

That single sentence is the hinge of the whole topic, so it is worth
restating slowly. Iron(III) oxide contains Fe³⁺ ions; aluminium oxide
contains Al³⁺ ions. Extraction supplies electrons to those ions —
with carbon as the electron donor where possible, or with electricity
where not — and the neutral metal comes out. Every method you meet in
this chapter is just a different answer to one question: *what will
force electrons onto this metal ion?* How expensive that answer is
depends on how reactive the metal is, which is the next section's
subject.

The second half of the chapter turns from *getting* metals to *using*
them, and its objectives are worth stating in full now. You must be
able to explain the uses of aluminium, copper, iron and steel in terms
of their properties, where the steel types are limited to three:
low-carbon (mild) steel, high-carbon steel and stainless steel. You
must know that an alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other
elements, usually other metals or carbon. And you must be able to
explain why alloys are harder than the pure metals they are made from
— an argument conducted entirely in the lattice language of
Chapter 9 (u1:c09).

If the oxidation/reduction vocabulary feels shaky, go back and re-read
Chapter 14 (u2:c14) before continuing: extraction questions are redox
questions, and the examiner's mark scheme uses those exact words.

:::exam-hint
The three objectives carried here are the "comment on" and "explain
the uses" family — the part of the chapter where examiners give you a
process or a metal you may never have met and expect you to reason
from principles. Detailed knowledge of any specific extraction plant
is *not* required. What earns the marks: spot the reduction, name the
reducing agent, link a property (density, conductivity, strength,
corrosion resistance) to a use, and describe the alloy lattice when
hardness is asked. Practise reading unfamiliar information calmly —
that skill *is* the objective.
:::

:::misconception
"Extracting copper from copper oxide is oxidation, because oxygen is
involved." The presence of oxygen in the *reactant* does not make the
process oxidation — watch what happens *to* the metal compound: it
*loses* oxygen, so the metal oxide is reduced. It is the reducing
agent (carbon, say) that is oxidised, by gaining the oxygen the metal
gave up. Direction of oxygen travel decides the labels, and the metal
always travels out of the ore by reduction.
:::
