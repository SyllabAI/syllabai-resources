How a metal is extracted depends, to a large extent, on its position in
the [[reactivity-series]] — because the position decides which
chemical or electrical process can do the reduction, and every process
costs money. A manufacturer wants the cheapest method that works, and
two costs dominate: the cost of **energy** and the cost of the
**reducing agent**. Those two numbers, run against the reactivity
series, explain the entire map of extraction methods.

For a metal below carbon, heating the oxide with carbon wins on both
counts: coke is cheap, and a furnace is cheaper than an electrolysis
hall. The extraction of iron is the flagship example. A main ore of
iron contains a high percentage of iron(III) oxide, and carbon strips
the oxygen away when heated:

Fe₂O₃(s) + 3C(s) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO(g)

Read the equation as the redox story it is. Carbon is above iron in
the series, so it takes the oxygen for itself: the iron(III) oxide is
**reduced** to iron, the carbon is **oxidised** to carbon monoxide,
and the carbon is therefore the [[reducing-agent]] — the substance
that removes the oxygen (and, in electron terms, supplies the
electrons) while being oxidised itself.

The real industrial process runs in a [[blast-furnace]], and it is a
step more subtle than the simple equation above. Other reactions occur
inside the furnace, and the main reducing agent in practice is
actually carbon monoxide, formed from the coke, which then carries the
oxygen away from the iron oxide:

Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g)

Same redox bookkeeping, same product: the oxide loses oxygen, the
reducing agent gains it. That is as far as the course goes — the full
internal choreography of the furnace is not required.

:::exam-hint
In the exam you will never be asked to reproduce the blast furnace in
detail — the syllabus says detailed knowledge of specific extraction
processes is not required. What you must be able to do is *comment*
on a process you are handed: identify what is reduced, what is
oxidised, which substance is the reducing agent, and why the method
suits the metal's reactivity. Also expect the two-cost question:
extraction choices come down to the cost of energy and the cost of the
reducing agent — quoting either one alone loses the pairing mark.
:::

:::misconception
"Carbon reduces iron(III) oxide because carbon is hotter than the
furnace, or because carbon is heavier." The reduction happens because
carbon is *more reactive* than iron — higher in the reactivity series
— so carbon out-competes iron for the oxygen. Temperature is the
condition that lets the reaction proceed at a useful rate, not the
cause of the oxygen transfer. Reactivity ranking, not heat alone,
decides who takes the oxygen.
:::
