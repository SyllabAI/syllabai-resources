Above zinc in the reactivity series, carbon's reducing power runs out.
Ores of metals higher than zinc cannot be reduced by carbon at
reasonable temperatures: those metals are more reactive than carbon,
so carbon simply cannot take their oxygen away. For potassium,
sodium, calcium, magnesium and aluminium, the winning method is
**electrolysis** — the method that never fails, because electrons are
supplied directly by the power supply rather than borrowed from a
reactive element. Iron and all other metals *could* also be extracted
by electrolysis, but the process is so expensive that it is used only
when it has to be.

Aluminium is the case to know. It is extracted by the electrolysis of
aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, dissolved in a molten salt called
**[[cryolite]]**. The dissolved oxide behaves exactly like the molten
electrolytes of Chapter 10 (u1:c10): aluminium ions migrate to the
cathode and are discharged as metal; oxide ions migrate to the anode
and are discharged as oxygen gas. The two half-equations carry the
whole story:

At the cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (**[[reduction]]**)

At the anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (**[[oxidation]]**)

Molten aluminium collects at the bottom of the cell.

:::key-point
The cryolite is there to make the process economical. Aluminium oxide
melts at over 2000 °C — far too hot to run an electrolysis cell. Dissolved
in molten cryolite, the whole process runs at around 1000 °C instead.
That saves an enormous amount of energy, and energy is money: the
mixture, not the oxide alone, is what makes aluminium extraction
commercially possible at all.
:::

Even so, the process demands huge amounts of electricity, which makes
aluminium expensive. The price is a redox consequence: aluminium has
to be extracted by electrolysis, while iron is simply heated with
carbon — so aluminium costs much more than iron even though aluminium
is *more abundant* in the Earth's crust. Abundance never sets the
price; the reactivity series, through the extraction it forces, does.

A few awkward metals need a third route. Titanium, for example, is
extracted by heating its compound with a more reactive metal —
effectively buying the reduction from a metal that outranks titanium.
That is expensive too, and for a built-in reason: the more reactive
metal itself first had to be extracted by an expensive process, and
its cost is baked into the titanium's.

:::exam-hint
The aluminium question is a guaranteed Paper 2C shape. The full-mark
chain: name the electrolyte (aluminium oxide dissolved in molten
cryolite); state cryolite's job (lowers the operating temperature from
over 2000 °C to around 1000 °C, saving energy); give both
half-equations with the correct charges, electrons and gas; and label
cathode as reduction, anode as oxidation. For titanium-style
processes, the expected comment is the expense chain — a more reactive
metal does the reducing, and that metal had to be expensively
extracted itself.
:::

:::misconception
"Cryolite is a catalyst for the electrolysis." Cryolite changes no
reaction rate and is not consumed as a catalyst would be — it is a
solvent that dissolves the aluminium oxide so the ions can move at a
*lower temperature*. The saving is thermodynamic bookkeeping (a
1000 °C cell instead of a 2000 °C melt), not catalysis. Candidates who
write "catalyst" hand the examiner an easy mark to refuse.
:::
