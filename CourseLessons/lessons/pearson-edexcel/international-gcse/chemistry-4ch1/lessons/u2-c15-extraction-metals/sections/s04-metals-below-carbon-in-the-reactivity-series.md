Take a metal oxide — iron(III) oxide, say — and ask what has to happen
to leave pure metal behind. The oxygen must go. **Reduction** is
the name for removal of oxygen, so obtaining a metal from its oxide is
always a reduction of the metal compound. That is the oxygen-language
statement; here is the same fact in electron language, and you need
both for the exam.

Metals exist as positive ions in their ionic compounds: iron(III)
oxide is Fe³⁺ and O²⁻ packed in a lattice, with no neutral iron atoms
anywhere. To produce the metal, each positive ion must be supplied
with enough electrons to balance its charge — Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe.
Addition of electrons is also called [[reduction]], so the two definitions
agree perfectly: removing oxygen from the oxide *is* giving electrons
back to the metal ion. The extracted metal is simply the ore's ions
re-clothed in their electrons.

This section covers the metals sitting *below* carbon in the
[[reactivity-series]] — zinc, iron, copper and the rest. The position
matters because carbon can only do the reducing job for metals it is
more reactive than: carbon takes oxygen from an oxide only if carbon
outranks the metal in the series. For everything below carbon, that
condition holds, and heating the oxide with carbon is the cheap route
to the metal. How that plays out for iron — and why aluminium needs
something far more aggressive — is the next section's economics.

:::exam-hint
Definition questions on reduction appear in extraction contexts every
year, and the mark scheme accepts either wording: "loss of oxygen" or
"gain of electrons". The safest answers give both, matched to the
reaction in front of you: the metal oxide is reduced because it loses
oxygen — and, in electron terms, because the metal ion gains
electrons. If asked about the metal ion specifically, the electron
version is the one that scores.
:::

:::misconception
"Reduction means the metal gets smaller or lighter." Reduction has
nothing to do with size — the word refers to the *oxide being reduced
back to the metal*, the ore's mass of oxygen leaving. Iron(III) oxide reduces to rather less iron, but the chemical meaning
is oxygen loss and electron gain, not shrinkage. Anchoring the word to
oxygen-and-electrons, rather than to everyday "reducing" imagery,
keeps every extraction answer on track.
:::
