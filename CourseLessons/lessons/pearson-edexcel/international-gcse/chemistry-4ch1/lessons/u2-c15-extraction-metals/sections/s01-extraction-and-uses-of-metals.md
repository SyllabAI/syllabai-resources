Look around: aircraft overhead, cables strung between pylons, rails,
reinforcing bars, coins in your pocket. Metals carry modern life — and
almost every one of them began as rock dug out of the ground. Very few
occur as the pure element ready to use. The rest are locked into
compounds in the Earth's crust, and the branch of chemistry that wins
them back is **extraction**: redox reactions run on an industrial
scale, using exactly the [[reactivity-series]] logic you built in
Chapter 14 (u2:c14).

One idea organises the whole chapter. A metal's position in the
reactivity series decides how hard it is to extract — and therefore
how it is extracted, and roughly what it costs. Gold sits so low that
it can be found [[native]], as the uncombined element, glinting in a
pan; iron needs a furnace fed by carbon; aluminium demands huge
quantities of electricity. Extraction method, price and even the
metal's everyday job all trace back to that one ranking.

The chapter runs in two halves. First, extraction: what an [[ore]] is,
why carbon can strip oxygen from some metal oxides but not others, why
electrolysis takes over above zinc, and how to reason your way through
an extraction process you have never seen. Then the payoff: what the
extracted metals are actually *for* — the uses of aluminium, copper,
iron and steel, the alloy families (brass, bronze, the steels), and
why hammering other elements into a metal makes it harder. Each half
is examined every year in Paper 2C, and the command word is usually
"explain": explain the method from the position, explain the use from
the property, explain the hardness from the structure.

:::prior-knowledge
Four earlier chapters load this one. From Chapter 14 (u2:c14) you need
the reactivity series itself — especially carbon's reference position
— and the redox vocabulary: oxidation as gain of oxygen or loss of
electrons, reduction as the reverse, and the agent terms. From
Chapter 9 (u1:c09) you need metallic structure: a regular lattice of
positive ions surrounded by delocalised electrons, because the
explanation of alloy hardness is pure lattice reasoning. From
Chapter 10 (u1:c10) you need electrolysis of molten ionic compounds —
electrodes, half-equations and which electrode attracts which ion —
because that is literally how aluminium is won. Equations throughout
assume the balancing and state-symbol skills of Chapter 5 (u1:c05).
:::

:::misconception
"Extracting a metal means separating it from the rock physically —
like sieving sand from gravel." For most metals that is impossible,
because the metal is not sitting in the rock as metal: it is chemically
combined inside mineral compounds such as oxides or sulfides. Winning
the metal is a *chemical* reduction — removing oxygen or adding
electrons — not a physical separation. Physical methods alone only
ever work for the unreactive metals that occur native, such as gold.
:::
