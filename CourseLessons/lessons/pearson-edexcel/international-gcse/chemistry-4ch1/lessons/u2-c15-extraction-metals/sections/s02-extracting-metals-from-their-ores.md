The Earth's crust holds most metals in combination with other
elements — as oxides, sulfides, carbonates and more. An individual
metal compound found in rock is a **[[mineral]]**. Two
iron-containing minerals make the idea concrete: one is iron sulfide,
FeS₂, and another is an iron oxide with formula Fe₃O₄. In real rock
these minerals are never neat and pure; they are scattered through
unwanted material.

An **[[ore]]** is a rock that contains enough of a metal-containing
mineral to make extracting the metal worthwhile. That definition has
two halves and both matter: the metal must be *there*, and there must
be *enough of it* for the process to pay for itself. Most metals are
extracted from ores found in the Earth's crust — but not all. A few
very unreactive metals, gold most famously, are found **[[native]]**:
existing naturally as the uncombined element, no chemistry required to
free them. Silver and copper are sometimes found native too, though
much more rarely — their slight reactivity means nearly all of their
atoms have combined with something over geological time.

Many ores contain the metal as an oxide already, or as a compound that
converts easily to one. Metal sulfides are the easy case: heating the
sulfide in air — a process known as **[[roasting]]** — converts it to
the oxide. The conversion for the zinc
sulfide ore shows the pattern:

2ZnS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO₂(g)

Zinc sulfide gains oxygen; the sulfur leaves as sulfur dioxide gas.
Roasting is the standard bridge from sulfide ore
to oxide, because the oxide is the starting point for the extraction
methods of the next sections.

:::did-you-know
The price of a metal reflects its ore: how common the ore is, and how
hard the metal is to extract from it. A common ore plus an easy
carbon-reduction drives the price down; a rare ore plus an
electricity-hungry extraction drives it up. That is why the extraction
method and the market price tell the same story from two directions.
:::

:::exam-hint
You are not required to remember the names of the ores or minerals —
examiners supply any mineral formulae you need. What you *are*
expected to know is the vocabulary and the logic: ore as
worthwhile-enough rock, native as uncombined element, and the fact
that most metals must be extracted from ores in the crust while
unreactive metals may occur uncombined. A two-mark "what is an ore"
question wants both halves of the definition — enough mineral, worth
extracting.
:::

:::misconception
"Gold is found native because it is dense and heavy, so it settles out
of rock." Density has nothing to do with it — panning gold works only
*because* it is already uncombined metal. Gold occurs native because
it is so unreactive: it has never combined with oxygen or sulfur in
the crust. A reactive metal such as sodium cannot be found native at
any density, because it has all reacted long ago.
:::
