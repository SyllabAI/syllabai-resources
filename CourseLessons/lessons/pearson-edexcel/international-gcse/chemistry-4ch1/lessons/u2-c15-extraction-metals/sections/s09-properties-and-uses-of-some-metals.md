Aluminium's job list follows directly from four properties — and from
one honest weakness. Pure aluminium is not very strong, so
**aluminium alloys** are normally used instead: the metal can be
strengthened by adding other elements such as silicon, copper or
magnesium. This is the [[alloy]] logic of the last section doing
commercial work — keep aluminium's gifts, patch its softness.

The gifts: aluminium has a **low density** (about a third of steel's),
it is a **good conductor of electricity and of heat**, and it
**resists corrosion**. Each property buys a use. Planes are built
from aluminium alloys because low density is everything when something
must fly, and the alloys supply the strength pure aluminium lacks.
Overhead electricity cables carry aluminium — excellent conductivity
at low mass — and the cable design shows the alloy thinking again:
the aluminium is strengthened by a core of steel, marrying
conductivity to tensile strength in one cable. Pots and pans use the
other two gifts: heat conducted quickly to the food, and no corrosion
from anything cooked in the pan.

The corrosion resistance has a mechanism worth stating precisely,
because examiners ask for it. Aluminium reacts with oxygen in the air
instantly — but the product is a very thin, very strong layer of
aluminium oxide on the surface. The layer clings tight, is
impermeable, and prevents anything else reaching the metal underneath
and reacting with it. So the metal protects itself: the corrosion
product *is* the coating. Compare iron, whose rust flakes off and lets
attack continue — aluminium's oxide layer is the difference between
corrosion that stops and corrosion that spreads.

:::exam-hint
"Explain why aluminium is used for X" questions are marked
property-by-property: link *low density* to aircraft and cable
weight, *conducts electricity* to cables, *conducts heat* to
cookware, *resists corrosion* to outdoor and kitchen durability — and
where strength appears, credit the *alloying* (silicon, copper or
magnesium added; steel core in cables). The corrosion explanation
must mention the thin aluminium oxide layer and that it prevents
further reaction — "aluminium does not react" is the refused answer,
because it does, immediately and usefully.
:::

:::misconception
"Aluminium resists corrosion because it is unreactive." Aluminium is
actually near the *top* of the reactivity series — far more reactive
than iron. Its corrosion resistance comes from the behaviour of its
oxide product, not from any reluctance to react: it reacts so fast
with air that a protective oxide film seals the surface before
anything deeper is touched. Reactive metal, protective product — the
paradox resolves in the oxide layer, and stating both halves is the
full-mark answer.
:::
