The second oxidation needs no flame and no laboratory: air and
microbes do it. Ethanol is oxidised by the air **in the presence of
microbes** — bacteria or yeast — and the product is
**[[ethanoic-acid]]**, CH₃COOH. This is **[[microbial-oxidation]]**,
and you have almost certainly watched it happen: a bottle of wine
left open to the air turns sour. The French for sour wine is *vin
aigre*, which over the centuries has been distorted into
**vinegar** — the word on the label is a record of this reaction.

Ethanoic acid is the family's first look at the carboxylic acids —
a later chapter in this course (u4:c27) takes that family properly.
What matters here is the transformation: ethanol's –OH carbon gains
a bond to oxygen, and the –CH₂CH₃ end of the molecule becomes the
–COOH (carboxylic acid) group. Drawn as displayed formulae, ethanol
and ethanoic acid sit side by side with exactly two extra oxygen
bonds marking the oxidation.

Statement 4.31C lists this route in its own words — *reaction with
oxygen in the air to form ethanoic acid (microbial oxidation)* —
so learn the phrase "microbial oxidation" as the name of the route,
with wine-turning-to-vinegar as its everyday evidence.

:::did-you-know
The old name for ethanoic acid was **acetic acid** — from the same
Latin *acetum* (sour) that gives vinegar its logic in both
languages. When you meet "acetic" on old bottles or in older
textbooks, it is this molecule: CH₃COOH, the oxidation product of
ethanol, wearing its historical name.
:::

:::exam-hint
For "ethanol is oxidised by the air", the mark scheme wants the
microbe credited: **oxidised by oxygen in the air with the help of
microorganisms (bacteria or yeast) to form ethanoic acid,
CH₃COOH**. Writing only "it turns to vinegar" describes the
evidence but misses the chemistry — name the product and the
agents (air + microbes), and the marks follow.
:::

:::misconception
"Wine turns sour because air oxidises the ethanol directly, no
living help needed." The air supplies the oxidant, but the
reaction needs the microbes' help — bacteria or yeast are the
workers, which is why the route is named *microbial* oxidation.
Sterile conditions slow it dramatically; the open bottle goes
sour because the microbial workforce is everywhere.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you name the second oxidation route in 4.31C's own words? Can
you write the formula of ethanoic acid and say which family it
belongs to? Can you tell the wine-to-vinegar story with the
chemistry in it? Three yeses and the gentlest oxidation is done.
:::
