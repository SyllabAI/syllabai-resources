Two routes, one product — and the comparison is the examinable
part. The table sets fermentation against the hydration of ethene
across five axes; learn it row by row, because "compare the two
methods" items pull directly from it.

| | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Use of resources | Uses **renewable** resources: sugar beet or sugar cane, corn and other starchy materials | Uses **finite, non-renewable** resources: once all the oil has been consumed, there won't be any more |
| Type of process | A **batch process**: everything is mixed together in a reaction vessel and left for several days; that batch is then removed and a new reaction is set up. Inefficient | A **continuous flow process**: a stream of reactants is constantly passed over the catalyst. More efficient than a batch process |
| Rate of reaction | Slow, taking several days for each batch | Quick |
| Quality of product | Produces **very impure** ethanol (capped at ~15% by the yeast) which needs further processing by fractional distillation | Produces **much purer** ethanol |
| Reaction conditions | Gentle temperatures and atmospheric pressure | High temperatures and pressures, requiring a high input of energy |

The vocabulary is itself examinable: a **[[batch-process]]** runs
discrete lots — mix, wait days, empty, repeat — while a
**[[continuous-process]]** flows reactants over the catalyst
without stopping, which is why it wins on efficiency and rate. And
the purity gap traces back to the biology: fermentation's yeast
ceiling means every litre needs **[[fractional-distillation]]**
afterwards, while hydration distils nothing biological and recycles
its own unreacted feedstock.

The strategic footnote is the future. At the moment, countries
with easy access to crude oil produce ethanol mainly from ethene —
but one day there will be no oil remaining. At that point, the
production of ethanol from sugar and starch provides an alternative
route to many of the organic chemicals we need: the renewable
route's gentle, slow, impure chemistry is the insurance policy
against the petrochemical route's finite feedstock.

:::key-point
Fermentation wins on resources (renewable, gentle conditions) and
loses on rate (days per batch), purity (very impure, ~15% yeast
ceiling, needs fractional distillation) and process type
(inefficient batch). Hydration wins on rate (quick), purity (much
purer) and process type (continuous flow) and loses on resources
(finite crude oil) and energy (high temperature and pressure).
:::

:::exam-hint
Comparison items are mark-scheme-row items: answer in the table's
own axes — resources, process type, rate, quality, conditions —
and quote the label words (**renewable/finite**, **batch/continuous
flow**, **impure/purer**, **gentle/high energy**). "Fermentation
is natural so it's better" is not a comparison; "fermentation uses
renewable sugar crops whereas hydration consumes finite crude oil"
is. And when the question asks for an *advantage of fermentation*,
reach for the resources row first — it is the row that never
flips.
:::

:::misconception
"The continuous process is better, so industry must always choose
hydration." Better *at what*? Hydration wins on speed and purity
but depends entirely on crude oil — a feedstock with an expiry
date. Countries without oil (Brazil is the standard example)
already prefer fermentation, and the textbook's own footnote makes
the long-run point: when the oil runs out, the batch process
becomes the only one left standing.
:::

name the purification fix? Three yeses and the comparison is
exam-ready.
:::
