The second industrial route comes straight out of the previous
lesson: ethanol is also made by reacting ethene with steam — a
process known as **[[hydration]]** (u4:c25, where the reaction made
its first appearance as an addition reaction of the alkenes). Now
it returns wearing its industrial hat, converting
ethene into [[ethanol]]:

CH₂=CH₂(g) + H₂O(g) → CH₃CH₂OH(g)

The conditions triplet is fixed: **ethene and steam**, a temperature
of about **300 °C**, a pressure of about **60–70 atmospheres**, and
a **phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) [[catalyst]]**. Only a small proportion of
the ethene reacts on each pass; the ethanol produced is condensed
as a liquid and the unreacted ethene is recycled through the
process — the recycle loop is what makes an inefficient single-pass
conversion industrially viable.

The reaction type deserves its own flag, because it is the same
addition chemistry the alkenes taught: this is an addition
reaction — the water is added to the ethene without removing
anything. No by-product leaves; the double bond is consumed; the
atoms all end up in one molecule of ethanol. Contrast that with
fermentation (Section 6), which *produces* carbon dioxide as a
co-product — one route adds everything it starts with, the other
splits a molecule and emits gas.

:::key-point
Hydration of ethene: ethene + steam, about 300 °C, about 60–70
atmospheres, phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) catalyst. It is an addition
reaction — the water adds to the ethene without removing anything
— and the unreacted ethene is recycled through the process.
:::

:::exam-hint
The hydration conditions are among the most examined triplets in
the course — deploy them verbatim: **steam, ~300 °C, ~60–70
atmospheres, phosphoric acid catalyst**. If asked to *describe two
conditions*, any two of the four (temperature, pressure, catalyst,
steam) earn the marks, but each must be specific — "high
temperature" alone is not; "about 300 °C" is. And name the
catalyst properly: **phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄**.
:::

:::misconception
"Hydration and fermentation differ only in speed." They differ in
nearly everything examinable: feedstock (petrochemical ethene vs
plant sugars), energy profile (forced conditions vs room-warm),
product purity (high vs very impure, capped at ~15%), reaction
type (addition vs enzymatic decomposition emitting CO₂), and
renewability (finite crude oil vs renewable crops). The
comparison section (Section 8) turns this contrast into a table —
but the table is only memorable once you know the two routes
individually.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the hydration equation with state symbols and name
the catalyst with its formula? Can you state all four process
conditions? Can you say why the reaction is classified as addition
and what the recycle loop is for? Four yeses and the petrochemical
route is banked.
:::
