The third oxidation is the one you can run yourself, and it is the
usual way of oxidising ethanol (and other alcohols) in the lab. The
**[[oxidising-agent]]** is a mixture of **potassium dichromate(VI)**
and **dilute sulfuric acid**. Potassium dichromate(VI) is a strong
oxidising agent with the formula K₂Cr₂O₇, and the dilute sulfuric
acid matters more than it looks: without the H⁺ ions from the acid,
no redox reaction will occur — the acid is a working partner, not a
spectator.

The procedure is a two-tube story in one test-tube. A few drops of
ethanol are added to a solution containing the **orange** mixture of
potassium dichromate(VI) and dilute sulfuric acid; the tube is
heated in a hot water bath; the solution turns **green**, and now
contains a very dilute solution of [[ethanoic-acid]] together with
other products. The green colour indicates the presence of Cr³⁺
ions, formed when the potassium dichromate(VI) is *reduced* —
orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ goes in, green Cr³⁺ comes out, and the ethanol pays
for it by being oxidised.

The full equation for this reaction is very complicated, so organic
chemistry uses a shorthand: the oxidising agent is written as
**[O]** in equations. Reading the [O] as "one oxygen atom delivered
by the oxidising agent", the reaction becomes clean:

CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O

Balance-check it: carbons 2 = 2; hydrogens 6 = 4 + 2 ✓; oxygens
1 + 2 = 3 on the left and 2 + 1 = 3 on the right. Two [O] units
take ethanol to ethanoic acid, with water as the co-product.

The reaction is run under **[[reflux]]**: heating with a condenser
placed vertically in the top of the flask, so that any vapours
condense and run back instead of escaping. Reflux keeps the
volatile ethanol in the reaction long enough to be fully oxidised.
You can even make a sample of pure-ish ethanoic acid this way:
heat ethanol with an *excess* of the oxidising agent under reflux,
then rearrange the condenser for a simple distillation — ethanoic
acid and water distil over together and are collected as a
solution.

:::safety
Wear eye protection and add the reactants very slowly — too
violent a reaction is possible. Never point the mouth of the
test-tube towards you: the contents may 'spit' out.
:::

:::exam-hint
The dichromate oxidation answer set: reagents (**potassium
dichromate(VI) and dilute sulfuric acid**), conditions (**heat
under reflux**), observation (**orange to green**), product
(**ethanoic acid**), and the equation with **[O]** standing in for
the oxidising agent. The (VI) in the name is not decoration —
other chromates exist that cannot oxidise alcohols, so the
statement 4.31C route is *dichromate(VI)* specifically. The
naming detail ('di-' = two chromium atoms; the roman numeral
reporting how the ion's charges and oxidation states work, met
with Cu(II) and Fe(III) in the ionic bonding chapter, u1:c07) is
extension territory — the oxidation state concept itself is not
required at International GCSE.
:::

:::misconception
"[O] is a real species — the equation shows an oxygen atom flying
around on its own." [O] is bookkeeping, not chemistry: a shorthand
for "one oxygen atom delivered by the oxidising agent", used
because the true dichromate equation is too complicated for this
course. Write it exactly as the convention — 2[O], square
brackets — and describe the oxidising agent by name in prose
(dichromate(VI)/acid), never as a free oxygen atom.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you name the reagent pair and the condition (reflux)? Can you
state the colour change and explain what the green colour tells
you about the dichromate? Can you write the [O] equation and
balance it by counting [O] units? Can you say why the acid is
essential? Four yeses and the lab oxidation is exam-ready.
:::
