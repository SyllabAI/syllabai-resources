The first of ethanol's three oxidations is the oldest one:
combustion. All alcohols burn to form carbon dioxide and water —
the same [[complete-combustion]] products as every hydrocarbon,
plus nothing new, because the –OH group's oxygen simply joins the
oxygen accounting. With ethanol:

C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

Balance it once slowly: carbons 2 = 2; hydrogens 6 = 6; oxygens
1 + 6 = 7 on the left and 4 + 3 = 7 on the right. The odd-looking
coefficient distribution — the same 3 serving both O₂ and H₂O —
is what the extra oxygen atom in the alcohol does to the arithmetic.

What makes this combustion worth a section of its own is where the
ethanol comes from. Ethanol is a **[[biofuel]]**: a fuel made from
biological sources such as sugar cane or corn, rather than from
crude oil. Mixtures of petrol with ethanol are increasingly used in
countries such as Brazil, which have little or no oil industry of
their own but a climate that grows sugar cane superbly. Other
countries are introducing biofuels such as ethanol to reduce
dependence on **fossil fuels, which are finite non-renewable
resources** — burn them and there are no more. The fuel itself is
ordinary combustion chemistry; the strategic point is the source of
the carbon.

:::did-you-know
Brazil's petrol pumps dispense the chemistry of this section at
national scale: sugarcane ethanol blended into petrol, a fleet
running partly on this year's harvest instead of hundred-million-
year-old oil. The same equation — C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O —
powers both a laboratory spirit lamp and a national fuel policy.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The biofuel story is a geography-and-economics case study wearing
a chemistry equation. Geography sets the stage: sugar cane needs
the tropical climate Brazil has and crude oil fields it lacks, so
the map of fuel production follows the climate zones, not the
geology. Economics supplies the motive: ethanol blends cut oil
imports and build energy independence, while the finiteness of
fossil reserves makes the renewable route a long-run strategy
rather than a fashion. The comparison section (Section 8) puts
numbers on the trade-off.
:::

:::exam-hint
The combustion-of-ethanol equation is a favourite one-mark
derivation because the oxygen in the –OH trips people up. Derive
in the fixed order — hydrogens to water first, carbons second,
oxygen last — and the (l) state symbol on the ethanol (it is a
liquid fuel) comes free with the working. Statement 4.31C wants
this as the *first* oxidation route: burning in air or oxygen.
:::

:::misconception
"Biofuels contain no carbon because they are 'green'." Ethanol
burns to carbon dioxide exactly like every other fuel — the –OH
group changes nothing about the products. What biofuel changes is
the *source* of the carbon (recent biological growth rather than
fossil deposits) and the renewability of the supply. Products:
CO₂ and H₂O, always.
:::

:::
