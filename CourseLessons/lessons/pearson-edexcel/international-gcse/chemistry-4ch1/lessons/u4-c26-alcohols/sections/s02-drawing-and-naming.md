Four alcohols cover the whole requirement: **[[methanol]]**,
**ethanol**, **propan-1-ol** and **butan-1-ol**. Their formulae
line up below — read the columns exactly as you read the alkane
and alkene tables, because the skill is the same one at a new
functional group.

| Name | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|
| methanol | CH₄O | CH₃OH |
| ethanol | C₂H₆O | CH₃CH₂OH |
| propan-1-ol | C₃H₈O | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH |
| butan-1-ol | C₄H₁₀O | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH |

Each molecule can also be drawn as a **[[displayed-formula]]**,
and the alcohol version has one discipline the hydrocarbon tables
never needed: the O–H bond must be shown explicitly. Methanol's
displayed formula is a central C bonded to three H atoms and to an
O, with the O bonded to one more H (the O–H); ethanol extends the
carbon chain by one carbon before the –OH; propan-1-ol and
butan-1-ol extend it further, with the –OH always on the end
carbon. Draw every bond as a line and every atom as a symbol, and
check that each carbon shows four bonds and the oxygen shows two.

The naming code decodes in three slices. The 'meth', 'eth',
'prop' and 'but' parts count the carbon atoms; the 'an' part says
the chain is saturated with no carbon-carbon double bonds; the
'ol' announces the –OH functional group. **Propanol** and
**butanol** are acceptable names in the exam — you do not need the
locant number for full credit — but you must understand it, because
the number tells you *where* the –OH group sits. Counting from the
end that gives the smaller number: –OH on the end carbon gives
propan-**1**-ol; –OH on the middle carbon gives propan-**2**-ol.

That positional freedom generates **[[isomer]]s** again: propan-1-ol
and propan-2-ol share one [[molecular-formula]] but differ in
structure, and the same holds for butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol. The
numbering convention from the alkene chapter (u4:c25) carries over
unchanged — smallest locant wins.

For the rest of this chapter, ethanol stands in for the whole
family: one molecule, three characteristic reactions, two
industrial routes.

:::exam-hint
Two drawing rules earn or lose the mark. First, when drawing the
displayed formula of any alcohol you **must show the bond between
the O and the H** — write the O–H explicitly; an oxygen left
hanging with no H is the classic lost mark. Second, the names
**propanol and butanol are acceptable in the exam** —
but if the question names propan-1-ol and you draw the –OH on a
middle carbon, the drawing is wrong no matter how tidy it is.
:::

:::key-point
The alcohol name decode: prefix counts the carbons (meth, eth,
prop, but), infix '-an-' certifies single bonds only, suffix
'-ol' flags the –OH group. The locant number in propan-1-ol
reports which carbon carries the –OH, counted from the end that
gives the smaller number.
:::

:::misconception
"Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are the same compound with the –OH
drawn at different places for convenience." Different placement of
the functional group means a genuinely different compound — the
defining test of an [[isomer]] pair — with different properties
and (at A level) different chemistry. The locant is structural
information, not a drawing convention.
:::

:::quick-check
Can you write the molecular and structural formulae of all four
alcohols from memory? Can you draw ethanol's displayed formula
with the O–H bond shown and every bond accounted for? Can you
explain what the 1 in propan-1-ol reports, and name the isomer it
hides? Four yeses and 4.30C is banked.
:::
