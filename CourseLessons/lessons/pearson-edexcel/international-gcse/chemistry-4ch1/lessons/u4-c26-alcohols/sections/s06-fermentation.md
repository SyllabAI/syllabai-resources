Industry makes ethanol two ways, and the first is older than
chemistry itself: **[[fermentation]]**. **[[yeast]]** is added to a
sugar (or starch) solution and left in the warm — about 30 °C — for
several days **in the absence of air** ([[anaerobic]] conditions).
**[[enzyme]]s** (biological catalysts) in the yeast convert the
sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Starting from [[glucose]], the
overall conversion is:

C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g)

One glucose molecule, two ethanol molecules, two carbon dioxides —
and no help from heat, pressure or an industrial catalyst, because
the enzymes do the work at room-warm temperatures.

The conditions are not preferences; they are requirements, and
statement 4.33C asks you to *explain* them. **Why the absence of
air?** In the presence of air (aerobic conditions), the enzymes in
the yeast produce carbon dioxide and water *instead of* ethanol —
the same biochemical machinery, a completely different product.
Anaerobic conditions force the glucose down the ethanol pathway.
**Why the temperature?** The enzymes are protein catalysts: if the
temperature rises much above 40 °C they lose their structure and
stop working — the proteins are said to be **denatured**. At a
lower temperature the reaction is simply too slow. Somewhere
between 30 and 40 °C is the optimum: fast enough to be useful,
gentle enough to keep the proteins folded.

The biochemistry underneath is more complicated than the overall
equation suggests — ordinary sugar (sucrose) is first split by
water into two smaller sugars, glucose and fructose, which are
structural isomers sharing the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆, and the
yeast's enzymes then convert the glucose to ethanol in many small
steps. The sucrose hydrolysis that opens the sequence:

C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁(aq) + H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + C₆H₁₂O₆(aq)

Fermentation's industrial wrapper: raw material glucose (from
sugar cane, maize/corn, wheat and other starchy materials),
temperature 30 °C, catalyst enzymes in yeast, other conditions
anaerobic. And one hard ceiling: yeast is killed by more than
about 15% of alcohol in the mixture, so it is impossible to make
pure alcohol by fermentation — the ethanol must afterwards be
purified by fractional distillation, which takes advantage of the
boiling-point gap between ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C). The
fractional-distillation technique itself was met earlier in the
course (u1:c02).

:::key-point
Fermentation: yeast + sugar solution, warm (~30 °C), several days,
in the absence of air. Enzymes in the yeast convert glucose into
ethanol and carbon dioxide. No air — because with air the enzymes
make CO₂ and water instead — and no more than ~40 °C — because
the protein enzymes denature above it.
:::

:::exam-hint
4.33C is an *explain* statement, so give mechanisms, not
adjectives. Absence of air: **with air (aerobic conditions) the
enzymes produce carbon dioxide and water instead of ethanol**.
Temperature between 30 and 40 °C: **enzymes are proteins; above
about 40 °C they denature (lose their structure) and stop
working, while below this the reaction is too slow — 30–40 °C is
the optimum**. And the yeast itself: it supplies the **enzymes**,
the biological catalysts, without which the sugar would not react
at all.
:::

:::did-you-know
The sucrose → glucose + fructose step hides an isomer surprise:
glucose and fructose have the *same* molecular formula (C₆H₁₂O₆)
but different structures — structural isomers, exactly as propan-1-ol
and propan-2-ol are in the alcohol family. The yeast's enzymes
are picky about which of the pair they process, which is part of
why the biochemistry takes many steps.
:::

:::misconception
"Fermentation eventually makes pure ethanol if you leave it long
enough." It never does: **yeast is killed by more than about 15%
alcohol**, so the reaction self-limits far below purity — the
product is very impure ethanol that needs fractional distillation
afterwards. Purity is the trade-off for fermentation's gentle,
renewable conditions; the hydration route buys purity with energy.
:::
