Everything so far described the *bookkeeping* of electron transfer. This
section zooms out to the actual substance: what a crystal of sodium
chloride physically is.

**All ionic compounds form crystals** consisting of **lattices** of
positive and negative ions packed together in a regular way. A
**[[lattice]]** is a regular array of particles — the book's everyday
analogy (Figure 7.16) is literally a lattice fence: a repeating pattern
that extends as far as you care to look. The ionic lattice is held together
by the strong [[electrostatic-attraction]] between the positively and
negatively charged ions, and in a **[[giant-ionic-lattice]]** that
attraction runs throughout the whole structure, in all directions.

:::exam-hint
The book prints this hint in bold urgency (chunk 0338): *you must not talk
about molecules of an ionic compound — this will be marked wrong in the
exam and you could lose all the marks for a question!* Say instead: ions,
lattice, giant structure, formula unit. The habit matters in every later
answer — "NaCl molecules exist in solution", "one molecule of MgO" — all
zero-mark phrasings. In electrolysis and structure questions (Units 1 and
2), the words "ions free to move" only make sense once "no molecules" is
reflex.
:::

:::misconception
"A crystal of NaCl is a huge molecule made of lots of NaCl units stuck
together." There are no NaCl units to stick together — that is precisely
what "giant" rules out. The structure is one continuous array of Na⁺ and
Cl⁻ ions with no natural boundary or repeat unit you could lift out and
call a molecule; any fragment you pick is arbitrary. The chemical formula
NaCl records the 1:1 *ratio*, not a particle that exists on its own.
:::

:::cross-curricular
The lattice is mathematics made solid: a **regular repeating array** is
exactly what a tessellation or wallpaper pattern is in geometry — one unit
cell (here, the 6-around-6 sodium-chloride arrangement) translated again and
again to fill space with no gaps. Crystallography, the science built on that
idea, won Nobel prizes by applying X-ray diffraction to precisely the
lattices this chapter describes — the same repeating-array mathematics also
underlies wallpaper groups, tiling problems and the way metals are forged.
When the book reaches for a lattice fence (Figure 7.16) to explain the word,
it is pointing at one of chemistry's deepest exports to mathematics.
:::
