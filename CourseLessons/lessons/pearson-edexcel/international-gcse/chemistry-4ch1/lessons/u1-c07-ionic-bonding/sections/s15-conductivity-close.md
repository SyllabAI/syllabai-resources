The last spec point is a two-state rule with one forbidden word.

**When solid:** ionic compounds **do not conduct** electricity — the ions are
**fixed in position** in the lattice and are not free to move around. **When
[[molten]]** (melted) **or in [[aqueous-solution]]** (dissolved in water):
they **do conduct** — because the ions then become **free to move** and can
carry the charge through the liquid.

One language rule carries the whole answer: the charge carriers are
**ions**. Do not say "electrons" — the examiners require the exact words:
*the ions are free to move*. That single substitution is the difference
between the mark and no mark.

:::summary
In one page: **formation** — metals lose electrons, non-metals gain them;
the charged particles are ions (cation +, anion −). **Diagrams** —
dot-and-cross, outer shells only, charges labelled. **Charges** — Groups
1/2/3 lose 1/2/3 electrons (1+/2+/3+); Groups 5/6/7 gain 3/2/1 (3−/2−/1−);
plus the learned list Zn²⁺, Ag⁺, H⁺, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, OH⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻ (not all
ions are noble-gas configurations — Fe³⁺ and friends). **Formulae** — equal
pluses and minuses; brackets only when a complex ion is multiplied;
-ide = two elements, -ate = oxygen present; crossover with the
same-charges trap (CaO, not Ca₂O₂). **Structure** — giant ionic lattice,
no molecules, 6-around-6. **Properties** — high melting/boiling points
(strong electrostatic attraction throughout the lattice), crystalline,
brittle, water-soluble/organic-insoluble, and conducting only when molten
or aqueous because only then are the ions free to move.
:::

:::exam-hint
The exam shape for this topic is stable and worth knowing in advance:
electronic configurations of the ions in named reactions, then dot-and-cross
diagrams for Group-1/2 × Group-7 combinations you have met here as families
(K/F, Ca/Br, Mg/I are the classics). Nearly every ionic-bonding question is
the same three moves in the same order: configurations → diagram → formula.
Drill the moves, not the examples.
:::

:::misconception
"Solid ionic compounds don't conduct because their electrons can't move."
Swap the carrier word and the answer is correct: the ions are the charge
carriers, and in the solid they are locked in the lattice. Writing
"electrons" in a 1.43 answer costs the mark even though the sentence sounds
physical — the spec words are *ions fixed in position* (solid) and *ions
free to move* (molten/aqueous). The forbidden word is the tell that the
learner has confused this with metallic conduction (Chapter 9).
:::
