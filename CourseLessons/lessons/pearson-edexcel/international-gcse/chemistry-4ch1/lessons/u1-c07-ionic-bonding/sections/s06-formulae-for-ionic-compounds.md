There are far too many ionic compounds on the course to learn formulae one
by one — the book is blunt about it: *you need a simple, shortcut method*.
The method rests on one non-negotiable law.

**The law: equal numbers of pluses and minuses.** Ions are formed when atoms
or groups of atoms lose or gain electrons, and every compound is electrically
**neutral** — so an ionic compound must contain the right number of each ion
for the total positive charge to cancel the total negative charge exactly.
To apply it you need the charges, and there are two ways to get them.

**Where you can work the charge out.** The group law from section 4, in the
book's own Table 7.1 (chunk 0322):

| Group in the Periodic Table | Charge on ion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1+ | Na⁺ |
| 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺ |
| 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| 5 | 3− | N³⁻ |
| 6 | 2− | O²⁻ |
| 7 | 1− | Br⁻ |

:::key-point
Chunk 0323 adds the two reading rules for Table 7.1. Any element in Group 2
has 2 outer electrons and loses them all → 2+; any element in Group 6 has 6
outer electrons and room for 2 more → 2−; similar arguments fix the other
groups shown. And the REMINDER (chunk 0319) stands behind every row: **all
metals form positive ions.**
:::

**Where the name tells you the charge.** For metals that form more than one
ion, the name carries the charge in Roman numerals: lead(II) oxide contains
Pb²⁺; iron(III) chloride contains Fe³⁺; copper(II) sulfate contains Cu²⁺.
The number in brackets *is* the number of charges.

:::key-point
Chunk 0321 gives the metal-free exception its full picture: ammonium
chloride (NH₄Cl) is ionic — there is ionic bonding between the NH₄⁺ and
Cl⁻ ions — yet *inside* the NH₄⁺ ion the N–H links are covalent (Chapter
8). An ionic compound can therefore contain covalent bonds in its
polyatomic ion; the two descriptions apply at different scales.
:::

**The learn-list.** Some charges cannot be worked out at all — the book's
Table 7.2 (chunk 0324), verbatim (OCR repairs disclosed in the receipt):

| Charge | Substance | Ion |
|---|---|---|
| positive | zinc | Zn²⁺ |
|  | silver | Ag⁺ |
|  | hydrogen | H⁺ |
|  | ammonium | NH₄⁺ |
| negative | nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
|  | hydroxide | OH⁻ |
|  | carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
|  | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |

:::key-point
Chunks 0326 + 0328 close the method. (1) These learn-list ions are the
difficult ones — learn **both the formula and the charge** for each; you
will meet others later, but this eight is the working set. (2) The brackets
law: when a formula needs **more than one** of a multi-atom
complex ion, write brackets — barium nitrate is
Ba(NO₃)₂; without the brackets, "BaNO32" would read as 1 barium, 1
nitrogen and **32 oxygens**. Anywhere else, brackets are unnecessary.
:::

:::exam-hint
The book's hint (chunk 0318) removes the last excuse: *you will always have
a copy of the Periodic Table, even in an exam*, so the group charges are
always available. What the table cannot tell you: lead forms Pb²⁺ (Group 4
elements form few ionic compounds and the situation is more complicated) —
Pb²⁺ rides on the named-ion list. Exam routine: group charges from the
Periodic Table; named charges from memory; Roman numerals from the name.
:::

:::misconception
"Ionic formulae are 100% memorisation." Only the eight learn-list charges
are memory; everything else is *derivation*. Balance the charges and count:
a 2+ ion meeting a 1− ion needs two of the 1− ion; a 3+ meeting a 2− needs
three and two respectively (6+ balances 6−). Learners who memorise formulae
one at a time drown by Unit 2; learners who balance charges write unfamiliar
formulae — aluminium sulfate, sodium carbonate — on demand.
:::

:::maths-skills
Balancing ion charges is a **common-multiple** problem wearing a chemistry
coat. Fe³⁺ brings 3 units; SO₄²⁻ brings 2 — the smallest total both can
reach is the lowest common multiple, 6, which takes 2 irons and 3 sulfates.
The same arithmetic the book's crossover exploits (Figure 7.13): swapping
the two charge numbers *is* dividing by the product over each charge.
Whenever the charge pair shares a factor (2+/2−, 3+/3−, 2+/4− beyond this
course), the LCM view also tells you the ratio will simplify — CaO not
Ca₂O₂.
:::
