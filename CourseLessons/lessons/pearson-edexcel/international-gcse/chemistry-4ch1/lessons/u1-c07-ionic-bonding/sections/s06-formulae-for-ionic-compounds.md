There are far too many ionic compounds on the course to learn formulae one
by one — you need a simple, shortcut method. The method rests on one
non-negotiable law.

**The law: equal numbers of pluses and minuses.** Ions are formed when atoms
or groups of atoms lose or gain electrons, and every compound is electrically
**neutral** — so an ionic compound must contain the right number of each ion
for the total positive charge to cancel the total negative charge exactly.
To apply it you need the charges, and there are two ways to get them.

**Where you can work the charge out.** The group law from section 4, in one
table:

| Group in the Periodic Table | Charge on ion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1+ | Na⁺ |
| 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺ |
| 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| 5 | 3− | N³⁻ |
| 6 | 2− | O²⁻ |
| 7 | 1− | Br⁻ |

:::key-point
Two reading rules for that table. Any element in Group 2 has 2 outer
electrons and loses them all → 2+; any element in Group 6 has 6 outer
electrons and room for 2 more → 2−; similar arguments fix the other groups
shown. And the reminder that stands behind every row: **all metals form
positive ions.**
:::

**Where the name tells you the charge.** For metals that form more than one
ion, the name carries the charge in Roman numerals: lead(II) oxide contains
Pb²⁺; iron(III) chloride contains Fe³⁺; copper(II) sulfate contains Cu²⁺.
The number in brackets *is* the number of charges.

:::key-point
The metal-free exception in its full picture: ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) is
ionic — there is ionic bonding between the NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions — yet *inside*
the NH₄⁺ ion the N–H links are covalent (Chapter 8). An ionic compound can
therefore contain covalent bonds in its polyatomic ion; the two
descriptions apply at different scales.
:::

**The learn-list.** Some charges cannot be worked out at all — memorise
this table (both the formula and the charge of every ion on it):

| Charge | Substance | Ion |
|---|---|---|
| positive | zinc | Zn²⁺ |
|  | silver | Ag⁺ |
|  | hydrogen | H⁺ |
|  | ammonium | NH₄⁺ |
| negative | nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
|  | hydroxide | OH⁻ |
|  | carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
|  | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |

:::key-point
Two closers for the method. (1) These learn-list ions are the difficult
ones — learn **both the formula and the charge** for each; you will meet
others later, but this eight is the working set. (2) The brackets law: when
a formula needs **more than one** of a multi-atom complex ion, write
brackets — barium nitrate is Ba(NO₃)₂; without the brackets, "BaNO32" would
read as 1 barium, 1 nitrogen and **32 oxygens**. Anywhere else, brackets
are unnecessary.
:::

:::exam-hint
One habit removes the last excuse: *you will always have a copy of the
Periodic Table, even in an exam* — so the group charges are always
available. What the table cannot tell you: lead forms Pb²⁺ (Group 4
elements form few ionic compounds and the situation is more complicated) —
Pb²⁺ rides on the named-ion list. Exam routine: group charges from the
Periodic Table; named charges from memory; Roman numerals from the name.
And one warning that earns its own headline: *not looking carefully at word
endings is one of the most common mistakes students make when they start to
write formulae* — the -ide/-ate endings get their own section next.
:::

:::misconception
"Ionic formulae are 100% memorisation." Only the eight learn-list charges
are memory; everything else is *derivation*. Balance the charges and count:
a 2+ ion meeting a 1− ion needs two of the 1− ion; a 3+ meeting a 2− needs
three and two respectively (6+ balances 6−). Learners who memorise formulae
one at a time drown by Unit 2; learners who balance charges write
unfamiliar formulae — aluminium sulfate, sodium carbonate — on demand.
:::

:::quick-check
Question-bank join: spec 1.38 items — charges of the learn-list ions and
group charges; spec 1.39 items — formulae from ion pairs (both-sides
drilling: Na⁺/O²⁻; Ca²⁺/Cl⁻; NH₄⁺/SO₄²⁻). The two tables are the recall
targets; drill until writing Na₂SO₄ and Al₂O₃ takes under ten seconds.
:::
