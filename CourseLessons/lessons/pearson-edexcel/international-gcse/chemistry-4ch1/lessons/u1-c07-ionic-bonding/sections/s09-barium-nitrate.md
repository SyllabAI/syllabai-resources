The second worked example adds the one piece of syntax the method needs:
**brackets around a multi-atom ion** when more than one of it appears.

**Barium nitrate.** Barium is in Group 2, so the ion is Ba²⁺. Nitrate ions
are NO₃⁻ — a [[complex-ion]] (an ion made of more than one atom) whose
charge you *remember*, not derive (Table 7.2, section 6). To equalise
charges you need **two nitrate ions for each barium ion** (2+ against
2 × 1−). The formula is **Ba(NO₃)₂**.

The brackets are load-bearing, not decoration: they say "everything inside
is doubled". Written without them the formula reads BaNO₃₂ — and the book's
KEY POINT makes the absurdity vivid: *that would read as 1 barium, 1
nitrogen and 32 oxygens!* With brackets: 1 barium, 2 nitrogens, 6 oxygens.

:::key-point
Chunk 0333 answers a question learners are often shy to ask — why don't
formulae show the charges? They *can*: sodium chloride is sometimes written
Na⁺Cl⁻ to make a point about the ions present. But for most purposes the
charges are left out: **in an ionic compound the charges are there, whether
you write them or not.** The formula records the neutral ratio; the charge
labels live on the ions, and on dot-and-cross diagrams.
:::

:::exam-hint
Brackets law in one line: brackets appear **only** when a complex ion is
multiplied. Ba(NO₃)₂ yes; Na₂SO₄ yes (sulfate doubled); NaNO₃ no (one
nitrate — brackets would be harmless but non-standard); NaCl no. When a
mark scheme says "correct use of brackets", it is checking exactly this
rule — and the (NO₃)₂ must be followed by the subscript *outside* the
bracket, never inside.
:::

:::misconception
"Ba(NO₃)₂ and BaNO₃₂ are the same formula written differently." They name
different substances: the bracketed form is 1 Ba, 2 N, 6 O; the unbracketed
run-together reads 1 Ba, 1 N, 32 O — physically nothing. The 2 in a formula
always multiplies *what it follows*: following a bracket it doubles the
whole ion; following an element symbol it multiplies only that element. That
subtle reading is why the bracket exists.
:::
