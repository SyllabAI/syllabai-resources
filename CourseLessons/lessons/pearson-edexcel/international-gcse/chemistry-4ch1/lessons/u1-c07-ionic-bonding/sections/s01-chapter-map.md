Chapter 7 takes the compound idea from Chapter 2 and finally explains what
"chemically combined" means for the most important family of compounds on
the course. Start from the everyday proof: sodium is a dangerously reactive
metal stored under oil, chlorine is a poisonous, reactive gas — yet the
compound they form, sodium chloride, is safe to eat in small quantities.
The properties of a compound are nothing like the properties of its
elements, and this lesson explains why: in ionic compounds the atoms have
**transferred electrons** and become charged ions.

All of the objectives of record for this lesson are **core** specification
statements — examined in **both Paper 1 (1C) and Paper 2 (2C)**, and shared
with Science Double Award:

- how **ions are formed by electron loss or gain** (spec 1.37);
- the **charges** on ions of Group 1/2/3 metals, Group 5/6/7 non-metals, and
  the named ions Ag⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺, H⁺, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻,
  NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻ (spec 1.38);
- **writing formulae** for compounds between those ions (spec 1.39);
- **dot-and-cross diagrams** for electron transfer, outer electrons only
  (spec 1.40);
- ionic bonding as **electrostatic attraction** (spec 1.41);
- why **giant ionic lattices** have high melting and boiling points (spec 1.42);
- **conductivity**: no conduction when solid, conduction when molten or in
  aqueous solution (spec 1.43).

The lesson runs in three movements: forming ions and showing the transfer
with dot-and-cross diagrams, then the formula shortcut — equal pluses and
minuses, with the learn-list of ion charges — then the giant-lattice
structure and the properties that follow from it. Master all three and
every ionic-bonding question on the course becomes routine.

:::prior-knowledge
This lesson builds straight on work you have already done, and three pieces
carry all the weight: the **definition of a compound** from Chapter 2
(u1:c02 — two or more elements chemically combined); **electronic
configurations** from Chapter 3 (u1:c03) — you need [2, 8, 1] for sodium and
[2, 8, 7] for chlorine without hesitation; and **groups of the Periodic
Table** from Chapter 4 (u1:c04) — the group number tells you how many
outer-shell electrons an atom has, which is what decides the ion charge. If
writing [2, 8, 8, 2] for calcium feels slow, revisit u1:c03/c04 first.
:::

:::exam-hint
Every objective in this lesson is core — it can be asked in Paper 1 or Paper
2, by every candidate including Double Award. Ion charges and formulae
(1.38/1.39) are the recall backbone of dozens of later questions: equations,
titrations, salt preparations and electrolysis all assume you can write
Na₂O or Fe₂(SO₄)₃ without hesitating. Treat the learn-list in section 6 as
non-negotiable memory work — it pays out across the whole qualification, not
just this lesson.
:::

:::misconception
"This is a brand-new topic, so it starts from scratch." It doesn't — it
*uses* Chapter 3 and 4 rather than replacing them. Every statement in this
lesson is the electronic-configuration idea pointed at a new job: the group
number (Chapter 4) predicts the charge; the outer shell (Chapter 3) is the
only part drawn in a dot-and-cross diagram. Learners who file ionic bonding
as a separate subject memorise; learners who see it as
configurations-in-action derive.
:::
