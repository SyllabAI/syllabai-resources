The method now runs on worked examples, and the first one is deliberately
the smallest case where a plain "1:1 cancels" reflex fails.

**Sodium oxide.** Sodium is in Group 1, so the ion is Na⁺. Oxygen is in
Group 6, so the ion is O²⁻. To equalise the charges you need **two sodium
ions** (2 × 1+ = 2+) for **one oxide ion** (2−): the 2+ from two sodiums
cancels the 2− of one oxide. The formula is therefore **Na₂O**.

The worked example is short because the method is short: name the ions,
count the charges, scale to balance, write the formula with the counts as
subscripts. Forty seconds in an exam, one mark banked.

:::exam-hint
Write the ion charges ABOVE the symbols before scaling: Na⁺ over Na, O²⁻
over O. The intermediate "2 Na⁺ per 1 O²⁻" line is what the mark scheme's
"balancing charges" credit rewards — and it catches the slide back to "NaO"
because the 2− makes you look for *two* positives, not one. Subscripts
belong to the atom that needs multiplying: Na₂O, never NaO₂ (two oxygens
would give 4− against 2+).
:::

:::misconception
"Na⁺ and O²⁻ — the + and − cancel 1-for-1, so NaO." The cancellation is
between total charge *magnitudes*, not between ions. One Na⁺ carries one
unit of positive charge; one O²⁻ carries TWO units of negative. Neutral
happens at two positives per one negative — 2+ against 2−. The charge
*number* (the superscript) is how many units each ion brings; the formula
counts how many ions bring it.
:::
