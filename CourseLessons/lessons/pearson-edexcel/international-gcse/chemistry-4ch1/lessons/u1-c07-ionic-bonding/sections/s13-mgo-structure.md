The book takes one crystal and dissects it. In diagrams the ions of sodium
chloride are usually drawn in an **"exploded" view** (Figure 7.18): each
sodium ion is surrounded by **6 chloride ions**, and each chloride ion is
surrounded by **6 sodium ions** — and that 6-around-6 pattern repeats
throughout the structure over vast numbers of ions. The lines in the
exploded diagram are not bonds; they only show which ions touch.

This is the **[[giant-ionic-lattice]]** in its full sense: *giant* is not
"big" — it means a structure with **no individual molecules**. All the
sodium ions attract all the chloride ions; you cannot pick out a sodium
chloride molecule, because none exists. The bonding extends throughout the
structure in every direction, with no limit to the particle count — all we
know is that the numbers of sodium and chloride ions must be equal.

**Magnesium oxide has exactly the same structure** — Mg²⁺ in place of Na⁺,
O²⁻ in place of Cl⁻, the same 6-around-6 lattice. The difference is force:
the magnesium oxide lattice is held together by **stronger
[[electrostatic-attraction]]**, because 2+ ions are attracting 2− ions,
where sodium chloride's attractions run only between 1+ and 1− ions.
(Extension work from the book: Mg²⁺ is also smaller than Na⁺ and O²⁻
smaller than Cl⁻, which raises the attraction further — but the charge
effect is the more important one.)

:::key-point
The 6-around-6 number and the "no molecules" law are the two facts examiners
probe in structure questions: describe the coordination (each ion surrounded
by six of the opposite charge) and name the force (electrostatic attraction
between oppositely charged ions, extending throughout the giant lattice).
The same-structure-different-strength comparison of MgO vs NaCl is the
classic "explain why magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium
chloride" answer at this level: higher charges → stronger attraction → more
energy to break the lattice.
:::

:::exam-hint
When explaining melting-point differences (spec 1.42), anchor every step to
the lattice: charge → attraction strength → energy to separate → melting
point. "MgO has stronger ionic bonds because the ions are charged 2+/2−"
scores; "MgO's bonds are just stronger" scores nothing — the examiner wants
the *property of the ions* (charge) linked to the *property of the lattice*
(attraction) to the *observable* (melting point). Keep the exploded-view
detail in reserve for "describe the structure" items: 6 of each, repeating,
no molecules.
:::

:::misconception
"The lines in the exploded lattice diagram are the ionic bonds — six bonds
per sodium ion." The book pre-empts this: the lines are *not* bonds, they
show touching/arrangement. Each ion's attraction is not six private links
but an electrostatic pull on all nearby oppositely charged ions, extending
throughout the giant lattice. Drawing conventions ≠ physical bonds — the
same discipline as dot-and-cross dots (section 2): notation is bookkeeping,
the physics is the attraction.
:::
