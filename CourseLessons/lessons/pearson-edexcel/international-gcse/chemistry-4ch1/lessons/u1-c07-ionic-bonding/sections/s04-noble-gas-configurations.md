Look back at the ions formed so far — Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg²⁺, O²⁻ — and a pattern
emerges: every one of them has a **noble gas electronic configuration**,
[2, 8] (neon) or [2, 8, 8] (argon). That is the deep "why" behind ion
formation for the first 20 elements: atoms lose or gain electrons so that
they **achieve a noble gas electronic configuration** — the already-stable
arrangement of Group 0.

The group law follows. Elements in **Groups 1, 2 and 3** *lose* their outer
shell electrons to form **1+, 2+ and 3+** ions; elements in **Groups 5, 6 and
7** *gain* electrons to form **3−, 2− and 1−** ions. The book's worked
corners: aluminium ([2, 8, 3]) loses three electrons → Al³⁺; nitrogen ([2, 5])
gains three → the nitride ion N³⁻. And elements in Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7
**always** form ions [[isoelectronic]] with the nearest noble gas: rubidium
(Group 1) loses its one outer electron to become isoelectronic with krypton;
iodine (Group 7) gains one electron to become isoelectronic with xenon.

:::key-point
Spec 1.38's charge table in one view (chunk 0311 + the chapter's Table 7.1,
rendered in section 6): Groups 1/2/3 lose 1/2/3 electrons → 1+/2+/3+;
Groups 5/6/7 gain 3/2/1 → 3−/2−/1−. The group number is the outer-electron
count; the charge is the cheapest route to a full outer shell.
:::

Then the book lands the caveat that keeps 1.38 honest: **a lot of common ions
do not have noble gas structures**. Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ag⁺ and Pb²⁺ are
all ions you will meet across the International GCSE course, and *not one of
them has a noble gas structure* (the book shows the Fe²⁺ ion with the caption
"definitely not a noble gas structure!"). You are not required to write their
electronic configurations — only their charges, learned as data (section 6,
Table 7.2).

:::exam-hint
"Noble gas configuration" is a *description*, not a *cause* you must argue at
this level: for the first 20 elements the stable arrangement explains the
group law, and that is the full depth the spec wants. The exam trap is
over-applying it — a question that offers Fe³⁺ or Zn²⁺ is testing whether you
know the named-ion charges (1.38) rather than the group rule. Learn both
lists and never mix them: group charges for Groups 1/2/3/5/6/7, learned
charges for the six named metals plus H⁺ and the four complex ions.
:::

:::misconception
"Every ion must be isoelectronic with a noble gas — so Fe³⁺ can't exist." It
exists, it's common, and it is not isoelectronic with anything noble — that is
exactly why the book flags it. The noble-gas rule is a *trend for the first 20
elements*, not a law of the universe. Transition-metal and heavy-metal ions
(Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Pb) have their own charges that are learned, not derived.
:::
