Two name-endings decide what a compound contains, and mixing them up is one
of the most common formula errors on the course (the book says so — the hint
is quoted in section 6's census and repeated here because this page is where
it pays).

**-ide: nothing complicated.** An *-ide* name means the compound contains
exactly the elements named and nothing else. Sodium chloride is just sodium
and chlorine combined; copper(II) **sulfide** contains copper and sulfur
only — Cu²⁺ and S²⁻ ions, formula **CuS** (the book's Figure 7.11).

**-ate: oxygen is there too.** An *-ate* name means oxygen (and possibly
other things) is present as well. Copper(II) **sulfate** contains copper,
sulfur **and oxygen** — Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻, formula **CuSO₄** (Figure 7.12).

:::exam-hint
The book's warning (chunk 0327): *not looking carefully at word endings is
one of the most common mistakes students make when they start to write
formulae.* Exam drill: read the ending FIRST — -ide → two-element pair;
-ate → oxygen present — then pick the ion from the learn-list. Sulfide S²⁻
versus sulfate SO₄²⁻; nitride N³⁻ versus nitrate NO₃⁻; chloride Cl⁻ versus
chlorate (you will not need chlorate on this course, but the ending logic
already tells you it carries oxygen).
:::

:::misconception
"-ate just sounds more chemical — the ending doesn't carry information." The
ending is a compressed composition statement, and it changes the ion you
select: copper(II) sulfide and copper(II) sulfate differ by a whole ion
(S²⁻ versus SO₄²⁻) and therefore by a whole formula (CuS versus CuSO₄).
Misreading one letter flips the answer — which is exactly why examiners
like the pair.
:::

:::quick-check
Question-bank join (derived-links law): spec 1.39 items — selecting the
correct ion from an -ide/-ate name before writing the formula (CuS vs
CuSO₄; NaCl vs Na₂SO₄-style pairs). Quick self-test: name the ions in
magnesium sulfide, magnesium sulfate, calcium nitride and calcium nitrate —
then write all four formulae by balancing charges.
:::
