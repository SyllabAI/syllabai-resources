Two name-endings decide what a compound contains, and mixing them up is one
of the most common formula errors on the course.

**-ide: nothing complicated.** An *-ide* name means the compound contains
exactly the elements named and nothing else. Sodium chloride is just sodium
and chlorine combined; copper(II) **sulfide** contains copper and sulfur
only — Cu²⁺ and S²⁻ ions, formula **CuS**.

**-ate: oxygen is there too.** An *-ate* name means oxygen (and possibly
other things) is present as well. Copper(II) **sulfate** contains copper,
sulfur **and oxygen** — Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻, formula **CuSO₄**.

:::exam-hint
Read the ending FIRST — -ide → two-element pair; -ate → oxygen present —
then pick the ion from the learn-list. Sulfide S²⁻ versus sulfate SO₄²⁻;
nitride N³⁻ versus nitrate NO₃⁻; chloride Cl⁻ versus chlorate (you will
not need chlorate on this course, but the ending logic already tells you it
carries oxygen). In "explain why the formula is wrong" items, the wrong
ending is usually the whole story.
:::

:::misconception
"-ate just sounds more chemical — the ending doesn't carry information." The
ending is a compressed composition statement, and it changes the ion you
select: copper(II) sulfide and copper(II) sulfate differ by a whole ion
(S²⁻ versus SO₄²⁻) and therefore by a whole formula (CuS versus CuSO₄).
Misreading one letter flips the answer — which is exactly why examiners
like the pair.
:::

Question-bank join: spec 1.39 items — selecting the correct ion from an
-ide/-ate name before writing the formula (CuS vs CuSO₄; NaCl vs Na₂SO₄
pairs). Quick self-test: name the ions in magnesium sulfide, magnesium
sulfate, calcium nitride and calcium nitrate — then write all four formulae
by balancing charges.
:::
