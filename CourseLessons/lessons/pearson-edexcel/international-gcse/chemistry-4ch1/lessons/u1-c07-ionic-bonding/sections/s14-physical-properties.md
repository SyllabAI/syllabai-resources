Four properties, one cause: the giant lattice.

**High melting and boiling points.** Ionic compounds have high melting
points and boiling points **because of the strong electrostatic forces of
attraction holding the lattice together**: a lot of energy must be supplied
to break the attractions between the many oppositely charged ions in the
giant structure. (Spec 1.42's whole demand.)

**Crystalline.** Ionic compounds tend to be crystalline — the visible echo
of the regular lattice. Some crystals are too small to see: magnesium oxide
is always a white powder because its individual crystals are microscopic.

**Brittle.** Ionic crystals tend to be brittle: any small distortion of the
crystal brings ions of the **same charge** alongside each other — like
charges repel — and the crystal splits itself apart along that plane.

**Solubility pattern.** Ionic substances tend to be **soluble in water** and
**insoluble in organic solvents** (ethanol, hexane and the petrol
hydrocarbons are the everyday examples of the organic-solvent family you
will meet properly in Unit 4). Going further: water is *polar* — its shared
electrons sit nearer the oxygen, leaving oxygen slightly negative and the
hydrogens slightly positive — so water molecules form strong enough
attractions to ions to pull the lattice apart. Hexane is non-polar and
cannot. Not every ionic salt obeys: magnesium oxide is insoluble in water —
its 2+/2− attractions are too strong for the water molecules to overcome.

:::key-point
The vocabulary note worth keeping: **organic solvents** include ethanol
(alcohol) and hydrocarbons such as hexane and the mixture in petrol. The
property pair to recall: soluble in water; insoluble in organic solvents.
:::

:::exam-hint
The word **molten** just means the salt has been melted — it is a liquid.
It appears in every conductivity and electrolysis question: "molten sodium
chloride" = melted NaCl, ions free to move; "aqueous" = dissolved in water,
same conclusion. Both words are route words, not chemistry content — know
them cold so a vocabulary wobble never costs a physics-of-ions mark.
(Spec 1.42's melting-point explanation gets its marks from the
lattice-energy chain of section 13, not from the word "strong".)
:::

:::misconception
"Ionic compounds have high melting points because an ionic bond is stronger
than a covalent bond." The comparison is not bond-versus-bond — it is
structure-versus-structure. Melting means separating the particles of the
lattice, and in a giant lattice *every* ion is attracting *many* oppositely
charged neighbours: you must overcome a vast network of attractions, not
one pair. State it structurally — strong electrostatic attraction
throughout the giant lattice, much energy to break — and the mark scheme's
levels are all covered.
:::
