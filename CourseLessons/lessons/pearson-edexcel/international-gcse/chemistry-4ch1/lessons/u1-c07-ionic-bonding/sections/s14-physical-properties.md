Four properties, one cause. (Provenance note: the book prints the brittleness
and solubility sentences on the chapter-questions page, and the chunker swept
them into the questions chunk 0345 ahead of the printed question list — they
are section prose here, with the questions staying page-jump-only, exactly the
c06 Example-4 spillover precedent.)

**High melting and boiling points.** Ionic compounds have high melting points
and boiling points **because of the strong electrostatic forces of attraction
holding the lattice together**: a lot of energy must be supplied to break the
attractions between the many oppositely charged ions in the giant structure.
(spec 1.42's whole demand.)

**Crystalline.** Ionic compounds tend to be crystalline — the visible echo of
the regular lattice. Some crystals are too small to see: magnesium oxide is
always a white powder because its individual crystals are microscopic.

**Brittle.** Ionic crystals tend to be brittle: any small distortion of the
crystal brings ions of the **same charge** alongside each other — like
charges repel — and the crystal splits itself apart along that plane (the
book's Figure 7.20 diagrams the repulsion between same-charge ions doing the
splitting).

**Solubility pattern.** Ionic substances tend to be **soluble in water** and
**insoluble in organic solvents** (ethanol, hexane and the petrol hydrocarbons
are the book's examples — chunk 0343). Extension work (chunk 0346): water is
*polar* — its shared electrons sit nearer the oxygen, leaving oxygen slightly
negative and the hydrogens slightly positive — so water molecules form strong
enough attractions to ions to pull the lattice apart. Hexane is non-polar and
cannot. Not every ionic salt obeys: magnesium oxide is insoluble in water —
its 2+/2− attractions are too strong for the water molecules to overcome.

:::key-point
Chunk 0343's vocabulary note: **organic solvents** include ethanol (alcohol)
and hydrocarbons such as hexane and the mixture in petrol — the Unit-4
organic chemistry of this course names the family. The property pair to
recall: soluble in water; insoluble in organic solvents.
:::

:::exam-hint
The book's hint (chunk 0344): *molten just means the salt has been melted —
it is a liquid.* The word appears in every conductivity and electrolysis
question: "molten sodium chloride" = melted NaCl, ions free to move;
"aqueous" = dissolved in water, same conclusion. Both words are route words,
not chemistry content — know them cold so a vocabulary wobble never costs a
physics-of-ions mark. (Spec 1.42's melting-point explanation gets its marks
from the lattice-energy chain of section 13, not from the word "strong".)
:::

:::misconception
"Ionic compounds have high melting points because an ionic bond is stronger
than a covalent bond." The comparison is not bond-versus-bond — it is
structure-versus-structure. Melting means separating the particles of the
lattice, and in a giant lattice *every* ion is attracting *many* oppositely
charged neighbours: you must overcome a vast network of attractions, not one
pair. State it structurally — strong electrostatic attraction throughout the
giant lattice, much energy to break — and the mark scheme's levels are all
covered.
:::
