The third worked example is the hard case the method was built for: a 3+
metal against a 2− complex ion.

**Iron(III) sulfate.** Iron(III) tells you the metal ion is Fe³⁺ (the name
carries the charge — section 6's Roman-numeral rule). Sulfate ions are
SO₄²⁻. Balancing: you need **two iron(III) ions for every three sulfate
ions** — 2 × 3+ = 6+ and 3 × 2− = 6−, equal and opposite. The formula is
**Fe₂(SO₄)₃**.

Both syntax laws fire at once: the subscript 2 on iron (multiplied because
2 ions are needed) and the brackets with subscript 3 on sulfate (a complex
ion multiplied). Total audit: 2 Fe, 4 × 3 = 12 O, charges 6+ = 6−.

:::key-point
The crossover shortcut: to work out complicated formulae, **cross over the
numbers in the charges** — the 3 from Fe³⁺ becomes the sulfate count; the 2
from SO₄²⁻ becomes the iron count. Fe³⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Fe₂(SO₄)₃ in one move.
The crossover *is* the balanced-charge arithmetic in disguise; the totals
check (6+/6−) is always available to prove it.
:::

:::maths-skills
The crossover is a **digit-swap** shortcut with a built-in audit. Swap the
magnitudes — 3 lands on the sulfate count, 2 on the iron count — then
verify by totals: 2 × 3+ = 6+ and 3 × 2− = 6−. The audit is the safety net
the swap lacks: applied blindly to Ca²⁺ + O²⁻ it yields Ca₂O₂, and the
totals check (4+ = 4−) says "consistent but not lowest" — simplify to CaO.
Swap, then always re-add the charges; if the sums are not equal, the
formula is wrong, whatever the swap said.
:::

:::exam-hint
The crossover shortcut is fast but it is *presentational* — the marks sit
in the balancing statement. Write "6+ and 6−" (or "2 × 3+ balances 3 × 2−")
before the formula: that sentence earns the marks even if the final formula
slips, and it catches the classic slip of crossing over to Fe₃(SO₄)₂ (which
gives 9+/4− — instantly unbalanced). In "explain why Fe₂(SO₄)₃ is the
correct formula" items, the totals line IS the answer.
:::

:::misconception
"3+ and 2− balance each other directly, so the formula is FeSO₄ with the
charges noted." FeSO₄ is iron(II) sulfate — a *different salt* made of
Fe²⁺. Charges balance as totals across the whole formula unit, not
ion-by-ion: 3+ against 2− needs the lowest common multiple (6), which takes
2 irons and 3 sulfates. The Roman numeral in the name is the charge of the
metal ion — and iron(II) versus iron(III) formulas are the standard exam
pair for testing exactly this.
:::
