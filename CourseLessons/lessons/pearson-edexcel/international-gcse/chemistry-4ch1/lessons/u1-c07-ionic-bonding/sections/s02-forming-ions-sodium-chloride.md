Sodium chloride is the book's flagship example: an **[[ion]]ic** compound
formed when a metal combines with a non-metal. You can usually recognise
ionic compounds because they contain a metal — magnesium oxide, calcium
fluoride and zinc bromide all follow the metal-plus-non-metal pattern. The
book's own hint (below) flags the ammonium exceptions early so the rule
never gets over-learned.

Here is the formation story for NaCl, one step at a time. A sodium atom has
electronic configuration [2, 8, 1] — one outer electron. A chlorine atom has
[2, 8, 7] — room for exactly one more. Chlorine attracts electrons more
strongly than sodium does, so **one electron is transferred** from the sodium's
outer shell to the chlorine's outer shell. Losing an electron leaves sodium
with one more proton than electron: charge 1+. Gaining one leaves chlorine
with one more electron than proton: charge 1−.

:::key-point
Two of the book's KEY POINTs anchor the mechanism. (1) The formation reaction
is a simplification — in reality you don't react sodium *atoms* with chlorine
*atoms* but with chlorine molecules, Cl₂ (chunk 0305); the atom-level story is
the model, not the laboratory recipe. (2) You can check the 1+ charge from the
Periodic Table: sodium has atomic number 11, so 11 protons give an 11+ nucleus;
the atom's 11 electrons cancel it to neutral, but the ion has only 10 electrons
— 11+ against 10− leaves an overall **1+** (chunk 0306).
:::

The charged particles formed are called **[[ion]]s** — charged particles made
when atoms (or groups of atoms) lose or gain electrons. A positive ion is a
**[[cation]]** (for example Na⁺); a negative ion is an **[[anion]]** (for
example Cl⁻). When an ionic compound forms, electrons are transferred from a
metal atom to a non-metal atom, and the compound is held together by strong
**[[electrostatic-attraction]]** — the attraction between the positive and
negative ions just formed (spec 1.41's whole idea in one line).

:::key-point
The book's definitions of record (chunks 0307 + 0308): *electrostatic
attraction simply means that positively charged particles attract negatively
charged particles*; and ionic bonding is often shown using **dot-and-cross
diagrams**, where the transferred electrons are drawn as dots for one atom and
crosses for the other. There is absolutely no difference between the electrons
in reality — dots and crosses only show which atom each electron came from
(two colours of dots, or two colours of crosses, would serve equally well).
:::

:::exam-hint
The book's hint (chunk 0304) protects you from the classic over-generalisation:
*there are one or two exceptions — ionic compounds that do not contain a
metal, for example those containing the ammonium ion (such as NH₄Cl,
(NH₄)₂SO₄)*. So "contains a metal" is a good recognition rule but never a
definition. In "state whether the bonding in X is ionic" questions, ammonium
salts are ionic despite having no metal — the examiners have used this exact
trap.
:::

:::misconception
"Chlorine turns negative because it gains a proton." Protons never move in
chemistry — they are the fixed identity of the nucleus. Gain or loss of
**electrons** is the only charge-changing move: Cl has 17 protons and 17
electrons; Cl⁻ has 17 protons and 18 electrons. The same discipline explains
the sodium side: Na⁺ is still sodium (11 protons) — it has simply lost one of
its 11 electrons.
:::
