Sodium chloride is the flagship example: an **[[ion]]ic** compound formed
when a metal combines with a non-metal. You can usually recognise ionic
compounds because they contain a metal — magnesium oxide, calcium fluoride
and zinc bromide all follow the metal-plus-non-metal pattern. Watch for the
ammonium exceptions flagged below, so the rule never gets over-learned.

Here is the formation story for NaCl, one step at a time. A sodium atom has
electronic configuration [2, 8, 1] — one outer electron. A chlorine atom has
[2, 8, 7] — room for exactly one more. Chlorine attracts electrons more
strongly than sodium does, so **one electron is transferred** from the
sodium's outer shell to the chlorine's outer shell. Losing an electron
leaves sodium with one more proton than electron: charge 1+. Gaining one
leaves chlorine with one more electron than proton: charge 1−.

:::key-point
Two anchors for the mechanism. (1) The formation reaction is a
simplification — in reality you don't react sodium *atoms* with chlorine
*atoms* but with chlorine molecules, Cl₂; the atom-level story is the model,
not the laboratory recipe. (2) You can check the 1+ charge from the Periodic
Table: sodium has atomic number 11, so 11 protons give an 11+ nucleus; the
atom's 11 electrons cancel it to neutral, but the ion has only 10 electrons
— 11+ against 10− leaves an overall **1+**.
:::

The charged particles formed are called **[[ion]]s** — charged particles
made when atoms (or groups of atoms) lose or gain electrons. A positive ion
is a **[[cation]]** (for example Na⁺); a negative ion is an **[[anion]]**
(for example Cl⁻). When an ionic compound forms, electrons are transferred
from a metal atom to a non-metal atom, and the compound is held together by
strong **[[electrostatic-attraction]]** — the attraction between the
positive and negative ions just formed (spec 1.41's whole idea in one line).

:::key-point
Two definitions of record. *Electrostatic attraction* simply means that
positively charged particles attract negatively charged particles. And
ionic bonding is often shown using **dot-and-cross diagrams**, where the
transferred electrons are drawn as dots for one atom and crosses for the
other. There is absolutely no difference between the electrons in reality —
dots and crosses only show which atom each electron came from (two colours
of dots, or two colours of crosses, would serve equally well).
:::

:::exam-hint
One classic trap lives here: *there are one or two exceptions — ionic
compounds that do not contain a metal, for example those containing the
ammonium ion (such as NH₄Cl, (NH₄)₂SO₄)*. So "contains a metal" is a good
recognition rule but never a definition. In "state whether the bonding in X
is ionic" questions, ammonium salts are ionic despite having no metal — the
examiners have used this exact trap.
:::

:::misconception
"Chlorine turns negative because it gains a proton." Protons never move in
chemistry — they are the fixed identity of the nucleus. Gain or loss of
**electrons** is the only charge-changing move: Cl has 17 protons and 17
electrons; Cl⁻ has 17 protons and 18 electrons. The same discipline explains
the sodium side: Na⁺ is still sodium (11 protons) — it has simply lost one
of its 11 electrons.
:::
