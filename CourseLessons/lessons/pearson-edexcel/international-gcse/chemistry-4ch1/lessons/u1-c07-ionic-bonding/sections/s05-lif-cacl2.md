The book's "other examples" section pairs a 1+/1− case with a 2+/1− case, so
you see the *same* transfer logic producing different formulas. Printed
heading provenance: the book pages this under "CALCIUM CHLORIDE", but the
chunk (and the printed page) actually runs lithium fluoride first (Figure
7.9), then calcium chloride (Figure 7.10) — both are carried here in book
order.

**Lithium fluoride, LiF.** The lithium atom has 1 outer electron that is
easily lost; fluorine has space to receive exactly one. One electron is
transferred from lithium to fluorine, giving Li⁺ and F⁻. Lithium fluoride is
held together by the strong [[electrostatic-attraction]] between positive
Li⁺ ions and negative F⁻ ions. One of each — the simplest possible ratio.

**Calcium chloride, CaCl₂.** The calcium atom [2, 8, 8, 2] has **two**
outer electrons, but each chlorine atom [2, 8, 7] has room to take only
**one**. You therefore need **two chlorines for every calcium**: the two
electrons are transferred from one calcium atom to two separate chlorine
atoms, one each. The formula is CaCl₂ — and because the Ca²⁺ ion is doubly
charged, the electrostatic attractions holding the ions together are very
strong.

:::key-point
The general law hiding in these two examples (chunk 0314): the *ratio* in
the formula is set by how many electrons must move and how many each
partner can accept or lose. Li gives 1, F takes 1 → LiF. Ca gives 2, Cl
takes 1 each → two Cl per Ca → CaCl₂. Section 6 turns this counting law
into the general formula shortcut.
:::

Note also what the diagrams do *not* show: CaCl₂'s diagram (Figure 7.10)
draws one calcium and two chlorines because that is the *ratio*, not
because a CaCl₂ unit floats around as a thing. Like every ionic compound,
calcium chloride is a giant lattice (section 12) — the dot-and-cross
diagram is the accounting for one calcium's two electrons.

:::exam-hint
For a "draw the dot-and-cross diagram for calcium chloride" question (1.40),
the mark scheme wants: calcium with empty outer shell labelled 2+, **two**
chlorine outer shells each labelled 1− and each showing eight outer
electrons (one of them a cross from calcium). The classic error is drawing
one chlorine receiving two electrons — a chlorine outer shell holds only
eight, and the 1− charge says it took one. Two transfer arrows to two
separate ions is the correct picture.
:::

:::misconception
"Calcium shares its two electrons, one with each chlorine." Sharing is
*covalent* bonding (Chapter 8); here the electrons **transfer** and stay —
each chlorine walks away with both of its new electrons permanently, and
the calcium is left 2+. The tell is in the products: charged ions (Ca²⁺,
2 Cl⁻), which is why the compound conducts when molten and needs the
electrostatic-lattice description of the sections that follow.
:::
