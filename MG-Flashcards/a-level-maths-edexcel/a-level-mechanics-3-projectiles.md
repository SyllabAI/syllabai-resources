## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** List 2 physical factors ignored in the standard particle model of a projectile.

**Back:** Air resistanceDimensions of the object

**Choices:**

- Air resistanceDimensions of the object ✔
  - _why:_ The standard particle model treats the projectile as a point mass and neglects air resistance, so these two physical factors are ignored.
- GravityMass of the object
  - _why:_ Gravity is included as the projectile’s constant downward acceleration, while mass is not usually needed once the forces are modelled. The ignored factors are air resistance and the object’s dimensions.
- Initial speedLaunch angle
  - _why:_ Initial speed and launch angle are essential starting conditions for the motion, not assumptions omitted by the model. The model ignores air resistance and the dimensions of the object.
- Horizontal velocityVertical velocity
  - _why:_ The velocity components describe the projectile’s motion and are used in the equations. The particle model instead ignores air resistance and the physical dimensions of the object.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the horizontal acceleration of a projectile modeled as zero?

**Back:** There are no horizontal forces acting on the particle after launch.

**Choices:**

- There are no horizontal forces acting on the particle after launch. ✔
  - _why:_ With no horizontal force after launch, Newton’s second law gives zero horizontal acceleration, so the horizontal velocity remains constant throughout the flight.
- There are no vertical forces acting on the particle after launch.
  - _why:_ Gravity is a vertical force and causes the projectile’s downward acceleration. The horizontal acceleration is zero because there are no horizontal forces acting after launch.
- The particle has no velocity acting on it after launch.
  - _why:_ A projectile continues moving with its initial velocity components after launch. Its horizontal acceleration is zero specifically because no horizontal force acts, not because its velocity disappears.
- The particle has constant vertical velocity after launch.
  - _why:_ The vertical velocity changes because gravity produces constant downward acceleration. The horizontal velocity is constant because no horizontal force acts on the particle.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** A particle is launched with speed $U$ at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. What is its initial vertical velocity component $u_y$?

**Back:** $$U
sin\theta$

**Choices:**

- $$U
sin\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ Resolving the initial velocity vector vertically gives the opposite component in the right-angled triangle, so the initial vertical velocity is $U\sin\theta$.
- $$U
cos\theta$
  - _why:_ Using cosine gives the component adjacent to the launch angle, which is horizontal. The vertical component is opposite the angle and therefore uses sine: $U\sin\theta$.
- $$\frac{U}{\sin\theta}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by sine is not the rule for resolving this velocity vector and gives the wrong dependence on the angle. The vertical component is found by multiplying $U$ by $\sin\theta$.
- $$U\tan\theta$
  - _why:_ Tangent relates the two velocity components rather than directly resolving the speed into the vertical component. The correct vertical component is $U\sin\theta$.

**Back:** $$U

**Choices:**

- $$U
cos\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ Resolving the initial velocity vector horizontally gives the component adjacent to the launch angle, so the initial horizontal velocity is $U\cos\theta$.
- $$U
sin\theta$
  - _why:_ Sine gives the component opposite the launch angle, which is vertical. The horizontal component is adjacent to the angle and is therefore $U\cos\theta$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Which variable links the horizontal and vertical SUVAT equations in a projectile problem?

**Back:** Time ($t$)

**Choices:**

- Time ($t$) ✔
  - _why:_ The same elapsed time $t$ describes both the horizontal and vertical motion, allowing the two independent SUVAT equations to be connected in a projectile problem.
- Distance ($s$)
  - _why:_ Horizontal and vertical distances generally differ, so distance does not directly link the two equations. The common variable is the elapsed time $t$ since launch.
- Acceleration ($a$)
  - _why:_ The horizontal and vertical accelerations are different: horizontal acceleration is zero while vertical acceleration is $-g$. The variable shared by both equations is time $t$.
- Velocity ($v$)
  - _why:_ The horizontal and vertical velocity components are generally different, so velocity is not the single linking variable. Both equations use the same elapsed time $t$.
