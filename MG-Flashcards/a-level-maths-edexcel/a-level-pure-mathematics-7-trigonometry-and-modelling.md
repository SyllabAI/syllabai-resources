## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the periods of the three main trigonometric functions?

**Back:** $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)$\tan x$: $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$)

**Choices:**

- $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)$\tan x$: $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$) ✔
  - _why:_ The sine and cosine graphs repeat every $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$ radians), while the tangent graph repeats every $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$ radians).
- $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$)$\tan x$: $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)
  - _why:_ The periods have been swapped between the function groups. Tangent repeats every $180^\circ$, whereas sine and cosine repeat every $360^\circ$.
- $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)$\tan x$: $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)
  - _why:_ All three functions do not have the same period: tangent repeats twice as often as sine and cosine. The correct periods are $360^\circ$ for sine and cosine and $180^\circ$ for tangent.
- $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: $180^\circ$ (or $2\pi$)$\tan x$: $360^\circ$ (or $\pi$)
  - _why:_ This reverses the usual radian conversions. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians, so sine and cosine have period $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$), while tangent has period $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$).

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** State the compound-angle formula for $\sin(A+B)$.

**Back:** $$\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$

**Choices:**

- $$\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$ ✔
  - _why:_ The sine addition identity combines the two cross-products with a positive sign: $\sin(A+B)=\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B$.
- $$\sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B$
  - _why:_ The subtraction sign changes the identity to $\sin(A-B)$. For a sum of angles, both cross-products are added, giving $\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$.
- $$\cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$
  - _why:_ Using matching trigonometric functions gives the cosine difference identity, $\cos(A-B)$. The sine addition formula uses cross-products: $\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$.
- $$\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$
  - _why:_ This is the compound-angle identity for $\cos(A+B)$, not $\sin(A+B)$. The sine formula uses cross-products joined by addition: $\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** State the compound-angle formula for $\cos(A+B)$.

**Back:** $$\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$

**Choices:**

- $$\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$ ✔
  - _why:_ The cosine addition identity uses the products of matching trigonometric functions, with subtraction between them: $\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B$.
- $$\cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$
  - _why:_ The positive sign makes this the identity for $\cos(A-B)$. For $\cos(A+B)$, the second product is subtracted: $\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$.
- $$\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$
  - _why:_ This is the sine addition identity, $\sin(A+B)$, because it contains cross-products. The cosine addition identity is $\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$.
- $$\cos A\sin B - \sin A\cos B$
  - _why:_ This cross-product expression equals $\sin(B-A)$, so it comes from a sine difference identity. Cosine addition requires matching products: $\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** State the compound-angle formula for $\tan(A+B)$.

**Back:** $$\frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the sine addition formula by the cosine addition formula gives $\tan(A+B)=\frac{\tan A+\tan B}{1-\tan A\tan B}$.
- $$\frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 + \tan A\tan B}$
  - _why:_ The plus sign in the denominator belongs to the tangent subtraction formula. For tangent addition, the denominator contains $1-\tan A\tan B$.
- $$\frac{\tan A - \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}$
  - _why:_ The numerator for tangent addition is the sum of the two tangents. A difference corresponds to subtracting angles, not adding them.
- $$\frac{\tan A - \tan B}{1 + \tan A\tan B}$
  - _why:_ Both signs have been changed from the addition formula. Tangent addition requires a sum in the numerator and a difference in the denominator.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** The double-angle identity for sine is $\sin 2A \equiv \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The double-angle identity for sine is $\sin 2A \equiv \boxed{2\sin A\cos A}$.

**Choices:**

- $$2\sin A\cos A$ ✔
  - _why:_ The identity follows from the sine compound-angle formula: $\sin(A+A)=\sin A\cos A+\cos A\sin A=2\sin A\cos A$.
- $$\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A$
  - _why:_ This expression is the double-angle identity for cosine, not sine. Sine uses the product $2\sin A\cos A$, whereas cosine involves squared sine and cosine terms.
- $$\sin A\cos A$
  - _why:_ The factor of $2$ has been omitted. The two equal cross-products from expanding $\sin(A+A)$ combine to give $2\sin A\cos A$.
- $$\frac{2\sin A}{\cos A}$
  - _why:_ This uses a tangent-style quotient and cannot represent the sine double angle. The correct identity is a product, $2\sin A\cos A$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** List the three identities for $\cos 2A$.

**Back:** $\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A$$2\cos^2 A - 1$$1 - 2\sin^2 A$

**Choices:**

- $\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A$$2\cos^2 A - 1$$1 - 2\sin^2 A$ ✔
  - _why:_ The three equivalent forms come from applying $\sin^2 A+\cos^2 A=1$ to $\cos^2 A-\sin^2 A$, eliminating either the sine or cosine squared term.
- $\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A$$2\cos^2 A - 1$$1 + 2\sin^2 A$
  - _why:_ The final form has the wrong sign pattern. Substituting $\cos^2 A=1-\sin^2 A$ gives $1-2\sin^2 A$, not $1+2\sin^2 A$.
- $\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A$$2\sin^2 A - 1$$1 - 2\sin^2 A$
  - _why:_ The second form incorrectly uses $2\sin^2 A-1$, which is the negative of the required rearrangement. The equivalent cosine form is $2\cos^2 A-1$.
- $\cos^2 A + \sin^2 A$$2\cos^2 A - 1$$1 - 2\sin^2 A$
  - _why:_ The first expression has been changed to a sum, but $\cos 2A$ is a difference of squares. The Pythagorean identity then produces the two stated alternatives.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** The double-angle identity for tangent is $\tan 2A \equiv \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The double-angle identity for tangent is $\tan 2A \equiv \boxed{\frac{2\tan A}{1 - \tan^2 A}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{2\tan A}{1 - \tan^2 A}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Substituting $2A$ into the tangent addition formula gives $\tan 2A=\frac{\tan A+\tan A}{1-\tan^2 A}=\frac{2\tan A}{1-\tan^2 A}$.
- $$\frac{2\tan A}{1 + \tan^2 A}$
  - _why:_ The denominator sign is reversed. A plus sign occurs in the denominator for the tangent subtraction identity, whereas doubling an angle gives $1-\tan^2 A$.
- $$\frac{\tan A}{1 - \tan^2 A}$
  - _why:_ The numerator must contain twice $\tan A$, because the two equal tangent terms are added. Omitting the factor of $2$ loses one of those terms.
- $$\frac{2\tan A}{1 - A^2}$
  - _why:_ The denominator should contain the square of $\tan A$, not the square of $A$. The double-angle formula is formed from $\tan A\tan A=\tan^2 A$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** State the triple-angle identity for $\sin 3\theta$.

**Back:** $$3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$

**Choices:**

- $$3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ The triple-angle identity follows by expanding $\sin(2\theta+\theta)$ and simplifying with $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$, giving $3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta$.
- $$3\sin\theta - 4\sin^2\theta$
  - _why:_ The coefficient and cubic term are incorrect. Expanding the compound angle produces three copies of $\sin\theta$ and a subtraction of four times $\sin^3\theta$.
- $$3\sin\theta + 4\sin^3\theta$
  - _why:_ The sign of the cubic term is wrong. The simplified triple-angle identity has $-4\sin^3\theta$, not a positive cubic term.
- $$4\sin\theta - 3\sin^3\theta$
  - _why:_ This confuses the sine triple-angle identity with a related cosine form. The sine expression uses a linear sine term and a cubic sine term with coefficients $3$ and $-4$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** State the triple-angle identity for $\cos 3\theta$.

**Back:** $$4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$

**Choices:**

- $$4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ The triple-angle identity follows from expanding $\cos(2\theta+\theta)$ and simplifying with $\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta$.
- $$3\cos\theta - 4\cos^3\theta$
  - _why:_ Reversing the terms gives the negative of the required expression, corresponding to $-\cos 3\theta$ rather than $\cos 3\theta$.
- $$3\cos^3\theta - 4\cos\theta$
  - _why:_ The coefficient of the cubic term is 4, while the linear term has coefficient 3; confusing these coefficients produces an incorrect identity.
- $$4\cos^3\theta + 3\cos\theta$
  - _why:_ This expression uses the wrong sign on the linear term. The correct triple-angle identity has a positive cubic term and a negative linear term.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving an equation like $\sin 3x = 0.5$ for $0 \leq x < 360^\circ$, why must you enlarge the interval for $u = 3x$?

**Back:** To avoid missing solutions that occur in later cycles of the expanded range.

**Choices:**

- To avoid missing solutions that occur in later cycles of the expanded range. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $u=3x$, the original interval becomes $0\leq u<1080^\circ$. Checking all three $360^\circ$ cycles prevents valid solutions from being omitted.
- To keep u within the original interval so the equation has fewer solutions.
  - _why:_ The substitution changes the upper bound from $360^\circ$ to $1080^\circ$, so restricting $u$ to the original interval discards valid values of $x$.
- To change the period of sine so that it matches the original interval.
  - _why:_ The interval must be enlarged because multiplying $x$ by 3 enlarges its range; changing the sine function's period is not the reason for the procedure.
- To remove extra solutions introduced by multiplying the angle by 3.
  - _why:_ The substitution does not create extra solutions that should be removed. It requires checking later cycles because those cycles contain genuine solutions for the original variable.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Define the reciprocal functions $\sec x$ and $\csc x$.

**Back:** $\sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}$$\csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}$

**Choices:**

- $\sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}$$\csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Secant is the reciprocal of cosine, while cosecant is the reciprocal of sine. Each reciprocal is defined where its denominator is non-zero.
- $\sec x = \frac{1}{\sin x}$$\csc x = \frac{1}{\cos x}$
  - _why:_ The two reciprocal functions have been interchanged. Secant corresponds to cosine and cosecant corresponds to sine, so both denominators are reversed here.
- $\sec x = \frac{1}{\sec x}$$\csc x = \frac{1}{\csc x}$
  - _why:_ These equations incorrectly make each function equal to the reciprocal of itself. Secant and cosecant are reciprocals of cosine and sine respectively, not of secant and cosecant.
- $\sec x = \cos x$$\csc x = \sin x$
  - _why:_ The reciprocal relationship has been omitted. Secant and cosecant are not equal directly to cosine and sine; they are their multiplicative reciprocals.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard procedure for proving a trigonometric identity?

**Back:** Simplify the more complicated side until it matches the other side (do not treat it as an equation to solve).

**Choices:**

- Simplify the more complicated side until it matches the other side (do not treat it as an equation to solve). ✔
  - _why:_ An identity is true for every permitted value, so simplifying one side is sufficient. Starting with the more complicated side and reaching the other proves equivalence without circular algebra.
- Simplify the less complicated side until it matches the other side (do not treat it as an equation to solve).
  - _why:_ Starting with the simpler side is not the standard efficient approach and may provide no meaningful simplification. The more complicated side should be reduced to it.
- Set both sides equal to zero and solve for the values of the variable.
  - _why:_ Treating an identity as an equation can lead to solving for particular values rather than proving it for all allowed values. The algebra should simplify one side identically.
- Rearrange both sides simultaneously until they produce a common numerical value.
  - _why:_ Combining both sides through simultaneous rearrangement can obscure whether an equivalent statement has been established. Simplifying one side avoids circular reasoning.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the amplitude $R$ calculated when converting $a\sin x + b\cos x$ to R-form?

**Back:** $$R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$

**Choices:**

- $$R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Matching coefficients after expanding the R-form gives $a=R\cos\alpha$ and $b=R\sin\alpha$. Squaring and adding eliminates $\alpha$, giving $R^2=a^2+b^2$.
- $$R = \sqrt{a + b}$
  - _why:_ Adding the coefficients before taking a square root ignores the right-triangle relationship between them. The amplitude depends on the sum of their squares, not their simple sum.
- $$R = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the squared coefficients is not valid because both coefficient contributions determine the amplitude. Their squares must be added before taking the square root.
- $$R = |a| + |b|$
  - _why:_ Adding the absolute coefficient values treats the contributions linearly rather than as perpendicular components. The correct magnitude is found using Pythagoras.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expansion of $R\sin(x+\alpha)$ is $\text{[...]} + R\sin\alpha\cos x$.

**Back:** The expansion of $R\sin(x+\alpha)$ is $\boxed{R\cos\alpha\sin x} + R\sin\alpha\cos x$.

**Choices:**

- $$R\cos\alpha\sin x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $\sin(x+\alpha)=\sin x\cos\alpha+\cos x\sin\alpha$ and multiplying by $R$ gives the missing first term.
- $$R\sin\alpha\sin x$
  - _why:_ This swaps the sine and cosine factors from the addition formula. The coefficient of $\sin x$ must contain $\cos\alpha$, so the correct term is $R\cos\alpha\sin x$.
- $$R\sin\alpha\cos x$
  - _why:_ This is the second term from the expansion, not the coefficient of $\sin x$. The first term requires $\cos\alpha$ alongside $\sin x$.
- $$-R\cos\alpha\sin x$
  - _why:_ The sign is not changed in the sine addition formula, and this term also places the wrong trigonometric factor with $\sin x$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expansion of $R\sin(x+\alpha)$ is $R\cos\alpha\sin x + \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The expansion of $R\sin(x+\alpha)$ is $R\cos\alpha\sin x + \boxed{R\sin\alpha\cos x}$.

**Choices:**

- $$R\sin\alpha\cos x$ ✔
  - _why:_ The sine addition formula contains $\cos x\sin\alpha$ as its second product. Multiplying by $R$ gives the missing term exactly.
- $$R\sin\alpha\sin x$
  - _why:_ This pairs $\sin\alpha$ with $\sin x$, but the addition formula pairs it with $\cos x$. The correct second term is $R\sin\alpha\cos x$.
- $$R\cos\alpha\sin x$
  - _why:_ This term belongs first in the expansion and uses the factor $\cos\alpha$. It cannot supply the second product involving $\cos x$.
- $$-R\sin\alpha\cos x$
  - _why:_ The negative sign belongs to the cosine subtraction formula, not the sine addition formula. The second term here is positive and contains $\sin\alpha\cos x$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Which identity for $\cos 2x$ should be used to solve an equation that also contains $\sin x$ terms?

**Back:** $$\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$

**Choices:**

- $$\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$ ✔
  - _why:_ This form expresses $\cos 2x$ entirely in terms of $\sin x$, allowing the equation to become a polynomial in one trigonometric function.
- $$\cos 2x = 1 - 2\cos^2 x$
  - _why:_ This swaps sine and cosine in the required identity: $1-2\cos^2x$ equals $-\cos 2x$. The suitable identity is $\cos 2x=1-2\sin^2x$.
- $$\cos 2x = 2\sin^2 x - 1$
  - _why:_ This has the signs reversed and therefore equals $-\cos 2x$. For an equation containing $\sin x$, use $\cos 2x=1-2\sin^2x$.
- $$\cos 2x = 1 - \sin^2 x$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of 2 and gives $\cos^2x$, not $\cos 2x$. The correct double-angle identity is $\cos 2x=1-2\sin^2x$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** In a trigonometric model, define midline and amplitude.

**Back:** Midline: The average value (vertical center) of the wave.Amplitude: The distance from the midline to the peak.

**Choices:**

- Midline: The average value (vertical center) of the wave.Amplitude: The distance from the midline to the peak. ✔
  - _why:_ The midline is the wave’s central horizontal level, halfway between its maximum and minimum. The amplitude is the vertical distance from that level to either extreme.
- Midline: The average value (vertical center) of the wave.Amplitude: The distance from the trough to the peak.
  - _why:_ The difference between peak and trough is the full vertical range, not the amplitude; the amplitude is half that range. The midline is the wave’s average value.
- Midline: The minimum value (lowest point) of the wave.Amplitude: The maximum value reached at the peak.
  - _why:_ The minimum is a wave’s trough rather than its midline, while the peak is a maximum rather than an amplitude. The midline is the average, and amplitude is its distance to the peak.
- Midline: The horizontal center of one complete cycle.Amplitude: The distance from one peak to the next.
  - _why:_ These definitions confuse horizontal and vertical features. The midline is the average vertical value, while the amplitude is the vertical distance from the midline to a peak.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** If a daily temperature model uses the term $\cos(15t)$ where $t$ is in hours, why is the period 24 hours?

**Back:** The coefficient 15 means the angle increases by $15^\circ$ per hour; $\frac{360}{15} = 24$.

**Choices:**

- The coefficient 15 means the angle increases by $15^\circ$ per hour; $\frac{360}{15} = 24$. ✔
  - _why:_ A cosine cycle covers $360^\circ$. Since the argument advances by $15^\circ$ each hour, the time for one cycle is $360\div15=24$ hours.
- The coefficient 15 means the angle increases by $30^\circ$ per hour; $\frac{360}{30} = 12$.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 30 mistakenly uses half a cycle, $180^\circ$, instead of the full $360^\circ$ cosine cycle. The period is $360\div15=24$ hours.
- The coefficient 15 means the wave completes one cycle every 15 hours; $\frac{360}{24} = 15$.
  - _why:_ The coefficient is an angular rate, not the period itself. One full cycle requires $360^\circ$, so dividing by $15^\circ$ per hour gives 24 hours.
- The coefficient 15 means the angle reaches $180^\circ$ in 30 hours; $15 \times 2 = 30$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 2 treats $180^\circ$ as a full cosine cycle. Cosine repeats after $360^\circ$, so the correct calculation is $360\div15=24$ hours.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the maximum value of $y = 10 + 5\sin(x - 30^\circ)$?

**Back:** Set the sine term to its maximum value of 1; $y_{\max} = 10 + 5(1) = 15$.

**Choices:**

- Set the sine term to its maximum value of 1; $y_{\max} = 10 + 5(1) = 15$. ✔
  - _why:_ Since the greatest possible value of sine is 1, the oscillating term reaches $5$. Adding the midline value 10 gives a maximum of 15.
- Set the sine term to its maximum value of 0; $y_{\max} = 10 + 5(0) = 10$.
  - _why:_ Zero is the middle value of sine, not its maximum, so this finds the midline. Using the maximum sine value 1 gives $y_{\max}=10+5=15$.
- Set the sine term to its maximum value of $-1$; $y_{\max} = 10 + 5(-1) = 5$.
  - _why:_ Using $-1$ selects the minimum of sine and therefore finds the model’s minimum value. For the maximum, use $1$, giving $y_{\max}=10+5=15$.
- Set the sine term to its maximum value of 5; $y_{\max} = 10 + 5(5) = 35$.
  - _why:_ The coefficient 5 is the amplitude, not a possible sine value; sine remains between $-1$ and 1. Substituting 1 gives the correct maximum of 15.

## Card 22 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the interval for $x$ is $0 \leq x < \pi$, the interval for $u = 3x$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If the interval for $x$ is $0 \leq x < \pi$, the interval for $u = 3x$ is $\boxed{0 \leq u < 3\pi}$.

**Choices:**

- $$0 \leq u < 3\pi$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every part of $0\leq x<\pi$ by the positive number 3 preserves the inequality signs and gives $0\leq3x<3\pi$, hence this interval.
- $$0 \leq u < \frac{\pi}{3}$
  - _why:_ This divides the endpoints by 3 instead of multiplying them. Since $u=3x$, the upper bound is tripled, so the correct interval is $0\leq u<3\pi$.
- $$0 \leq u \leq 3\pi$
  - _why:_ Multiplication by 3 does not change an excluded endpoint into an included one. Because $x<\pi$, it follows that $u<3\pi$, so the upper inequality remains strict.
- $$0 \leq u < \pi$
  - _why:_ This leaves the interval unchanged despite replacing $x$ with three times its value. The endpoints must be multiplied by 3, producing $0\leq u<3\pi$.
