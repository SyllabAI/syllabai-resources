## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due [ ___ ] and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due [ ___ ].

**Back:** In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due east and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due north.

**Choices:**

- In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due east and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due north. ✔
  - _why:_ In the standard Cartesian convention, the unit vector \(\mathbf{i}\) points horizontally east and \(\mathbf{j}\) points vertically north.
- In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due north and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due east.
  - _why:_ The directions have been reversed. By convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) represents east and \(\mathbf{j}\) represents north, so this completion assigns both unit vectors incorrectly.
- In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due east and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due south.
  - _why:_ The first direction is correct, but the second is not. The standard convention uses \(\mathbf{j}\) for north, not south, so this confuses positive and negative vertical directions.
- In the usual convention, \(\mathbf{i}\) is often due west and \(\mathbf{j}\) is often due north.
  - _why:_ The second direction is correct, but \(\mathbf{i}\) points east rather than west. West is the negative \(\mathbf{i}\) direction, so this reverses the horizontal axis.

- 3 units in the negative \(\mathbf{i}\) direction4 units in the \(\mathbf{j}\) direction
  - _why:_ The negative sign applies to the \(\mathbf{j}\) component, not the \(\mathbf{i}\) component. The correct interpretation is 3 units in \(\mathbf{i}\) and 4 units in negative \(\mathbf{j}\).

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find displacement from initial and final position vectors?

**Back:** Displacement = final position - initial position.

**Choices:**

- Displacement = final position - initial position. ✔
  - _why:_ Displacement describes the change in position, so it is obtained by subtracting the initial position vector from the final position vector.
- Displacement = initial position - final position.
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction order. Initial position minus final position gives the negative of the displacement, which points in the opposite direction.
- Displacement = final position + initial position.
  - _why:_ Adding the two position vectors does not measure the change between them. Displacement requires final position minus initial position so the starting point is removed.
- Displacement = final position.
  - _why:_ This uses only the final position and ignores where the particle started. Displacement must compare both positions using final minus initial.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** The speed of a particle is the [ ___ ] of its velocity vector.

**Back:** The speed of a particle is the magnitude of its velocity vector.

**Choices:**

- magnitude ✔
  - _why:_ Velocity is a vector containing both speed and direction. Its magnitude removes the directional information and leaves the particle’s scalar speed.
- direction
  - _why:_ Direction is part of velocity but not speed. Speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector, so it has no directional component.
- component
  - _why:_ A component is only one projection of a vector and may be positive, negative, or zero. Speed uses the complete vector’s magnitude instead.
- scalar
  - _why:_ Velocity is a vector, whereas speed is a scalar. Calling speed the scalar of velocity is imprecise; the required operation is taking its magnitude.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** For constant velocity, what is the position vector at time \(t\)?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{v}t\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{v}t\). ✔
  - _why:_ With constant velocity, displacement from the initial position is \(\mathbf{v}t\). Adding this to \(\mathbf{r}_0\) gives the position at time \(t\).
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0-\mathbf{v}t\).
  - _why:_ The velocity term must be added because it represents displacement from the initial position. Subtracting it reverses the direction of motion.
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{v}t\).
  - _why:_ This omits the initial position, so it assumes the particle starts at the origin. The general expression must include \(\mathbf{r}_0\).
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{t}\).
  - _why:_ Time multiplies velocity to produce displacement; it does not divide it. The correct constant-velocity relation adds \(\mathbf{v}t\) to the initial position.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Given positions \(\mathbf{r}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2\) separated by time \(\Delta t\), how do you find constant velocity?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1}{\Delta t}\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1}{\Delta t}\). ✔
  - _why:_ Constant velocity equals displacement divided by the elapsed time. The displacement from the first position to the second is \(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1\).
- \(\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2}{\Delta t}\).
  - _why:_ This reverses the displacement, producing the velocity in the opposite direction. The correct order is final position minus initial position: \(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1\).
- \(\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_2+\mathbf{r}_1}{\Delta t}\).
  - _why:_ Adding positions does not give the displacement between them. Velocity requires their difference, divided by the elapsed time.
- \(\mathbf{v}=\left(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1\right)\Delta t\).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by time gives a displacement, not a velocity. To obtain velocity, divide the displacement \(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1\) by \(\Delta t\).

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** A bearing is measured [ ___ ] from [ ___ ].

**Back:** A bearing is measured clockwise from north.

**Choices:**

- A bearing is measured clockwise from north. ✔
  - _why:_ A bearing is defined as a clockwise angle measured from north. This convention gives a unique three-figure direction for navigation and vector problems.
- A bearing is measured anticlockwise from north.
  - _why:_ Bearings are measured clockwise, not anticlockwise. Reversing the sense of rotation would give the opposite angular convention.
- A bearing is measured clockwise from east.
  - _why:_ The rotation is clockwise, but the reference direction is wrong. Bearings start from north, whereas east is associated with an angle of 090°.
- A bearing is measured anticlockwise from east.
  - _why:_ Both parts use the wrong convention. A bearing starts at north and is measured clockwise, rather than anticlockwise from east.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the bearing of velocity \(-4\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}\), to the nearest degree?

**Back:** \(321^\circ\) approximately.

**Choices:**

- \(321^\circ\) approximately. ✔
  - _why:_ The vector points 4 units west and 5 units north. Its angle west of north is \(\tan^{-1}(4/5)\approx38.7^\circ\), giving bearing \(360^\circ-38.7^\circ\approx321^\circ\).
- \(39^\circ\) approximately.
  - _why:_ This is approximately the angle measured from the east-west axis, not a bearing from north. Bearings require clockwise measurement from north, giving \(321^\circ\).
- \(309^\circ\) approximately.
  - _why:_ Using \(\tan^{-1}(5/4)\) instead of \(\tan^{-1}(4/5)\) measures the wrong acute angle. The correct bearing is \(360^\circ-\tan^{-1}(4/5)\approx321^\circ\).
- \(141^\circ\) approximately.
  - _why:_ This places the vector in the south-east quadrant, as though the signs of both components had been mishandled. The vector is north-west, with bearing approximately \(321^\circ\).

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** With constant acceleration, what vector equation links \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(t\)?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t\). ✔
  - _why:_ Under constant acceleration, the change in velocity after time $t$ is $\mathbf{a}t$. Adding this change to the initial velocity $\mathbf{u}$ gives the final velocity $\mathbf{v}$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{a}t\).
  - _why:_ Subtracting $\mathbf{a}t$ reverses the effect of acceleration. With the acceleration vector defined in its actual direction, the velocity change is added: $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t\).
  - _why:_ The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ belongs to the displacement term for constant acceleration, not the velocity equation. Integrating constant acceleration once gives $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t^2\).
  - _why:_ Acceleration contributes a velocity change proportional to $t$, not $t^2$. The squared-time term appears when velocity is integrated to obtain position, so $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** With constant acceleration, what is the position vector at time \(t\)?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2\). ✔
  - _why:_ Integrating $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t$ gives displacement $\mathbf{u}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$. Adding the initial position $\mathbf{r}_0$ gives the position vector at time $t$.
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t+\mathbf{a}t^2\).
  - _why:_ The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ is lost if the acceleration term is not integrated correctly. Since $\int \mathbf{a}t\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$, the correct coefficient is one half.
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2\).
  - _why:_ The sign of the acceleration vector should not be changed arbitrarily. Integrating the given acceleration produces $+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$; any direction is already encoded in $\mathbf{a}$.
- \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t^2+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t\).
  - _why:_ Initial velocity contributes displacement proportional to $t$, whereas constant acceleration contributes displacement proportional to $t^2$. The correct position is $\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{u}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** A direction vector gives the [ ___ ], not necessarily the velocity itself.

**Back:** A direction vector gives the ratio of components, not necessarily the velocity itself.

**Choices:**

- ratio of components ✔
  - _why:_ Parallel vectors are scalar multiples, so their corresponding components remain in a fixed proportion. A direction vector specifies this proportion without fixing the vector’s magnitude.
- magnitude of velocity
  - _why:_ A direction vector does not determine how large the velocity is; the speed is needed for that. It specifies the ratio between corresponding components of the velocity.
- unit vector
  - _why:_ A direction vector need not have magnitude one, so it is not automatically a unit vector. Its essential information is the ratio of its components.
- acceleration components
  - _why:_ Acceleration describes the rate of change of velocity and cannot be inferred from a direction vector alone. The direction vector instead determines the ratio of velocity components.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you convert speed \(s\) in direction vector \(\mathbf{d}\) into velocity?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{v}=s\dfrac{\mathbf{d}}{|\mathbf{d}|}\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{v}=s\dfrac{\mathbf{d}}{|\mathbf{d}|}\). ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing $\mathbf{d}$ by its magnitude produces a unit vector in the required direction. Multiplying that unit vector by $s$ gives a velocity whose magnitude is exactly $s$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=s\mathbf{d}\).
  - _why:_ Multiplying directly by $\mathbf{d}$ gives magnitude $s|\mathbf{d}|$, not $s$, unless $\mathbf{d}$ is already a unit vector. The direction vector must first be divided by $|\mathbf{d}|$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=s|\mathbf{d}|\mathbf{d}\).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $|\mathbf{d}|$ increases the magnitude rather than normalising the direction. The required unit direction is $\mathbf{d}/|\mathbf{d}|$, which is then multiplied by $s$.
- \(\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{d}}{s|\mathbf{d}|}\).
  - _why:_ Dividing by the speed does not produce a velocity of magnitude $s$; it gives magnitude $|\mathbf{d}|/s$. Normalise $\mathbf{d}$ and then multiply by $s$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Given \(\mathbf{u}=12\mathbf{i}+16\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{v}=-5\mathbf{i}+18\mathbf{j}\) after 5 s, what is \(\mathbf{a}\)?

**Back:** \(-\frac{17}{5}\mathbf{i}+\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}\).

**Choices:**

- \(-\frac{17}{5}\mathbf{i}+\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}\). ✔
  - _why:_ Constant acceleration is the velocity change divided by time. Here $\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}=-17\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$, and dividing both components by $5$ gives the stated acceleration.
- \(\frac{17}{5}\mathbf{i}-\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}\).
  - _why:_ This reverses the velocity subtraction by calculating $\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$. Acceleration uses $(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})/5$, giving $-\frac{17}{5}\mathbf{i}+\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
- \(-17\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}\).
  - _why:_ This gives the change in velocity but omits division by the elapsed $5$ seconds. Dividing $-17\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$ by $5$ gives the correct acceleration.
- \(\frac{7}{5}\mathbf{i}+\frac{34}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}\).
  - _why:_ Adding the initial and final velocities is not how acceleration is found. Subtract the initial velocity from the final velocity, then divide by $5$, obtaining $-\frac{17}{5}\mathbf{i}+\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{j}\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** A vector perpendicular to \(\mathbf{i}\) has \(\mathbf{i}\)-component [ ___ ].

**Back:** A vector perpendicular to \(\mathbf{i}\) has \(\mathbf{i}\)-component zero.

**Choices:**

- zero ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular vectors have zero scalar product. Since $\mathbf{i}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j})=a$, perpendicularity to $\mathbf{i}$ requires the coefficient $a$ to vanish.
- one
  - _why:_ A component of one would give a non-zero scalar product with $\mathbf{i}$, so the vectors would not be perpendicular. The $\mathbf{i}$-component must be zero.
- non-zero
  - _why:_ Any non-zero $\mathbf{i}$-component creates a component parallel to $\mathbf{i}$ and a non-zero scalar product. Perpendicularity therefore requires an $\mathbf{i}$-component of zero.
- undefined
  - _why:_ The component is fully determined by the perpendicularity condition rather than being undefined. A zero scalar product with $\mathbf{i}$ forces the $\mathbf{i}$-component to be zero.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** For motion in the direction \(\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\), what relation must the velocity components satisfy?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{j}\text{-component}=2\times\mathbf{i}\text{-component}\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{j}\text{-component}=2\times\mathbf{i}\text{-component}\). ✔
  - _why:_ Any velocity in direction $\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$ is a scalar multiple $\lambda\mathbf{i}+2\lambda\mathbf{j}$. Its $\mathbf{j}$-component must therefore be twice its $\mathbf{i}$-component.
- \(\mathbf{i}\text{-component}=2\times\mathbf{j}\text{-component}\).
  - _why:_ This reverses the component ratio. The direction $\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$ has component ratio $1:2$, so the $\mathbf{j}$-component is twice the $\mathbf{i}$-component.
- \(\mathbf{j}\text{-component}=\mathbf{i}\text{-component}\).
  - _why:_ Equal components describe a direction parallel to $\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}$, not $\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$. The required component ratio is $1:2$.
- \(\mathbf{j}\text{-component}=\mathbf{i}\text{-component}+2\).
  - _why:_ The components must be proportional, not differ by a fixed additive amount. For direction $\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$, the second component equals twice the first.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** For \(\mathbf{v}=(4t-8)\mathbf{i}+(3t+1)\mathbf{j}\), when is motion in direction \(\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\)?

**Back:** \(t=\frac{17}{5}\).

**Choices:**

- \(t=\frac{17}{5}\). ✔
  - _why:_ The required direction has $\mathbf{j}$-component twice the $\mathbf{i}$-component. Solving $3t+1=2(4t-8)$ gives $3t+1=8t-16$, hence $t=17/5$.
- \(t=\frac{19}{2}\).
  - _why:_ This results from reversing the required component relationship. The correct equation is $3t+1=2(4t-8)$, because the $\mathbf{j}$-component must be twice the $\mathbf{i}$-component, giving $t=17/5$.
- \(t=3\).
  - _why:_ This comes from mishandling the constant when expanding $2(4t-8)$. The expansion is $8t-16$, so solving $3t+1=8t-16$ gives $t=17/5$.
- \(t=9\).
  - _why:_ This treats the two velocity components as equal, which would give direction $\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}$. The required ratio is $1:2$, so $3t+1=2(4t-8)$ and $t=17/5$.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find a minimum speed, minimise [ ___ ] to avoid square roots.

**Back:** To find a minimum speed, minimise the square of the speed to avoid square roots.

**Choices:**

- the square of the speed ✔
  - _why:_ Minimising the square of the speed gives the same minimum time as minimising speed because speed is non-negative, while avoiding differentiation of a square-root expression.
- the velocity of the particle
  - _why:_ Minimising velocity treats a vector as though it were a scalar and does not define minimum speed. The square of the speed should be minimised because it is scalar and avoids square roots.
- the magnitude of acceleration
  - _why:_ Minimising acceleration does not generally locate the minimum speed, especially when acceleration is constant. Instead, minimise the square of the speed, which has the same minimum as speed itself.
- the reciprocal of the speed
  - _why:_ Minimising the reciprocal reverses the optimisation: a smaller reciprocal corresponds to a larger non-zero speed. Minimise the square of the speed to preserve the required minimum without square roots.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** For \(V^2=25t^2-58t+65\), when is speed minimum?

**Back:** \(t=\frac{29}{25}\).

**Choices:**

- \(t=\frac{29}{25}\). ✔
  - _why:_ The quadratic reaches its minimum at its vertex, where $t=-b/(2a)=58/(2\times25)=29/25$. Since the coefficient of $t^2$ is positive, this is a minimum.
- \(t=\frac{29}{50}\).
  - _why:_ This results from dividing $58$ by $2$ twice when applying the vertex formula. Using $t=-b/(2a)$ gives $t=58/50=29/25$.
- \(t=\frac{58}{25}\).
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $2$ in the denominator of the quadratic vertex formula. The correct calculation is $t=58/(2\times25)=29/25$.
- \(t=-\frac{29}{25}\).
  - _why:_ The sign has been mishandled in $t=-b/(2a)$. Here $b=-58$, so $-b$ is positive and the minimum occurs at $t=29/25$.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** With constant acceleration, minimum speed occurs when velocity is [ ___ ] to acceleration, if the time is allowed.

**Back:** With constant acceleration, minimum speed occurs when velocity is perpendicular to acceleration, if the time is allowed.

**Choices:**

- perpendicular ✔
  - _why:_ Since $\frac{d}{dt}(|\mathbf{v}|^2)=2\mathbf{v}\mathbin{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}\mathbf{a}$, stationary speed occurs when the dot product is zero, meaning velocity and acceleration are perpendicular.
- parallel
  - _why:_ Parallel velocity and acceleration have a non-zero dot product, so the speed is changing rather than stationary. Minimum speed occurs when velocity is perpendicular to acceleration, provided that time is permitted.
- opposite
  - _why:_ Opposite velocity and acceleration mean the particle is slowing down, but speed has not yet necessarily reached its minimum. The minimum occurs when velocity becomes perpendicular to acceleration.
- equal
  - _why:_ Equal velocity and acceleration is dimensionally inappropriate because they have different units, and it does not identify a speed minimum. The required geometric condition is that they are perpendicular.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the position of \(B\) relative to \(A\)?

**Back:** \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A\).

**Choices:**

- \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A\). ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting the position vector of \(A\) from that of \(B\) gives the displacement from \(A\) to \(B\), which is the position of \(B\) relative to \(A\).
- \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_A-\mathbf{r}_B\).
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction gives the position of \(A\) relative to \(B\), pointing in the opposite direction. For \(B\) relative to \(A\), use \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A\).
- \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B+\mathbf{r}_A\).
  - _why:_ Adding the position vectors does not produce a displacement between the particles. Relative position is found by subtracting the reference position: \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A\).
- \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_B\).
  - _why:_ Subtracting \(B\)'s position from itself always gives the zero vector, regardless of \(A\). The required displacement from \(A\) to \(B\) is \(\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A\).

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What condition must hold for two objects to collide in 2D?

**Back:** Both components of \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\) are zero at the same time.

**Choices:**

- Both components of \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\) are zero at the same time. ✔
  - _why:_ A collision requires the objects to occupy the same horizontal and vertical coordinates simultaneously. Therefore, both components of their relative position vector must equal zero at one common time.
- Either component of \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\) is zero at any time.
  - _why:_ One zero component only shows that the objects share one coordinate, so they may still be separated in the other direction. Both relative-position components must be zero simultaneously.
- Both components of \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\) are zero at different times.
  - _why:_ The components becoming zero at different times means the objects separately share each coordinate, but never necessarily share one position. Both components must be zero at the same time.
- Both components of \(\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\) are equal at the same time.
  - _why:_ Equal relative-position components can both be non-zero, leaving a definite separation between the objects. For a collision, both components of the relative position must equal zero simultaneously.

**Front:** In variable vector kinematics, [ ___ ]{dt}\)}} and [ ___ ]{dt}\)}}.

**Back:** In variable vector kinematics, \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{r{dt}\)}} and \(\mathbf{a}=\frac{d\mathbf{v{dt}\)}}.

**Choices:**

- In variable vector kinematics, \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{r{dt}\)}} and \(\mathbf{a}=\frac{d\mathbf{v{dt}\)}}. ✔
  - _why:_ Velocity is the time derivative of position, and acceleration is the time derivative of velocity. These vector derivative relationships apply component by component in variable kinematics.
- In variable vector kinematics, \(\mathbf{r}=\frac{d\mathbf{v{dt}\)}} and \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{a{dt}\)}}.
  - _why:_ This reverses both derivative relationships. Differentiating position gives velocity and differentiating velocity gives acceleration, rather than obtaining position and velocity by differentiating later quantities.
- In variable vector kinematics, \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{r{dt}\)}} and \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{a{dt}\)}}.
  - _why:_ The first relationship is correct, but differentiating acceleration gives jerk rather than velocity. Acceleration is found by differentiating velocity: $\mathbf{a}=d\mathbf{v}/dt$.
- In variable vector kinematics, \(\mathbf{a}=\frac{d\mathbf{r{dt}\)}} and \(\mathbf{v}=\frac{d\mathbf{a{dt}\)}}.
  - _why:_ Acceleration is not the time derivative of position; that derivative is velocity. The correct chain is $\mathbf{v}=d\mathbf{r}/dt$ followed by $\mathbf{a}=d\mathbf{v}/dt$.

## Card 23 — `basic`

**Front:** When integrating in vector kinematics, how are the constants found?

**Back:** Use the given conditions.

**Choices:**

- Use the given conditions. ✔
  - _why:_ Integration introduces a constant vector, or a constant for each component. Substituting the stated initial or boundary conditions determines these constants uniquely.
- Set the constants to zero.
  - _why:_ Setting every constant to zero silently assumes zero initial values, which may not be given. The constants must instead be determined by substituting the given conditions.
- Use the derived equations.
  - _why:_ Differentiating can check an antiderivative but eliminates constants, so it cannot determine their values. The values come from applying the given initial or boundary conditions.
- Equate the vector components.
  - _why:_ Comparing vector components is part of the calculation but does not supply missing initial information. Integration constants are found by substituting the given conditions into the integrated expression.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** If given \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and asked for distance between two positions, what is the method?

**Back:** Integrate velocity to get displacement.Take the magnitude of displacement.

**Choices:**

- Integrate velocity to get displacement.Take the magnitude of displacement. ✔
  - _why:_ Integrating the velocity vector over the interval gives the displacement vector between the positions. Its magnitude is the straight-line distance separating those two positions.
- Differentiate velocity to get acceleration.Take the magnitude of acceleration.
  - _why:_ Differentiating velocity gives acceleration, whose magnitude does not measure the separation of two positions. Instead, integrate velocity to obtain displacement and then take its magnitude.
- Integrate velocity to get displacement.Take the sum of its components.
  - _why:_ Adding displacement components does not give a vector’s magnitude and can cause cancellation. After integrating velocity, use the resultant magnitude, such as $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ in two dimensions.
- Take the magnitude of velocity.Integrate it over the interval.
  - _why:_ Integrating speed gives total path length, which may exceed the straight-line distance between the two positions. Integrate velocity first, then take the magnitude of the resulting displacement.
