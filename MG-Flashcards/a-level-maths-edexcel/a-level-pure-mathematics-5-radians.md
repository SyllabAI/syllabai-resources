## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the angle in radians for a full circle ($360^\circ$)?

**Back:** $$2\pi$

**Choices:**

- $$2\pi$ ✔
  - _why:_ A full circle measures $360^\circ$, which corresponds to $2\pi$ radians because $180^\circ=\pi$ radians.
- $$\pi$
  - _why:_ This is the measure of a half circle, not a full circle. A complete revolution is twice this angle, so the correct value is $2\pi$ radians.
- $$\frac{\pi}{2}$
  - _why:_ This represents a quarter circle, since a right angle is $\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians. A full circle contains four right angles, giving $2\pi$ radians.
- $$4\pi$
  - _why:_ This is twice the angle in a full circle and would represent two complete revolutions. One complete revolution is $2\pi$ radians.

**Back:** One radian is defined as the central angle subtended by an arc where the arc length is equal to the radius.

  - _why:_ By definition, one radian is the central angle subtended by an arc whose length equals the circle’s radius.
- circumference
  - _why:_ The angle is defined using the arc’s length, not its curvature. The required quantity is the arc length, which must equal the radius.
- diameter
  - _why:_ The diameter is twice the radius, so using it would define an angle larger than one radian. The relevant quantity is the arc length.
- sector area
  - _why:_ The sector’s area is not used in the definition of a radian. The defining condition is that the arc length equals the radius.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you convert an angle from degrees to radians?

**Back:** Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$

**Choices:**

- Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ ✔
  - _why:_ There are $180$ degrees in $\pi$ radians, so multiplying a degree measure by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ changes the unit to radians.
- Multiply by $\frac{180}{\pi}$
  - _why:_ This conversion factor changes radians into degrees, so using it here reverses the required conversion. Degrees must be multiplied by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ to become radians.
- Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{360}$
  - _why:_ The factor has an extra division by two, so it would produce an angle half the correct radian measure. The required factor is $\frac{\pi}{180}$.
- Divide by $\frac{\pi}{180}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $\frac{180}{\pi}$, which converts in the opposite direction. Use multiplication by $\frac{\pi}{180}$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for arc length $s$ using radius $r$ and angle $\theta$.

**Back:** $$s = r\theta$

**Choices:**

- $$s = r\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ For an angle measured in radians, arc length equals the fraction of the full circumference represented by the angle, giving the formula $s=r\theta$.
- $$s = 2r\theta$
  - _why:_ This introduces an unnecessary factor of two. The radian definition already gives the direct relationship $s=r\theta$, without doubling the radius.
- $$s = \frac{r}{\theta}$
  - _why:_ Arc length increases with the angle, rather than decreasing as division would imply. The correct relationship is the product $s=r\theta$.
- $$s = r + \theta$
  - _why:_ Radius and angle combine multiplicatively in the arc-length formula, not additively. The correct expression is $s=r\theta$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the area of a sector $A$ using radius $r$ and angle $\theta$.

**Back:** $$A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ A sector’s area is the fraction of the circle represented by $\theta$. With $\theta$ in radians, this simplifies to $A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$.
- $$A = r\theta$
  - _why:_ This is the arc-length relationship with an extra area label, so it lacks the required factor of $r$ and the factor of one half. The sector-area formula is $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$.
- $$A = r^2\theta$
  - _why:_ The radius must be squared because area has two dimensions, and a factor of one half is also required. The correct formula is $A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$.
- $$A = 2r^2\theta$
  - _why:_ This has the right squared radius but doubles the sector area by omitting the factor of one half. The required expression is $A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$.

**Back:** RadiusRadiusArc length ($r\theta$)

  - _why:_ Sector area measures a two-dimensional region and cannot be added to lengths. The curved boundary contributes arc length, calculated as $r\theta$, alongside the two radii.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the area of a minor segment calculated conceptually?

**Back:** Sector area minus the area of the isosceles triangle formed by the radii and chord.

**Choices:**

- Sector area minus the area of the isosceles triangle formed by the radii and chord. ✔
  - _why:_ A minor segment is the part of the sector outside the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord. Subtracting the triangle’s area from the sector area gives the segment area.
- Sector area plus the area of the isosceles triangle formed by the radii and chord.
  - _why:_ The triangle lies inside the sector, so adding its area counts that region twice. The minor segment is obtained by subtracting the isosceles triangle’s area from the sector area.
- Area of the isosceles triangle formed by the radii and chord minus sector area.
  - _why:_ The triangle is smaller than the corresponding sector for a minor angle, so reversing the subtraction would produce a negative value. The correct calculation is sector area minus triangle area.
- Sector area minus the area of the equilateral triangle formed by the radii and chord.
  - _why:_ The triangle formed by two radii and a chord is generally isosceles, not necessarily equilateral. The segment calculation subtracts the area of that isosceles triangle from the sector area.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The area of a minor segment is given by the formula $A = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The area of a minor segment is given by the formula $A = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta - \sin\theta)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta - \sin\theta)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The minor segment equals sector area, $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$, minus triangle area, $\frac{1}{2}r^2\sin\theta$. Factoring gives $\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta-\sin\theta)$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta + \sin\theta)$
  - _why:_ The triangle area must be subtracted from the sector area, not added. Using a plus sign gives the combined area rather than the small region between the chord and arc.
- $$r^2(\theta - \sin\theta)$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ present in both the sector and triangle area formulas. The correct expression is $\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta-\sin\theta)$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}r(\theta - \sin\theta)$
  - _why:_ Area terms require a squared length, so the radius must appear as $r^2$. Using only $r$ gives the wrong dimensions; the correct formula contains $\frac{1}{2}r^2$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the central angle $\theta$ if given a chord of length $L$ and radius $r$?

**Back:** Bisect the chord to create a right-angled triangle and solve $\sin(\frac{\theta}{2}) = \frac{L/2}{r}$.

**Choices:**

- Bisect the chord to create a right-angled triangle and solve $\sin(\frac{\theta}{2}) = \frac{L/2}{r}$. ✔
  - _why:_ The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, producing a right-angled triangle with opposite side $L/2$ and hypotenuse $r$. Hence $\sin(\theta/2)=(L/2)/r$.
- Bisect the chord to create a right-angled triangle and solve $\cos(\frac{\theta}{2}) = \frac{L/2}{r}$.
  - _why:_ In the resulting triangle, $L/2$ is opposite the half-angle, not adjacent to it. Therefore it must be divided by the radius using sine, giving $\sin(\theta/2)=(L/2)/r$.
- Bisect the chord to create a right-angled triangle and solve $\tan(\frac{\theta}{2}) = \frac{L/2}{r}$.
  - _why:_ The radius is the hypotenuse, so the ratio involving $L/2$ and $r$ is sine rather than tangent. Tangent would require the perpendicular distance from the centre.
- Use the full chord directly and solve $\sin\theta = \frac{L}{r}$.
  - _why:_ The symmetry creates two right-angled triangles, so both the chord and central angle must be halved. The correct relationship is $\sin(\theta/2)=(L/2)/r$.

**Back:** For small angles in radians:$\sin x \approx \boxed{x}$$\tan x \approx x$

**Choices:**

- $$x$ ✔
  - _why:_ For angles measured in radians and close to zero, the sine curve is well approximated by its first Taylor term, so $\sin x\approx x$. The tangent approximation is also $x$.
- $$\frac{x^2}{2}$
  - _why:_ This is the magnitude of the second-order term associated with cosine, not the leading small-angle approximation for sine. Near zero, sine is approximately linear: $\sin x\approx x$.
- $$1 - \frac{x^2}{2}$
  - _why:_ This is the small-angle approximation for cosine, whose value starts near $1$. Sine starts at zero with gradient $1$, so its leading approximation is $x$.
- $$\tan x$
  - _why:_ Replacing sine with tangent does not provide the required simplified approximation and leaves the expression unchanged. For small radian angles, the specific approximation is $\sin x\approx x$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the small angle approximation for $\cos x$?

**Back:** $$1 - \frac{x^2}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$1 - \frac{x^2}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The Taylor expansion of cosine about $x=0$ begins $1-\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots$. For small radian angles, later powers are negligible, leaving this approximation.
- $$1 + \frac{x^2}{2}$
  - _why:_ The quadratic term in cosine has a negative sign, reflecting that cosine decreases from $1$ near zero. The correct approximation is $1-\frac{x^2}{2}$.
- $$x - \frac{x^3}{6}$
  - _why:_ This is the beginning of the Taylor expansion for sine, an odd function that starts at zero. Cosine is even and begins with $1-\frac{x^2}{2}$.
- $$\frac{x^2}{2}$
  - _why:_ This keeps only the magnitude of cosine’s second-order correction and omits the constant term $1$. Near zero, cosine is close to $1$, so the approximation is $1-\frac{x^2}{2}$.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Replacing trigonometric functions with their small-angle expansions turns expressions into algebraic forms such as polynomials or linear terms, making manipulation and derivation much easier.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for percentage error between an approximation and a true value.

**Back:** $$\frac{|\text{approximation} - \text{true value}|}{|\text{true value}|} \times 100$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{|\text{approximation} - \text{true value}|}{|\text{true value}|} \times 100$ ✔
  - _why:_ Percentage error compares the absolute difference between the approximation and true value with the magnitude of the true value, then multiplies by 100 to express the result as a percentage.
- $$\frac{|\text{approximation} - \text{true value}|}{|\text{approximation}|} \times 100$
  - _why:_ Using the approximation in the denominator makes the result dependent on the estimated value rather than the reference value. Percentage error is measured relative to the true value.
- $$\frac{|\text{approximation} + \text{true value}|}{|\text{true value}|} \times 100$
  - _why:_ Adding the two values does not measure their discrepancy and can produce a large result even when they are close. The numerator must be their absolute difference.
- $$\frac{|\text{approximation} - \text{true value}|}{|\text{true value}|}$
  - _why:_ This gives the relative error as a decimal, not the percentage error. Multiplication by 100 is required to convert the ratio into a percentage.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** When applying small angle approximations to $\cos(2\theta)$, what is the result?

**Back:** $$1 - \frac{(2\theta)^2}{2} = 1 - 2\theta^2$

**Choices:**

- $$1 - \frac{(2\theta)^2}{2} = 1 - 2\theta^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ For small angles, $\cos u \approx 1 - \frac{u^2}{2}$. Substituting $u=2\theta$ gives $1 - \frac{4\theta^2}{2}$, which simplifies to the stated result.
- $$1 - \frac{(2\theta)^2}{2} = 1 - \theta^2$
  - _why:_ The square of $2\theta$ is $4\theta^2$, so dividing by 2 leaves $2\theta^2$, not $\theta^2$. This loses a factor of 2 during simplification.
- $$1 - \frac{(2\theta)^2}{2} = 1 - 2\theta$
  - _why:_ The small-angle cosine expansion contains a squared angle, not a linear term. Replacing the squared term with $2\theta$ confuses the cosine approximation with a first-order approximation.
- $$1 - \frac{(2\theta)^2}{2} = 1 - 4\theta^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring $2\theta$ produces $4\theta^2$, but the expansion divides this by 2. Omitting that division gives a coefficient twice as large as the correct one.
