## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a Cartesian equation compared to parametric equations?

**Back:** It connects $x$ and $y$ directly in a single equation without a third variable.

**Choices:**

- It connects $x$ and $y$ directly in a single equation without a third variable. ✔
  - _why:_ A Cartesian equation relates the two coordinates directly, such as $y=x^2$, rather than defining each coordinate separately in terms of a parameter.
- It defines $x$ and $y$ separately using two equations involving a third variable.
  - _why:_ Defining both coordinates using a third variable describes parametric equations. A Cartesian equation instead connects $x$ and $y$ directly in one equation.
- It connects $x$ and $y$ linearly in a single equation without any squared terms.
  - _why:_ Restricting an equation to linear terms is unrelated to whether it is Cartesian. A Cartesian equation directly connects $x$ and $y$ and may be linear or nonlinear.
- It defines $x$ and $y$ independently using a different variable for each coordinate.
  - _why:_ Using separate independent variables does not produce a relation between the coordinates. A Cartesian equation directly connects $x$ and $y$ without introducing a third variable.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In parametric equations, the variable used to define $x$ and $y$ (usually $t$ or $\theta$) is called the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In parametric equations, the variable used to define $x$ and $y$ (usually $t$ or $\theta$) is called the parameter.

**Choices:**

- parameter ✔
  - _why:_ A parameter is the auxiliary variable from which both coordinates are defined. Different permitted parameter values generate the points lying on the parametric curve.
- coefficient
  - _why:_ A coefficient is a fixed multiplier of a variable, not the varying quantity used to define both coordinates. That auxiliary variable is called the parameter.
- constant
  - _why:_ A constant has a fixed value, whereas the defining variable varies to trace the curve. The variable used to define $x$ and $y$ is the parameter.
- gradient
  - _why:_ The gradient measures the slope of a curve or line rather than generating its coordinates. The variable used to define both coordinates is called the parameter.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the standard 2-step process for eliminating the parameter $t$.

**Back:** Rearrange one equation to make $t$ the subjectSubstitute this expression for $t$ into the other equation

**Choices:**

- Rearrange one equation to make $t$ the subjectSubstitute this expression for $t$ into the other equation ✔
  - _why:_ Expressing $t$ in terms of one coordinate and substituting into the other equation removes $t$, leaving a direct Cartesian relationship between $x$ and $y$.
- Rearrange one equation to make $x$ the subjectSubstitute this expression for $x$ into the other equation
  - _why:_ Making $x$ the subject does not necessarily remove $t$, and substituting for $x$ can simply reproduce the original parametric relationship. Instead, isolate $t$ and substitute its expression.
- Rearrange both equations to make $x$ and $y$ the subjectsEquate the resulting expressions for $x$ and $y$
  - _why:_ Equating the expressions for $x$ and $y$ wrongly treats the two coordinates as equal. The valid method is to isolate $t$ in one equation and substitute into the other.
- Differentiate both equations with respect to $t$ to find the derivativesDivide the derivative of $y$ by the derivative of $x$
  - _why:_ Differentiating changes the equations and is used to find gradients, not to obtain the Cartesian equation. Eliminate $t$ by isolating it and substituting into the other equation.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the x-intercepts of a parametric curve?

**Back:** Set $y=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $x$.

**Choices:**

- Set $y=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ At an x-intercept, the point lies on the x-axis, so its $y$-coordinate is zero. The corresponding parameter values then determine the required $x$-coordinates.
- Set $x=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $y$.
  - _why:_ Setting $x=0$ finds points on the y-axis, so this procedure gives y-intercepts. For x-intercepts, set $y=0$ and calculate the corresponding $x$-values.
- Set $x=0$ and $y=0$, then solve both equations simultaneously for the parameter.
  - _why:_ Setting both coordinates to zero tests only whether the curve passes through the origin and misses other x-intercepts. Set $y=0$, then find the corresponding $x$-values.
- Set the parameter equal to $0$, and substitute this value into the equations for $x$ and $y$.
  - _why:_ Setting the parameter to zero finds one particular point on the curve, not necessarily an intercept. An x-intercept requires $y=0$, followed by calculation of $x$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the y-intercepts of a parametric curve?

**Back:** Set $x=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $y$.

**Choices:**

- Set $x=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $y$. ✔
  - _why:_ At a y-intercept, the point lies on the y-axis, so its $x$-coordinate is zero. The resulting parameter values are substituted into $y$ to obtain the intercepts.
- Set $y=0$, solve for the parameter, and substitute those values into the equation for $x$.
  - _why:_ Setting $y=0$ identifies points on the x-axis and therefore finds x-intercepts. For y-intercepts, set $x=0$ and calculate the corresponding $y$-values.
- Set $x=0$ and $y=0$, then solve both equations simultaneously for the parameter.
  - _why:_ Setting both coordinates to zero checks only for the origin, rather than all y-intercepts. Set $x=0$, solve for the parameter, and then determine $y$.
- Set the parameter equal to $0$, and substitute this value into the equations for $x$ and $y$.
  - _why:_ A zero parameter selects a specific point but does not ensure that point lies on the y-axis. A y-intercept is found by imposing $x=0$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a circle defined by $x=a+r\cos\theta$ and $y=b+r\sin\theta$, the centre is $\text{[...]}$ and the radius is $r$.

**Back:** For a circle defined by $x=a+r\cos\theta$ and $y=b+r\sin\theta$, the centre is $\boxed{(a,b)}$ and the radius is $r$.

**Choices:**

- $$(a,b)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The constants $a$ and $b$ translate the standard circle horizontally and vertically, so the centre moves from $(0,0)$ to $(a,b)$.
- $$(-a,b)$
  - _why:_ The horizontal translation has not been reversed: $x=a+r\cos\theta$ gives $x-a=r\cos\theta$, so the centre’s x-coordinate is $a$, making the centre $(a,b)$.
- $$(a,-b)$
  - _why:_ The vertical translation has not been reversed: $y=b+r\sin\theta$ gives $y-b=r\sin\theta$, so the centre’s y-coordinate is $b$, making the centre $(a,b)$.
- $$(r,r)$
  - _why:_ The coefficients of sine and cosine determine the radius, not the centre. The translations $a$ and $b$ place the circle’s centre at $(a,b)$.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a circle defined by $x=a+r\cos\theta$ and $y=b+r\sin\theta$, the centre is $(a,b)$ and the radius is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** For a circle defined by $x=a+r\cos\theta$ and $y=b+r\sin\theta$, the centre is $(a,b)$ and the radius is $\boxed{r}$.

**Choices:**

- $$r$ ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficients of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ give the horizontal and vertical distance from the centre, so their common magnitude $r$ is the radius.
- $$r^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring occurs in the Cartesian equation, where the right-hand side is $r^2$, but the geometric radius itself is $r$, not its square.
- $$2r$
  - _why:_ Doubling the radius gives the diameter, which is the full distance across the circle. The distance from the centre to the circumference is $r$.
- $$\sqrt{r}$
  - _why:_ Taking a square root confuses the parameter coefficient with the squared radius in the Cartesian form. Since the trigonometric coefficients are $r$, the radius is $r$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the Cartesian equation of a circle with parametric equations $x=a+r\cos\theta$ and $y=b+r\sin\theta$?

**Back:** $$(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting the centre coordinates gives $x-a=r\cos\theta$ and $y-b=r\sin\theta$. Squaring and adding uses $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$, producing the stated equation.
- $$(x+a)^2 + (y+b)^2 = r^2$
  - _why:_ The signs inside the brackets must be opposite to the centre coordinates. This equation has centre $(-a,-b)$; the given parametrisation instead has centre $(a,b)$.
- $$(x+a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$
  - _why:_ The $x$ translation is reversed here, giving centre $(-a,b)$. From $x=a+r\cos\theta$, the correct horizontal term is $(x-a)^2$.
- $$(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r$
  - _why:_ Squaring the coordinate differences means the right-hand side is also squared after applying the trigonometric identity. The correct equation therefore has $r^2$, not $r$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Which trigonometric identity is typically used to eliminate the parameter for circles/ellipses involving $\sin \theta$ and $\cos \theta$?

**Back:** $$\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$

**Choices:**

- $$\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$ ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring and adding expressions involving sine and cosine removes the parameter because their squared values always sum to 1.
- $$\sin \theta + \cos \theta = 1$
  - _why:_ Sine and cosine themselves do not generally sum to 1. The relevant Pythagorean identity is $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$.
- $$\sin^2 \theta - \cos^2 \theta = 1$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the squared terms does not produce a constant value. Parameter elimination for these circle or ellipse forms uses $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$.
- $$1 + \tan^2 \theta = \sec^2 \theta$
  - _why:_ This identity concerns tangent and secant, so it does not directly combine expressions involving sine and cosine. The required identity is $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** If $x=t+4$ and $y=t^2+2$, find the Cartesian equation.

**Back:** $$y = (x-4)^2 + 2$

**Choices:**

- $$y = (x-4)^2 + 2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging $x=t+4$ gives $t=x-4$. Substituting this into $y=t^2+2$ produces $y=(x-4)^2+2$.
- $$y = (x+4)^2 + 2$
  - _why:_ The sign changes when $x=t+4$ is rearranged: $t=x-4$, not $x+4$. Therefore the Cartesian equation is $y=(x-4)^2+2$.
- $$y = (x-4)^2 - 2$
  - _why:_ Although $t=x-4$ has been substituted correctly, the constant in $y=t^2+2$ must remain positive. The equation is $y=(x-4)^2+2$.
- $$y = (x-2)^2 + 4$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly interchanges the horizontal shift of 4 and vertical shift of 2. Eliminating $t$ using $t=x-4$ gives $y=(x-4)^2+2$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a line segment in parametric form compared to an infinite straight line?

**Back:** The parameter is restricted to a finite interval (e.g., $0 \le t \le 4$).

**Choices:**

- The parameter is restricted to a finite interval (e.g., $0 \le t \le 4$). ✔
  - _why:_ Restricting the parameter to a continuous finite interval supplies two endpoints and traces only the portion of the line lying between them.
- The parameter is unrestricted over all real values (e.g., $-\infty < t < \infty$).
  - _why:_ Allowing every real parameter value traces the straight line indefinitely in both directions. A line segment instead requires the parameter to lie in a finite interval.
- The parameter is restricted to a finite set (e.g., $t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$).
  - _why:_ A finite set of parameter values produces separate points rather than a continuous segment. The parameter must vary continuously across a finite interval, such as $0 \le t \le 4$.
- The coordinates are restricted to integer values (e.g., $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$).
  - _why:_ Restricting the coordinates to integers would select isolated lattice points and would not define a continuous segment. It is the parameter that must have a finite interval.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** The trigonometric identity used to eliminate the parameter when $x = \tan t$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The trigonometric identity used to eliminate the parameter when $x = \tan t$ is $\boxed{\sec^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\sec^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t$ ✔
  - _why:_ The Pythagorean tangent identity expresses secant squared in terms of tangent squared, allowing $\tan t$ and $\sec^2 t$ to be related when eliminating the parameter.
- $$\sec^2 t = 1 - \tan^2 t$
  - _why:_ The tangent term is added, not subtracted, in the Pythagorean identity. The correct relationship is $\sec^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t$.
- $$\tan^2 t = 1 + \sec^2 t$
  - _why:_ This reverses the roles of tangent and secant and would imply an impossible negative difference. The identity is $\sec^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t$.
- $$\csc^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t$
  - _why:_ Cosecant belongs to the identity involving cotangent, not tangent. For $x=\tan t$, the relevant identity is $\sec^2 t = 1 + \tan^2 t$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Calculate the domain of $x$ for the curve $x = 2 + \cos t$.

**Back:** $$1 \le x \le 3$ (since $-1 \le \cos t \le 1$)

**Choices:**

- $$1 \le x \le 3$ (since $-1 \le \cos t \le 1$) ✔
  - _why:_ Since cosine ranges from $-1$ to $1$, adding 2 translates both endpoints by 2. Thus $x=2+\cos t$ ranges from 1 to 3.
- $$-1 \le x \le 1$ (since $-1 \le \cos t \le 1$)
  - _why:_ This omits the horizontal translation by 2. Adding 2 to the cosine range $[-1,1]$ gives the correct domain $1 \le x \le 3$.
- $$2 \le x \le 3$ (since $0 \le \cos t \le 1$)
  - _why:_ Cosine can be negative as well as positive, so its minimum is $-1$, not 0. Adding 2 gives the full domain $1 \le x \le 3$.
- $$1 \le x \le 2$ (since $-1 \le \cos t \le 0$)
  - _why:_ This includes the translated minimum but incorrectly uses 0 as the maximum of cosine. Since cosine reaches 1, the correct upper endpoint is 3.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** If $x = \cos t$ and $y = \cos 2t$, express $y$ in terms of $x$.

**Back:** $$y = 2x^2 - 1$

**Choices:**

- $$y = 2x^2 - 1$ ✔
  - _why:_ The double-angle identity is $\cos 2t=2\cos^2 t-1$. Since $x=\cos t$, direct substitution gives $y=2x^2-1$.
- $$y = 2x - 1$
  - _why:_ The double-angle formula requires the cosine to be squared. Omitting that square is the common slip; substituting $x=\cos t$ gives $y=2x^2-1$.
- $$y = 1 - 2x^2$
  - _why:_ This has both terms' signs reversed from the relevant double-angle identity. Since $\cos 2t=2\cos^2 t-1$, the correct expression is $y=2x^2-1$.
- $$y = x^2 - 1$
  - _why:_ This misses the coefficient 2 in the cosine double-angle identity. Using $\cos 2t=2\cos^2 t-1$ and $x=\cos t$ gives $y=2x^2-1$.

  - _why:_ Intercepts describe only particular points and need not include the circle intersections. The correct method substitutes the parametric expressions for $x$ and $y$ into the circle equation.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Find the vertex of the curve $x=t-5, y=t^2+1$.

**Back:** Vertex is at $(-5, 1)$ (where $t=0$).

**Choices:**

- Vertex is at $(-5, 1)$ (where $t=0$). ✔
  - _why:_ The minimum of $y=t^2+1$ occurs at $t=0$. At that parameter value, $x=0-5=-5$ and $y=1$, locating the vertex.
- Vertex is at $(5, 1)$ (where $t=0$).
  - _why:_ The sign of the horizontal translation has been reversed. At the vertex $t=0$, the equation $x=t-5$ gives $x=-5$, so the vertex is $(-5,1)$.
- Vertex is at $(-5, 0)$ (where $t=0$).
  - _why:_ The constant 1 shifts the parabola vertically, so its minimum $y$-value is 1 rather than 0. At $t=0$, the vertex is $(-5,1)$.
- Vertex is at $(0, 1)$ (where $t=0$).
  - _why:_ This mistakes the parameter value for the $x$-coordinate. Although the vertex occurs when $t=0$, substituting into $x=t-5$ gives $x=-5$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** When working with rational parametric equations like $x = \frac{1+6t}{t+1}$, what must you check regarding the denominator?

**Back:** Ensure the denominator is not zero within the given parameter interval.

**Choices:**

- Ensure the denominator is not zero within the given parameter interval. ✔
  - _why:_ A zero denominator makes the expression for $x$ undefined. Therefore, any parameter value satisfying $t+1=0$ must be excluded from the stated interval.
- Ensure the denominator is always positive within the given parameter interval.
  - _why:_ Requiring a positive denominator unnecessarily excludes valid negative denominator values. The denominator only needs to be non-zero throughout the permitted parameter interval.
- Ensure the denominator is not negative within the given parameter interval.
  - _why:_ A negative denominator still produces a defined value of $x$. The actual restriction is that the denominator must not equal zero in the parameter interval.
- Ensure the denominator is equal to zero within the given parameter interval.
  - _why:_ Setting the denominator equal to zero identifies an excluded parameter value, not an allowed one. The denominator must be non-zero within the given interval.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the endpoints of a curve defined for $a \le t \le b$?

**Back:** Substitute $t=a$ and $t=b$ into the parametric equations for $x$ and $y$.

**Choices:**

- Substitute $t=a$ and $t=b$ into the parametric equations for $x$ and $y$. ✔
  - _why:_ The parameter interval begins at $t=a$ and ends at $t=b$. Substituting both boundary values into both equations gives the coordinates of the curve’s endpoints.
- Differentiate the parametric equations at $t=a$ and $t=b$ for $x$ and $y$.
  - _why:_ Differentiation finds gradients or stationary points rather than the coordinates at the interval boundaries. The endpoints come from substituting $t=a$ and $t=b$ into both parametric equations.
- Set $x=0$ and $y=0$ in the parametric equations and solve for $t$.
  - _why:_ Solving $x=0$ and $y=0$ locates axis intercepts, which need not be endpoints. Endpoints are found by substituting the two boundary parameter values into both equations.
- Substitute $t=\frac{a+b}{2}$ into the parametric equations for $x$ and $y$.
  - _why:_ Using the midpoint gives one interior point, not the two endpoints. Substitute $t=a$ and $t=b$ separately into the equations for $x$ and $y$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** If $x = \ln(t+2)$, why must $t > -2$?

**Back:** The argument of a natural logarithm must be greater than zero ($t+2 > 0$).

**Choices:**

- The argument of a natural logarithm must be greater than zero ($t+2 > 0$). ✔
  - _why:_ A real natural logarithm is defined only when its argument is positive. Thus $t+2>0$, which rearranges directly to the restriction $t>-2$.
- The argument of a natural logarithm must be greater than or equal to zero ($t+2 \ge 0$).
  - _why:_ Including zero is a common boundary error because $\ln(0)$ is undefined. The argument must be strictly greater than zero, giving $t+2>0$ and hence $t>-2$.
- The argument of a natural logarithm must be less than zero ($t+2 < 0$).
  - _why:_ Negative arguments do not produce real values of the natural logarithm. The required condition is $t+2>0$, which is why the parameter must satisfy $t>-2$.
- The argument of a natural logarithm must be not equal to zero ($t+2 \ne 0$).
  - _why:_ Being non-zero is sufficient for a denominator but not for a real logarithm, since negative arguments are also invalid. The argument must satisfy $t+2>0$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** A point on a curve is found by substituting $t=0$ into $x=t+2, y=t^2-1$. What are its coordinates?

**Back:** $$(2, -1)$

**Choices:**

- $$(2, -1)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Substituting $t=0$ gives $x=0+2=2$ and $y=0^2-1=-1$. Therefore, the corresponding point is $(2,-1)$.
- $$(2, 1)$
  - _why:_ The $x$-coordinate is correct, but treating $0^2-1$ as positive causes a sign error. It equals $-1$, so the point is $(2,-1)$.
- $$(-1, 2)$
  - _why:_ This reverses the coordinates after evaluating them. Since $x=2$ and $y=-1$ when $t=0$, the ordered pair must be $(2,-1)$.
- $$(0, 0)$
  - _why:_ This omits the constant terms and incorrectly treats substitution as producing the origin. At $t=0$, $x=2$ and $y=-1$, giving $(2,-1)$.
