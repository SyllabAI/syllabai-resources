## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the two properties that define a vector?

**Back:** Size (magnitude)Direction

**Choices:**

- Size (magnitude)Direction ✔
  - _why:_ A vector is fully defined by its magnitude, which describes its size, and its direction, which describes the way it acts or points.
- MassSpeed
  - _why:_ Mass and speed are scalar quantities, not the defining properties of a vector. A vector is defined by its magnitude, or size, and its direction.
- PositionTime
  - _why:_ Position and time do not define vectors in general. The required properties are magnitude, meaning size, and direction.
- MagnitudeDistance
  - _why:_ Magnitude and distance are both measures of size, so this omits direction. Vectors require size, or magnitude, together with direction.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Back:** A scalar quantity has size only, whereas a vector has both size and direction.

**Choices:**

- scalar ✔

- vector
  - _why:_ A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction, so it cannot describe a quantity with size only. The correct term for size alone is scalar.
- displacement
  - _why:_ A displacement has magnitude and direction, making it a vector rather than a size-only quantity. The blank requires the term scalar.
- magnitude
  - _why:_ A magnitude describes the size of a quantity but is not the classification of a size-only quantity. The correct classification is scalar.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the resultant of several 3D vectors?

**Back:** Add the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components separately.

**Choices:**

- Add the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components separately. ✔
  - _why:_ Adding corresponding components separately preserves their directions: sum all the $\mathbf{i}$ components, all the $\mathbf{j}$ components, and all the $\mathbf{k}$ components.
- Multiply the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components separately.
  - _why:_ Multiplying components combines magnitudes incorrectly and does not produce vector addition. The resultant is found by adding the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components separately.
- Add the vector magnitudes separately.
  - _why:_ Adding only magnitudes loses directional information and cannot determine a 3D resultant. Add the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components separately.
- Add the $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ components together.
  - _why:_ Adding vectors in a single combined step without matching components can mix perpendicular directions. The correct method is to add each $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ component separately.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the physical meaning of a position vector?

**Back:** A vector that gives the position of a point relative to a fixed origin $O$.

**Choices:**

- A vector that gives the position of a point relative to a fixed origin $O$. ✔
  - _why:_ A position vector is drawn from the fixed origin $O$ to the point, so it specifies that point’s position relative to the chosen origin.
- A vector that gives the position of a point relative to a moving point $O$.
  - _why:_ A position vector is measured from a fixed origin, not from any moving point. It gives the position of a point relative to the fixed origin $O$.
- A vector that gives the distance travelled by a point relative to a fixed origin $O$.
  - _why:_ A position vector describes location, not the distance travelled over time. It gives a point’s position relative to a fixed origin $O$.
- A vector that gives the position of a point relative to the point from a fixed origin $O$.
  - _why:_ A position vector points from the origin to the point, rather than from the point to the origin. It gives the point’s position relative to the fixed origin $O$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** If point $A$ has position vector $\mathbf{a}$ and point $B$ has position vector $\mathbf{b}$, the displacement vector $\vec{AB}$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If point $A$ has position vector $\mathbf{a}$ and point $B$ has position vector $\mathbf{b}$, the displacement vector $\vec{AB}$ is $\boxed{\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The displacement from $A$ to $B$ is found by subtracting the position vector of the starting point from that of the finishing point, giving $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$.
- $$\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{a}$
  - _why:_ Adding the two position vectors does not give the displacement between the points. Displacement from $A$ to $B$ is finishing position minus starting position: $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$.
- $$\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$
  - _why:_ The expression $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$ gives the displacement from $B$ to $A$, reversing the required direction. From $A$ to $B$, the answer is $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$.
- $$-\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting both position vectors reverses and combines the wrong quantities. The displacement from $A$ to $B$ requires the final position minus the initial position, $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the magnitude of vector $\mathbf{v}=x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k}$?

**Back:** $$|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$

**Choices:**

- $$|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The magnitude follows the three-dimensional Pythagorean theorem: square each Cartesian component, add the squares, and take the positive square root.
- $$|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(x+y+z)^2}$
  - _why:_ Adding the components before squaring ignores their independent perpendicular directions. The magnitude is $|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$ by the three-dimensional Pythagorean theorem.
- $$|\mathbf{v}|=x^2+y^2+z^2$
  - _why:_ Omitting the square root gives the squared magnitude rather than the magnitude itself. The required formula is $|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.
- $$|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2-z^2}$
  - _why:_ The signs of components do not remain after applying Pythagoras because every component is squared. The correct formula is $|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How does the vector $\vec{BA}$ relate to the vector $\vec{AB}$?

**Back:** It has the same length but the opposite direction ($-\vec{AB}$).

**Choices:**

- It has the same length but the opposite direction ($-\vec{AB}$). ✔
  - _why:_ Reversing the order of the endpoints reverses a vector’s direction without changing its length. Therefore $\vec{BA}=-\vec{AB}$.
- It has the opposite length but the same direction ($\vec{AB}$).
  - _why:_ Reversing the endpoints does not change the distance between them, so the length cannot change. It gives the same length and opposite direction: $\vec{BA}=-\vec{AB}$.
- It has the same length and the same direction ($\vec{AB}$).
  - _why:_ The endpoint order determines direction, so $\vec{BA}$ cannot point the same way as $\vec{AB}$. It has the same length but opposite direction, giving $-\vec{AB}$.
- It has a different length and the opposite direction ($-2\vec{AB}$).
  - _why:_ Negating a vector reverses its direction but preserves its magnitude; it does not create a different length. Thus $\vec{BA}$ has the same length and equals $-\vec{AB}$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving for an unknown component in $|2\mathbf{i}+m\mathbf{j}-3\mathbf{k}|=7$, why might there be two possible values for $m$?

**Back:** The magnitude formula squares components, so both $m$ and $-m$ can produce the same result.

**Choices:**

- The magnitude formula squares components, so both $m$ and $-m$ can produce the same result. ✔
  - _why:_ The equation contains $m^2$, and positive and negative values with the same magnitude have equal squares. Consequently, both signs can satisfy the magnitude condition.
- The magnitude formula adds components, so both $m$ and $-m$ can produce the same result.
  - _why:_ The absolute value of the whole vector is not applied separately to each component. Two solutions arise because the magnitude equation contains $m^2$, so opposite values of $m$ have equal squares.
- The negative component $-3$ changes sign, so both $m$ and $-m$ can produce the same result.
  - _why:_ The fixed negative component becomes positive when squared, but that alone does not create two values of $m$. The pair arises because $m^2$ is unchanged when $m$ is negated.
- The magnitude formula has a square root, so both $m$ and $-m$ can produce the same result.
  - _why:_ A square root conventionally gives its non-negative principal value, not both signs. The two values occur earlier, when solving an equation involving $m^2$, since $m$ and $-m$ square equally.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** The journey rule states that the total journey $\vec{AC}$ is equal to $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The journey rule states that the total journey $\vec{AC}$ is equal to $\boxed{\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Travelling from $A$ to $B$ and then from $B$ to $C$ has the same overall displacement as travelling directly from $A$ to $C$.
- $$\vec{AB}-\vec{BC}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the second stage does not represent consecutive travel from $A$ through $B$ to $C$. The journey rule is $\vec{AC}=\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}$.
- $$\vec{BA}+\vec{CB}$
  - _why:_ The vectors $\vec{BA}$ and $\vec{CB}$ both point opposite to the required stages, so their sum gives $\vec{CA}$. The correct journey is $\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}$.
- $$\vec{AB}+\vec{CB}$
  - _why:_ These vectors do not connect head-to-tail: both terminate at $B$. The route from $A$ to $C$ must be written as $\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** If $A$ and $B$ have position vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, what is the position vector of their midpoint?

**Back:** $$\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A midpoint lies halfway between the two position vectors, so its vector is their average: add $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, then divide by $2$.
- $$\mathbf{a}+\frac{\mathbf{b}}{2}$
  - _why:_ Dividing only $\mathbf{b}$ does not average the two endpoints and treats them asymmetrically. The midpoint position vector is $\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$.
- $$\frac{\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}}{2}$
  - _why:_ The difference $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$ is the displacement from $A$ to $B$, so halving it is not generally a position vector. The midpoint is $\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$.
- $$\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$
  - _why:_ The sum points to twice the midpoint position when measured from the origin. Averaging requires division by $2$, giving $\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the condition for two non-zero vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ to be parallel?

**Back:** One vector is a scalar multiple of the other ($\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$).

**Choices:**

- One vector is a scalar multiple of the other ($\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$). ✔
  - _why:_ Parallel vectors have proportional corresponding components, so one is a scalar multiple of the other. A negative scalar permits them to point in opposite directions.
- The vectors have a zero scalar product with each other ($\mathbf{u}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{v}=0$).
  - _why:_ A zero dot product is the condition for perpendicular vectors, not parallel vectors. Parallel non-zero vectors satisfy $\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $\lambda$.
- The vectors have the same magnitude as each other ($|\mathbf{u}|=|\mathbf{v}|$).
  - _why:_ Equal magnitudes do not ensure equal directions, so vectors of the same length can meet at any angle. Parallelism requires one vector to be a scalar multiple of the other.
- One vector is exactly equal to the other ($\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}$).
  - _why:_ Requiring equality is too restrictive because parallel vectors may have different lengths or opposite directions. The general condition is $\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$ for a scalar $\lambda$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if both pairs of opposite side vectors are [ ___ ] (or equal).

**Back:** A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if both pairs of opposite side vectors are parallel (or equal).

**Choices:**

- parallel ✔
  - _why:_ In a parallelogram, each pair of opposite sides has the same direction line, so their vectors are parallel. Equivalently oriented side vectors may also be shown equal.
- perpendicular
  - _why:_ Perpendicular adjacent sides identify right angles but do not describe opposite-side relationships, and parallelograms need not contain right angles. Both pairs of opposite side vectors must be parallel.
- equal in magnitude
  - _why:_ Equal magnitudes alone establish only equal side lengths and do not ensure the opposite sides have matching directions. A parallelogram requires both pairs of opposite side vectors to be parallel.
- collinear
  - _why:_ Opposite sides of a non-degenerate parallelogram lie on distinct parallel lines rather than on one common line. The required vector relationship is parallelism for both opposite pairs.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you prove a quadrilateral $OABC$ is a trapezium using vectors?

**Back:** Show that exactly one pair of opposite sides are scalar multiples of each other.

**Choices:**

- Show that exactly one pair of opposite sides are scalar multiples of each other. ✔
  - _why:_ Under the UK definition used here, a trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel opposite sides. Parallel vectors are scalar multiples, so demonstrating exactly one such pair proves the classification.
- Show that at least one pair of opposite sides are scalar multiples of each other.
  - _why:_ Showing at least one parallel pair does not exclude a parallelogram, which has two parallel pairs. To prove a trapezium, show exactly one pair of opposite sides are scalar multiples.
- Show that both pairs of opposite sides are scalar multiples of each other.
  - _why:_ Two pairs of opposite sides that are scalar multiples establish two pairs of parallel sides, which characterises a parallelogram. A trapezium requires exactly one parallel pair.
- Show that exactly one pair of opposite sides have equal magnitudes to each other.
  - _why:_ Equal lengths do not establish parallel directions, so this test cannot prove a trapezium. The vector test is that exactly one pair of opposite sides are scalar multiples.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** In an isosceles triangle where $AB=AC$, how is the perpendicular height from $A$ found using vectors?

**Back:** Calculate the vector from $A$ to the midpoint of the base $BC$.

**Choices:**

- Calculate the vector from $A$ to the midpoint of the base $BC$. ✔
  - _why:_ In an isosceles triangle, the perpendicular from the apex $A$ bisects the base $BC$. Therefore, its vector runs from $A$ to the midpoint of $BC$.
- Calculate the vector from $A$ to the endpoint $B$ of the base $BC$.
  - _why:_ The vector from $A$ to point $B$ follows an equal side rather than the perpendicular height. The height runs from $A$ to the midpoint of $BC$.
- Calculate the vector from $A$ to the midpoint of the side $AB$.
  - _why:_ The midpoint of $AB$ lies on one of the equal sides, so this vector is not the height. The required vector goes from $A$ to the midpoint of $BC$.
- Calculate the vector from $B$ to the endpoint $C$ of the base $BC$.
  - _why:_ The vector between the endpoints of $BC$ lies along the base, not perpendicular to it. The height is the vector from $A$ to the midpoint of $BC$.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** To solve problems involving unknown components and magnitudes, apply the formula then [ ___ ] both sides to eliminate the square root.

**Back:** To solve problems involving unknown components and magnitudes, apply the formula then square both sides to eliminate the square root.

**Choices:**

- square ✔
  - _why:_ A magnitude formula contains a square root, so squaring both sides removes that root and produces an equation that can be solved for the unknown component.
- square-root
  - _why:_ Square-rooting introduces another root rather than eliminating the existing one. The correct operation is to square both sides of the magnitude equation.
- divide
  - _why:_ Dividing both sides does not generally remove a square root surrounding a sum of squared components. Squaring both sides eliminates the root correctly.
- expand
  - _why:_ Expanding may simplify expressions inside brackets, but it does not undo an outer square root. The required inverse operation is to square both sides.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Express the vector $3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}+5\mathbf{k}$ as a column vector.

**Back:** $$$\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\\5\end{pmatrix}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\\5\end{pmatrix}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficients of $mathbf{i}$, $mathbf{j}$ and $mathbf{k}$ become the first, second and third entries respectively, including the negative sign on the second component.
- $$$begin{pmatrix}325end{pmatrix}$$
  - _why:_ The second entry must retain the negative sign from $-2mathbf{j}$. The correct column vector is $begin{pmatrix}3-25end{pmatrix}$.
- $$$begin{pmatrix}35-2end{pmatrix}$$
  - _why:_ The entries must follow the order $mathbf{i}$, $mathbf{j}$, $mathbf{k}$, not place the $mathbf{k}$-component second. The correct entries are $3,-2,5$.
- $$$begin{pmatrix}-32-5end{pmatrix}$$
  - _why:_ Negating every component gives the opposite vector, not the given vector. The coefficients must be copied directly, producing entries $3,-2,5$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** If $\vec{AB}=\vec{BD}$, how is the position vector $\mathbf{d}$ calculated from point $B$?

**Back:** Add vector $\vec{AB}$ to the position vector of $B$ ($\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{b}+\vec{AB}$).

**Choices:**

- Add vector $\vec{AB}$ to the position vector of $B$ ($\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{b}+\vec{AB}$). ✔
  - _why:_ Starting at the origin, travel to $B$ using $mathbf{b}$ and then from $B$ to $D$ using $vec{AB}$. Vector addition therefore gives $mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+vec{AB}$.
- Subtract vector $vec{AB}$ from the position vector of $B$ ($mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}-vec{AB}$).
  - _why:_ Subtracting $vec{AB}$ moves from $B$ in the direction opposite to $vec{BD}$. Since $vec{BD}=vec{AB}$, the correct calculation is $mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+vec{AB}$.
- Add the position vector of $A$ to the position vector of $B$ ($mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+mathbf{a}$).
  - _why:_ Adding the position vectors of $A$ and $B$ does not represent the displacement from $B$ to $D$. The required relation is $mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+vec{AB}$.
- Add the position vector of $B$ to itself ($mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+mathbf{b}$).
  - _why:_ Doubling $mathbf{b}$ incorrectly treats $vec{BD}$ as equal to the position vector of $B$. Instead, add the given displacement: $mathbf{d}=mathbf{b}+vec{AB}$.
