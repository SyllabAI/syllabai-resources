## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the coordinates of the origin?

**Back:** $$(0,0)$

**Choices:**

- $$(0,0)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The origin is the point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect, so both its x-coordinate and y-coordinate are zero.
- $$(1,0)$
  - _why:_ This point lies one unit to the right of the origin on the x-axis. The origin has both coordinates equal to zero, so it is $(0,0)$.
- $$(0,1)$
  - _why:_ This point lies one unit above the origin on the y-axis. At the origin, both coordinate values are zero, giving $(0,0)$.
- $$(1,1)$
  - _why:_ This confuses the origin with a point one unit along both axes. The two axes intersect where $x=0$ and $y=0$, at $(0,0)$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, what does $m$ represent?

**Back:** The gradient (steepness and direction).

**Choices:**

- The gradient (steepness and direction). ✔
  - _why:_ In $y=mx+c$, $m$ gives the change in $y$ for each unit change in $x$. Its sign and magnitude determine the line’s direction and steepness.
- The y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis).
  - _why:_ The y-intercept is represented by $c$, not $m$. In $y=mx+c$, $m$ is the gradient, which describes the line’s steepness and direction.
- The x-intercept (where the line crosses the x-axis).
  - _why:_ The x-intercept must be found by setting $y=0$ and is not represented directly by $m$. The value of $m$ represents the gradient.
- The constant term (the value independent of x).
  - _why:_ The constant term is $c$, whereas $m$ is the coefficient of $x$. Therefore, $m$ represents the gradient of the straight line.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, what does $c$ represent?

**Back:** The y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis).

**Choices:**

- The y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis). ✔
  - _why:_ When $x=0$, the equation becomes $y=c$. Therefore, $c$ gives the y-coordinate where the straight line crosses the y-axis.
- The gradient (steepness and direction).
  - _why:_ The gradient is represented by $m$, not $c$. The constant $c$ gives the y-intercept because the line has $y=c$ when $x=0$.
- The x-intercept (where the line crosses the x-axis).
  - _why:_ The x-intercept is found by setting $y=0$ and solving for $x$. The value $c$ instead identifies where the line crosses the y-axis.
- The origin (where the two axes intersect).
  - _why:_ The origin is always $(0,0)$ and is not generally represented by $c$. In $y=mx+c$, $c$ represents the line’s y-intercept.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the gradient of the line $y=5-x$?

**Back:** $$-1$

**Choices:**

- $$-1$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rewriting the equation as $y=-x+5$ shows that the coefficient of $x$ is $-1$. Therefore, the line’s gradient is $-1$.
- $$1$
  - _why:_ This ignores the negative sign before $x$. Since $y=5-x$ can be written as $y=-x+5$, its gradient is $-1$, not $1$.
- $$5$
  - _why:_ This mistakes the constant term for the gradient. The number $5$ is the y-intercept, while the coefficient of $x$ is $-1$.
- $$-5$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly attaches the negative sign to the constant rather than reading the coefficient of $x$. In $y=-x+5$, the gradient is $-1$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coordinate of the y-intercept for the line $y=4x-6$?

**Back:** $$(0,-6)$

**Choices:**

- $$(0,-6)$ ✔
  - _why:_ A y-intercept occurs where $x=0$. Substituting $x=0$ into $y=4x-6$ gives $y=-6$, so the coordinate is $(0,-6)$.
- $$(-6,0)$
  - _why:_ This reverses the coordinate order. At a y-intercept, the x-coordinate must be zero, so the correct coordinate is $(0,-6)$ rather than $(-6,0)$.
- $$(0,6)$
  - _why:_ This loses the negative sign on the constant term. Setting $x=0$ gives $y=-6$, so the y-intercept is $(0,-6)$.
- $$(4,-6)$
  - _why:_ This uses the gradient as the x-coordinate. Every y-intercept has $x=0$; substituting this into the equation gives the point $(0,-6)$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When rearranging an equation into the form $y=mx+c$, what must you do when dividing by the coefficient of $y$?

**Back:** Divide every term on the other side of the equation.

**Choices:**

- Divide every term on the other side of the equation. ✔
  - _why:_ Division must preserve equality by applying to the entire expression on the other side. Dividing every term produces the correct gradient and intercept in $y=mx+c$ form.
- Divide only the x-term on the other side of the equation.
  - _why:_ Dividing only the term containing $x$ changes the equation because the constant remains unscaled. Every term on the other side must be divided by the coefficient of $y$.
- Divide only the constant term on the other side of the equation.
  - _why:_ Dividing only the constant leaves the coefficient of $x$ incorrect and does not preserve the original equation. The coefficient must divide every term on the other side.
- Multiply every term on the other side of the equation.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is the inverse operation needed when a fraction multiplies $y$, not when $y$ has a numerical coefficient. Here, every term must be divided by that coefficient.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the gradient of a line with equation $3y = 12x + 9$?

**Back:** $$4$ (divide both sides by 3).

**Choices:**

- $$4$ (divide both sides by 3). ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing every term in $3y=12x+9$ by $3$ gives $y=4x+3$. The coefficient of $x$, and therefore the gradient, is $4$.
- $$12$ (use the coefficient of x).
  - _why:_ This reads the coefficient of $x$ before isolating $y$. Dividing the entire equation by $3$ gives $y=4x+3$, so the gradient is $4$.
- $$3$ (divide the constant by 3).
  - _why:_ This mistakes the new constant term for the gradient. After division by $3$, the equation is $y=4x+3$, where $4$ is the gradient.
- $$rac{1}{4}$ (divide 3 by 12).
  - _why:_ This divides $3$ by $12$, reversing the required ratio. Isolating $y$ divides $12$ by $3$, so the gradient is $4$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** If a line passes through $(0,2)$ and $(3,8)$, what is the y-intercept ($c$)?

**Back:** $$2$

**Choices:**

- $$2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The y-intercept is the y-value where $x=0$. Since the given point $(0,2)$ lies on the line, the y-intercept $c$ is $2$.
- $$8$
  - _why:_ This uses the y-coordinate of the second point, where $x=3$, rather than the y-intercept. At $x=0$, the given y-value is $2$.
- $$3$
  - _why:_ This uses the second point’s x-coordinate as the intercept. The y-intercept is read from the point with $x=0$, namely $(0,2)$, so $c=2$.
- $$6$
  - _why:_ This takes the change in y, $8-2=6$, which is not the intercept. The point $(0,2)$ directly shows that $c=2$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you tell if two lines are parallel from their equations?

**Back:** They have the same gradient ($m$).

**Choices:**

- They have the same gradient ($m$). ✔
  - _why:_ Parallel lines have equal gradients, so their equations contain the same value of $m$. Their intercepts must differ unless the equations represent the same line.
- They have the same y-intercept ($c$).
  - _why:_ Sharing a y-intercept means the lines meet at the y-axis, not that they are parallel. Parallel lines have the same gradient ($m$).
- They have opposite gradients ($m$).
  - _why:_ Opposite gradients generally produce lines sloping in different directions and do not make them parallel. Parallel lines have the same gradient ($m$).
- Their gradients have a product of -1.
  - _why:_ Gradients with a product of $-1$ identify perpendicular lines, not parallel ones. Parallel lines instead have the same gradient ($m$).

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Write the equation of a line parallel to $y=-3x+4$ that passes through $(0,7)$.

**Back:** $$y=-3x+7$

**Choices:**

- $$y=-3x+7$ ✔
  - _why:_ A parallel line keeps the gradient $-3$. Since passing through $(0,7)$ gives a y-intercept of $7$, its equation is $y=-3x+7$.
- $$y=3x+7$
  - _why:_ Changing the gradient from $-3$ to $3$ reverses the slope, so the line is not parallel. The required equation is $y=-3x+7$.
- $$y=-3x+4$
  - _why:_ This retains the original y-intercept, so it describes the given line and does not pass through $(0,7)$. The required equation is $y=-3x+7$.
- $$y=-3x-7$
  - _why:_ The gradient is correctly retained, but substituting $x=0$ gives $y=-7$, not $7$. The correct y-intercept produces $y=-3x+7$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the general equation for a horizontal line?

**Back:** $$y=c$ (where $c$ is a constant).

**Choices:**

- $$y=c$ (where $c$ is a constant). ✔
  - _why:_ Every point on a horizontal line has the same y-coordinate. Therefore its equation is $y=c$, where the constant $c$ gives the line’s fixed height.
- $$x=c$ (where $c$ is a constant).
  - _why:_ Fixing the x-coordinate creates a vertical line rather than a horizontal one. A horizontal line fixes the y-coordinate, giving $y=c$ where $c$ is constant.
- $$y=cx$ (where $c$ is a constant).
  - _why:_ The equation $y=cx$ generally has gradient $c$ and passes through the origin. A horizontal line has fixed y-coordinate, so its equation is $y=c$.
- $$x+y=c$ (where $c$ is a constant).
  - _why:_ Rearranging this gives $y=-x+c$, which has gradient $-1$ rather than $0$. A horizontal line has the form $y=c$, with constant height.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Identify the gradient and y-intercept for the line $y=7-2x$.

**Back:** Gradient: -2y-intercept: 7

**Choices:**

- Gradient: -2y-intercept: 7 ✔
  - _why:_ Writing the equation as $y=-2x+7$ shows that the coefficient of $x$ is the gradient, $-2$, while the constant is the y-intercept, $7$.
- Gradient: 7y-intercept: -2
  - _why:_ The coefficient and constant have been confused. In $y=-2x+7$, the coefficient of $x$ gives gradient $-2$, and the constant gives y-intercept $7$.
- Gradient: 2y-intercept: 7
  - _why:_ Reordering $7-2x$ as $-2x+7$ does not change the negative sign on the $x$ coefficient. The gradient is $-2$ and the y-intercept is $7$.
- Gradient: -2y-intercept: -7
  - _why:_ The gradient is read correctly, but the constant term is positive $7$, not negative. Thus the line crosses the y-axis at $7$ and has gradient $-2$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the gradient of a line that slopes downwards from left to right?

**Back:** A negative gradient.

**Choices:**

- A negative gradient. ✔
  - _why:_ As $x$ increases from left to right, a downward-sloping line’s $y$-value decreases. Therefore the change in $y$ and its gradient are negative.
- A positive gradient.
  - _why:_ A positive gradient means $y$ increases as $x$ increases, producing an upward slope from left to right. A downward-sloping line has a negative gradient.
- A zero gradient.
  - _why:_ A zero gradient describes a horizontal line whose y-value does not change. If the line slopes downwards from left to right, its gradient is negative.
- An undefined gradient.
  - _why:_ An undefined gradient belongs to a vertical line because its horizontal change is zero. A line sloping downwards from left to right has a negative gradient.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the two most important clues to look for when matching a graph to an equation?

**Back:** The direction of the slope (positive or negative)The y-intercept value

**Choices:**

- The direction of the slope (positive or negative)The y-intercept value ✔
  - _why:_ The slope’s direction reveals whether the gradient is positive or negative, while the point where the graph crosses the y-axis gives the equation’s y-intercept.
- The steepness of the slope (large or small)The x-intercept value
  - _why:_ Steepness helps estimate the gradient’s magnitude, but the x-intercept is not the constant in $y=mx+c$. First identify the slope’s direction and the y-intercept.
- The length of the line (long or short)The midpoint value
  - _why:_ A drawn segment’s length and midpoint can change with the viewing window without changing its equation. Match using the slope’s direction and the y-intercept value.
- The scale of the axes (large or small)The x-intercept value
  - _why:_ Axis scales affect how steep a graph appears, and the x-intercept is not directly the constant term. The key clues are slope direction and y-intercept value.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Calculate the gradient of a line passing through $(0,5)$ and $(2,-1)$.

**Back:** $$-3$ (Calculation: $\frac{-1-5}{2-0} = \frac{-6}{2}$)

**Choices:**

- $$-3$ (Calculation: $\frac{-1-5}{2-0} = \frac{-6}{2}$) ✔
  - _why:_ Gradient is change in $y$ divided by change in $x$. Here the changes are $-1-5=-6$ and $2-0=2$, so the gradient is $-3$.
- $$3$ (Calculation: $\frac{5-(-1)}{2-0} = \frac{6}{2}$)
  - _why:_ The numerator and denominator must use the same point order. Reversing only the y-subtraction loses the negative sign; correctly, $(-1-5)/(2-0)=-3$.
- $$-2$ (Calculation: $\frac{-1+5}{2-0} = \frac{4}{2}$)
  - _why:_ Subtracting the y-coordinates incorrectly gives $-4$ instead of $-6$. The vertical change is $-1-5=-6$, so dividing by $2$ gives $-3$.
- $$-\frac{1}{3}$ (Calculation: $\frac{2-0}{-1-5} = \frac{2}{-6}$)
  - _why:_ This divides the horizontal change by the vertical change, calculating the reciprocal of the gradient. Gradient is change in $y$ over change in $x$, giving $-3$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Rearrange $x+4y=12$ to make $y$ the subject.

**Back:** $$y = -\frac{1}{4}x + 3$

**Choices:**

- $$y = -\frac{1}{4}x + 3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $x$ gives $4y=12-x$, then dividing every term by $4$ gives $y=3-\frac{1}{4}x$, equivalent to the stated equation.
- $$y = \frac{1}{4}x + 3$
  - _why:_ The $x$ term becomes negative when moved to the other side: $4y=12-x$. Dividing by $4$ therefore gives $y=-\frac{1}{4}x+3$.
- $$y = -\frac{1}{4}x + 12$
  - _why:_ Dividing $4y=12-x$ by $4$ must divide both terms on the right. The constant becomes $12/4=3$, so $y=-\frac{1}{4}x+3$.
- $$y = -4x + 3$
  - _why:_ The coefficient of $x$ must also be divided by $4$ after rearranging. From $4y=12-x$, the correct result is $y=-\frac{1}{4}x+3$.
