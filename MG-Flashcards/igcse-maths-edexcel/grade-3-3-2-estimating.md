## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** For most IGCSE estimating questions, round each number to [ ___ ] before calculating.

**Back:** For most IGCSE estimating questions, round each number to one significant figure before calculating.

**Choices:**

- one significant figure ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding each value to one significant figure usually makes the arithmetic simple while keeping the estimate reasonably close to the exact answer.
- one decimal place
  - _why:_ Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, so this method does not simplify numbers consistently. For standard estimates, round each number to one significant figure.
- two significant figures
  - _why:_ Two significant figures may leave unnecessarily difficult arithmetic and is not the usual IGCSE estimation convention. The values should normally be rounded to one significant figure.
- the nearest integer
  - _why:_ Rounding to integers can turn small decimals into zero and make an estimate meaningless. IGCSE estimating questions usually require rounding each value to one significant figure.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The first significant figure of a number is the first [ ___ ] digit.

**Back:** The first significant figure of a number is the first non-zero digit.

**Choices:**

- non-zero ✔
  - _why:_ Leading zeros only show place value, so the first significant figure is the first digit that is not zero when reading from left to right.
- decimal
  - _why:_ The first digit after a decimal point may be zero and therefore not significant. The first significant figure is the first non-zero digit.
- left-hand
  - _why:_ The leftmost digit can be a leading zero, especially in a decimal less than 1. The first significant figure is specifically the first non-zero digit.
- even-numbered
  - _why:_ Whether a digit is even or odd has no effect on significance. The first significant figure is the first non-zero digit in the number.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the result of rounding 0.196 to one significant figure?

**Back:** 0.2

**Choices:**

- 0.2 ✔
  - _why:_ The first significant digit is 1, and the next digit is 9, so 1 rounds up to 2. Therefore, 0.196 becomes 0.2.
- 0.1
  - _why:_ This truncates the number without using the next digit. Since the second significant digit is 9, the first digit rounds up, giving 0.2.
- 0.02
  - _why:_ This shifts the place value of the significant digit and makes the result ten times too small. Rounding 0.196 to one significant figure gives 0.2.
- 0.19
  - _why:_ This keeps two significant figures and also fails to round using the following digit. To one significant figure, 0.196 rounds to 0.2.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the result of rounding 431.1 to one significant figure?

**Back:** 400

**Choices:**

- 400 ✔
  - _why:_ The first significant digit is 4, and the next digit is 3. Since 3 is below 5, 431.1 rounds down to 400.
- 430
  - _why:_ This is 431.1 rounded to two significant figures, not one. Keeping only the first significant digit gives 400 because the following digit is 3.
- 431
  - _why:_ This rounds to the nearest integer and keeps three significant figures. For one significant figure, the 3 does not round the 4 up, so the result is 400.
- 500
  - _why:_ This incorrectly rounds up even though the second significant digit is 3. A digit below 5 leaves the first digit unchanged, giving 400.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a number like $0.049$, the zeros before the 4 are only [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a number like $0.049$, the zeros before the 4 are only placeholders.

**Choices:**

- placeholders ✔
  - _why:_ The zeros locate the decimal places but do not contribute to the measured precision. The first significant digit in $0.049$ is therefore 4.
- significant figures
  - _why:_ Leading zeros only indicate place value and are not counted as significant figures. In $0.049$, the first significant digit is 4, so the zeros are placeholders.
- decimal values
  - _why:_ The zeros are digits used to position the 4 correctly, rather than separate decimal values. They are placeholders and do not count as significant figures.
- rounding digits
  - _why:_ A rounding digit is the digit inspected after the required accuracy has been chosen. The leading zeros in $0.049$ simply act as placeholders.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When estimating a calculation involving multiple numbers, when should you round the values?

**Back:** Before performing any calculations.

**Choices:**

- Before performing any calculations. ✔
  - _why:_ Estimating requires simplifying the original values first and then calculating with those rounded values. This ensures that the resulting answer is genuinely an estimate.
- After performing all calculations.
  - _why:_ Rounding only after calculating produces a rounded exact answer, not an estimate. The values must be rounded before performing any calculations.
- During each calculation step.
  - _why:_ Repeated rounding during the working can introduce extra errors and does not follow the standard estimation method. Round the original values before performing any calculations.
- Between the calculation steps.
  - _why:_ Rounding intermediate results confuses estimation with step-by-step approximation. For a standard estimate, round all given values before carrying out the calculation.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Estimate the value of $\frac{49.3 \times 58.7}{9.8}$.

**Back:** Round to $\frac{50 \times 60}{10}$$3000 \div 10 = 300$

**Choices:**

- Round to $\frac{50 \times 60}{10}$$3000 \div 10 = 300$ ✔
  - _why:_ Each number rounds to one significant figure: $49.3$ to $50$, $58.7$ to $60$, and $9.8$ to $10$. The resulting estimate is $300$.
- Round to $\frac{50 \times 60}{1}$$3000 \div 1 = 3000$
  - _why:_ Rounding $9.8$ to $1$ loses its place value; to one significant figure it is $10$. The correct simplified calculation gives an estimate of $300$.
- Round to $\frac{40 \times 50}{10}$$2000 \div 10 = 200$
  - _why:_ This rounds both numerator values down rather than to the nearest significant figure. They should become $50$ and $60$, producing the estimate $300$.
- Round to $\frac{50 \times 60}{100}$$3000 \div 100 = 30$
  - _why:_ Rounding $9.8$ to $100$ introduces a place-value error of a factor of ten. It should round to $10$, so the estimate is $300$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How does dividing by 0.2 affect the size of a number?

**Back:** It makes the number larger (equivalent to multiplying by 5).

**Choices:**

- It makes the number larger (equivalent to multiplying by 5). ✔
  - _why:_ Since $0.2 = \frac{1}{5}$, dividing by $0.2$ is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal, 5. The original number therefore becomes larger.
- It makes the number smaller (equivalent to dividing by 5).
  - _why:_ This confuses dividing by one fifth with dividing by five. Since $0.2 = \frac{1}{5}$, division by $0.2$ multiplies the number by 5.
- It makes the number larger (equivalent to multiplying by 2).
  - _why:_ The multiplier must be the reciprocal of $0.2$, which is 5 rather than 2. Dividing by $0.2$ therefore makes the number five times larger.
- It makes the number smaller (equivalent to multiplying by 0.2).
  - _why:_ This treats division by $0.2$ as multiplication by $0.2$. Division uses the reciprocal, so it is equivalent to multiplying by 5 and makes the number larger.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Estimate the value of $\frac{7.9 + 22.6}{0.204}$.

**Back:** Round to $\frac{8 + 20}{0.2}$$28 \div 0.2 = 140$

**Choices:**

- Round to $\frac{8 + 20}{0.2}$$28 \div 0.2 = 140$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding each number to one significant figure gives $8$, $20$ and $0.2$. Dividing $28$ by $0.2$ gives the suitable estimate of $140$.
- Round to $\frac{8 + 20}{0.02}$$28 \div 0.02 = 1400$
  - _why:_ The denominator has been rounded to the wrong place value: $0.204$ rounds to $0.2$, not $0.02$. The correct estimate is therefore $28 \div 0.2 = 140$.
- Round to $\frac{8 + 20}{2}$$28 \div 2 = 14$
  - _why:_ This loses the decimal place when rounding $0.204$; it should become $0.2$, not $2$. Using the correct denominator gives $28 \div 0.2 = 140$.
- Round to $\frac{8 + 30}{0.2}$$38 \div 0.2 = 190$
  - _why:_ Rounding $22.6$ to one significant figure gives $20$, not $30$. The numerator should be $8 + 20 = 28$, producing an estimate of $140$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you round a value under a square root (e.g. $\sqrt{97.6}$) when estimating?

**Back:** Round it to the nearest square number (e.g. $\sqrt{100} = 10$).

**Choices:**

- Round it to the nearest square number (e.g. $\sqrt{100} = 10$). ✔
  - _why:_ A square number has an integer square root, making the calculation easy without a calculator. Since $97.6$ is close to $100$, its square root is approximately $10$.
- Round it to the nearest cube number (e.g. $\sqrt{125} \approx 11$).
  - _why:_ Cube numbers are useful when estimating cube roots, not square roots. For a square root, round to a nearby square number, such as $100$, giving $\sqrt{100} = 10$.
- Round it to the nearest whole number (e.g. $\sqrt{98} \approx 10$).
  - _why:_ A whole number does not necessarily have an easy square root, so this rounding may not simplify the calculation. Use the nearest square number, such as $100$.
- Round it to the nearest power of two (e.g. $\sqrt{128} \approx 11$).
  - _why:_ Powers of two are not generally easy to square-root mentally. The useful nearby values are square numbers, so $97.6$ should be rounded to $100$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is an estimate useful when using a calculator?

**Back:** To check if the calculator answer is a sensible/reasonable size.

**Choices:**

- To check if the calculator answer is a sensible/reasonable size. ✔
  - _why:_ An estimate gives an expected approximate size for the result. Comparing it with the calculator answer helps reveal typing errors, incorrect operations or misplaced decimal points.
- To check if the calculator answer has the maximum possible accuracy.
  - _why:_ Estimation checks the approximate size of an answer, not its accuracy to many decimal places. Its purpose is to confirm that the calculator result is sensible and reasonable.
- To replace the calculator answer with an exact whole-number result.
  - _why:_ An estimate is approximate and does not replace an exact calculator result. It is used to check whether the calculated answer has a sensible or reasonable size.
- To determine how many digits the calculator should display on its screen.
  - _why:_ The number of displayed digits depends on the calculator and required accuracy, not the estimate. Estimation instead checks whether the final answer is a sensible size.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** An estimate uses $10 \times 40$ to calculate $9.82 \times 38$. Is this an overestimate or underestimate?

**Back:** Overestimate because both values were rounded up.

**Choices:**

- Overestimate because both values were rounded up. ✔
  - _why:_ Both factors increase: $9.82$ becomes $10$ and $38$ becomes $40$. Multiplying two larger positive values produces a result greater than the exact product.
- Underestimate because both values were rounded up.
  - _why:_ Rounding both positive factors upwards makes their product larger, not smaller. Therefore $10 \times 40$ is an overestimate of $9.82 \times 38$.
- Underestimate because both values were rounded down.
  - _why:_ Neither value was rounded down: $9.82$ increased to $10$ and $38$ increased to $40$. The product is consequently an overestimate.
- Exact estimate because the rounding errors cancel out.
  - _why:_ The rounding errors do not cancel because both values were increased. Their effects reinforce each other, making $10 \times 40$ an overestimate rather than the exact product.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** If the cost of an item is 3.1 pence, what rounded value should be used to estimate the total cost in pounds?

**Back:** 3 pence (later divided by 100 to convert to pounds).

**Choices:**

- 3 pence (later divided by 100 to convert to pounds). ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding $3.1$ pence to one significant figure gives $3$ pence. Because there are $100$ pence in one pound, the total in pence is divided by $100$.
- 3 pence (later multiplied by 100 to convert to pounds).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $100$ converts pounds to pence, so the conversion direction has been reversed. After estimating with $3$ pence, divide the total by $100$ to obtain pounds.
- 30 pence (later divided by 100 to convert to pounds).
  - _why:_ This shifts the decimal point and makes the estimated cost ten times too large. The item should be estimated as $3$ pence, then the total divided by $100$.
- 3.1 pence (later divided by 100 to convert to pounds).
  - _why:_ Keeping $3.1$ does not perform the intended rounding for a simple estimate. Use $3$ pence per item, then divide the total number of pence by $100$.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** In division, making the denominator [ ___ ] makes the final estimate smaller.

**Back:** In division, making the denominator bigger makes the final estimate smaller.

**Choices:**

- bigger ✔
  - _why:_ For a fixed positive numerator, dividing by a larger denominator produces a smaller quotient. Therefore making the denominator bigger makes the final estimate smaller.
- smaller
  - _why:_ A smaller denominator makes a positive quotient larger because the numerator is split into fewer denominator-sized parts. The denominator must become bigger to make the estimate smaller.
- unchanged
  - _why:_ Changing only the denominator changes the value of the quotient. To make a positive division result smaller while keeping the numerator fixed, the denominator must be bigger.
- negative
  - _why:_ Making a denominator negative changes the sign rather than simply reducing the estimated magnitude. For positive quantities, a bigger denominator produces the required smaller estimate.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** In division, making the denominator bigger makes the final estimate [ ___ ].

**Back:** In division, making the denominator bigger makes the final estimate smaller.

**Choices:**

- smaller ✔
  - _why:_ With a fixed positive numerator, increasing the denominator reduces the quotient. The same amount is divided into more or larger parts, so the final estimate becomes smaller.
- bigger
  - _why:_ This reverses the relationship between denominator and quotient. When the positive numerator stays fixed, making the denominator bigger makes the division result smaller, not bigger.
- unchanged
  - _why:_ The quotient remains unchanged only if numerator and denominator are scaled by the same factor. Increasing the denominator alone makes the final estimate smaller.
- negative
  - _why:_ Increasing a positive denominator does not change the sign of the quotient. It reduces the quotient’s size, so the final estimate becomes smaller rather than negative.

- divide ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the total available seconds by the seconds required for one event gives the number of complete event intervals that fit into the year.

  - _why:_ Multiplying combines the two time quantities and produces the wrong scale for a frequency. The number of events is found by dividing total seconds by seconds per event.

  - _why:_ Adding the times does not count how many event intervals fit into the year. Division is needed to compare the total duration with the duration of one event.

  - _why:_ Subtracting removes one duration from another but does not determine how many repeated events occur. Dividing the yearly total by the duration of one event gives the count.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** If you round the denominator of a fraction down, does the estimate become an overestimate or underestimate?

**Back:** Overestimate (dividing by a smaller number gives a larger result).

**Choices:**

- Overestimate (dividing by a smaller number gives a larger result). ✔
  - _why:_ With a positive numerator, reducing the denominator makes each unit of the denominator represent more of the quotient. Therefore, dividing by a smaller rounded denominator gives an overestimate.
- Underestimate (dividing by a larger number gives a smaller result).
  - _why:_ That reasoning applies when the denominator is rounded up, not down. A smaller positive denominator makes the quotient larger, so the estimate is an overestimate.
- Neither (rounding the denominator does not affect the estimate).
  - _why:_ Changing the denominator changes the quotient, so the estimate is affected. Rounding it down makes the denominator smaller and produces an overestimate.
- Cannot tell (the denominator has no effect on the quotient).
  - _why:_ The denominator directly controls the size of a positive fraction. Making it smaller increases the quotient, so rounding the denominator down gives an overestimate.
