## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coefficient of $x$ in the expression $5x^2-3x+8$?

**Back:** -3

**Choices:**

- -3 ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficient is the numerical multiplier of $x$. In the term $-3x$, that multiplier is $-3$, including its negative sign.
- 5
  - _why:_ Taking the coefficient from $5x^2$ confuses the quadratic term with the linear term. The $x$ term is $-3x$, so its coefficient is $-3$.
- 3
  - _why:_ Ignoring the negative sign changes the coefficient. Since the linear term is $-3x$, the coefficient of $x$ is $-3$, not positive $3$.
- 8
  - _why:_ The number $8$ is the constant term because it has no variable attached. The coefficient of $x$ comes from $-3x$ and is $-3$.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** To factorise $ax^2+bx+c$, find two numbers that multiply to $\text{[...]}$ and add to $b$.

**Back:** To factorise $ax^2+bx+c$, find two numbers that multiply to $\boxed{ac}$ and add to $b$.

**Choices:**

- $$ac$ ✔
  - _why:_ The product must combine the leading coefficient and constant term. Therefore, the two splitting numbers must multiply to $a \times c=ac$.
- $$c$
  - _why:_ Using only the constant term is the monic-quadratic method and overlooks the leading coefficient $a$. For a general quadratic, the product target is $ac$.
- $$b$
  - _why:_ The middle coefficient is the sum target, not the product target. The two numbers must add to $b$ but multiply to $ac$.
- $$ab$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the leading and middle coefficients uses the wrong pair of terms. The required product is the leading coefficient times the constant, namely $ac$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** To factorise $ax^2+bx+c$, find two numbers that multiply to $ac$ and add to $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To factorise $ax^2+bx+c$, find two numbers that multiply to $ac$ and add to $\boxed{b}$.

**Choices:**

- $$b$ ✔
  - _why:_ The two numbers are used to split the middle term $bx$. Their sum must therefore equal $b$, while their product must equal $ac$.
- $$c$
  - _why:_ The constant coefficient contributes to the product target rather than the sum target. The two numbers multiply to $ac$ and must add to $b$.
- $$a$
  - _why:_ The leading coefficient helps form the product target $ac$, but it is not the required sum. The two splitting numbers must add to $b$.
- $$ac$
  - _why:_ The expression $ac$ is the product target, so using it again as the sum confuses the two conditions. The required sum is $b$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** If the constant term $c$ is positive and the $x$ coefficient $b$ is negative, what are the signs of the two numbers?

**Back:** Both are negative.

**Choices:**

- Both are negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A positive product means the numbers have the same sign. Since their sum is negative, both numbers must be negative.
- Both are positive.
  - _why:_ Two positive numbers have a positive sum, which cannot produce a negative middle coefficient. A positive product and negative sum require both numbers to be negative.
- One is positive and one is negative.
  - _why:_ Numbers with opposite signs have a negative product, contradicting the positive constant term. The positive product and negative sum mean both numbers are negative.
- One is zero and one is negative.
  - _why:_ Including zero would make the product zero rather than positive. To give a positive product and a negative sum, both numbers must be negative.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** If the constant term $c$ is negative, what are the signs of the two numbers?

**Back:** One is positive and one is negative.

**Choices:**

- One is positive and one is negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A negative product can only result from multiplying numbers with opposite signs. Therefore, one of the two numbers is positive and the other is negative.
- Both are negative.
  - _why:_ Two negative numbers multiply to give a positive result, not a negative one. A negative constant requires one positive number and one negative number.
- Both are positive.
  - _why:_ Two positive numbers produce a positive product, so they cannot give a negative constant. The two numbers must instead have opposite signs.
- One is zero and one is negative.
  - _why:_ Multiplication by zero gives zero rather than a negative result. A negative product requires exactly one positive factor and one negative factor.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the product target for splitting the middle term of $2x^2+x-15$?

**Back:** -30 ($2 \times -15$)

**Choices:**

- -30 ($2 \times -15$) ✔
  - _why:_ The product target is $ac$, found by multiplying the coefficient of $x^2$ by the constant term: $2 \times -15=-30$.
- 30 ($2 \times 15$)
  - _why:_ Dropping the constant’s negative sign produces a positive product incorrectly. Multiplying the actual coefficients gives $2 \times -15=-30$.
- -13 ($2 + -15$)
  - _why:_ Adding the leading coefficient and constant is not the product-target rule. The target is found by multiplication, giving $2 \times -15=-30$.
- -15 ($1 \times -15$)
  - _why:_ Using the middle coefficient instead of the leading coefficient misses the factor of $2$. The correct product target is $2 \times -15=-30$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Back:** 1

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ The sum target is the coefficient of the middle term. Since the middle term is $+x=1x$, the required sum is $1$.
- -30
  - _why:_ The value $-30$ is the product target $ac$, not the sum target. The sum must equal the coefficient of $x$, which is $1$.
- -15
  - _why:_ The value $-15$ is the constant term rather than the middle coefficient. The coefficient of $x$ is $1$, so the sum target is $1$.
- 3
  - _why:_ Adding the visible coefficients $2$ and $1$ does not determine the sum target. It is simply the coefficient of $x$, which is $1$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Which two numbers multiply to $-30$ and add to $1$?

**Back:** 6 and -5

**Choices:**

- 6 and -5 ✔
  - _why:_ The product is $6 \times -5=-30$, and the sum is $6+(-5)=1$. Both required conditions are therefore satisfied.
- 5 and -6
  - _why:_ Reversing the signs preserves the product $-30$ but changes the sum to $-1$. The required pair is $6$ and $-5$, whose sum is $1$.
- 10 and -3
  - _why:_ Although $10 \times -3=-30$, these numbers add to $7$, not $1$. The pair satisfying both conditions is $6$ and $-5$.
- 6 and 5
  - _why:_ These numbers add to $11$ and multiply to positive $30$, so neither condition is met. The correct pair is $6$ and $-5$.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expression $2x^2+x-15$ is split into $\text{[...]}$ before grouping.

**Back:** The expression $2x^2+x-15$ is split into $\boxed{2x^2+6x-5x-15}$ before grouping.

**Choices:**

- $$2x^2+6x-5x-15$ ✔
  - _why:_ The middle terms must add to $x$ and multiply to $-30$, the product of $2$ and $-15$. Therefore, $6x$ and $-5x$ give the required split.
- $$2x^2+3x-5x-15$
  - _why:_ These middle terms multiply correctly to $-15$ but do not produce the required coefficient of $x$. The correct split uses $6x$ and $-5x$, whose product is $-30$.
- $$2x^2-6x+5x-15$
  - _why:_ The signs make the middle terms combine to $-x$, not $+x$. The correct split is $2x^2+6x-5x-15$, giving both the required sum and product.
- $$2x^2+5x-3x-15$
  - _why:_ These terms add to $2x$, so they do not reproduce the original middle coefficient. The required pair is $6x$ and $-5x$, which add to $x$ and multiply to $-30$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** When factorising by grouping, how do you check if your pair of numbers or signs are correct?

**Back:** Both parts of the expression must contain the same bracket.

**Choices:**

- Both parts of the expression must contain the same bracket. ✔
  - _why:_ After splitting and grouping, both groups should have an identical bracket. This confirms that the chosen pair of numbers and signs allows the expression to be factorised correctly.
- Both parts of the expression must contain opposite brackets.
  - _why:_ Opposite brackets do not provide a common factor for grouping. The correct check is that both parts contain the same bracket, allowing that bracket to be taken out.
- Both parts of the expression must contain the same coefficient.
  - _why:_ A matching coefficient alone does not show that grouping has worked. The essential check is a common bracket appearing in both groups, which can then be factored out.
- Both parts of the expression must contain different brackets.
  - _why:_ Different brackets cannot be taken out as one common factor. Correct grouping produces the same bracket in both parts of the expression.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The zero product property states that if $AB=0$, then $\text{[...]}$ or $B=0$.

**Back:** The zero product property states that if $AB=0$, then $\boxed{A=0}$ or $B=0$.

**Choices:**

- $$A=0$ ✔
  - _why:_ The zero product property says a product is zero only when at least one factor is zero. Thus, if $AB=0$, one possible conclusion is $A=0$.
- $$A=1$
  - _why:_ A factor equal to $1$ leaves the other factor unchanged, so it does not make the product zero. The correct condition is $A=0$, because zero multiplied by any value is zero.
- $$A=-1$
  - _why:_ A factor of $-1$ changes the sign but does not make a product zero. The zero product property requires $A=0$ or $B=0$, so the missing statement is $A=0$.
- $$A=B$
  - _why:_ The two factors do not need to be equal for their product to be zero. At least one must equal zero, and here the missing conclusion is $A=0$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** The zero product property states that if $AB=0$, then $A=0$ or $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The zero product property states that if $AB=0$, then $A=0$ or $\boxed{B=0}$.

**Choices:**

- $$B=0$ ✔
  - _why:_ If the product $AB$ equals zero, at least one factor must be zero. In this part of the statement, the second possible factor is therefore $B=0$.
- $$B=1$
  - _why:_ Setting a factor to $1$ does not make the product zero. The zero product property requires one factor to equal zero, so the correct missing statement is $B=0$.
- $$B=-1$
  - _why:_ A factor of $-1$ produces a nonzero product unless the other factor is zero. The required conclusion here is $B=0$, not a nonzero value.
- $$A=B$
  - _why:_ The factors can have completely different values when their product is zero. One factor must be zero, and in this position the correct conclusion is $B=0$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the roots of a quadratic equation once it is factorised into brackets?

**Back:** Set each bracket equal to zero and solve for $x$.

**Choices:**

- Set each bracket equal to zero and solve for $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ A product is zero when at least one factor is zero. Setting each bracket to zero gives the equations whose solutions are the roots of the factorised quadratic.
- Set each bracket equal to one and solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ A product being zero does not mean its factors equal one. Set each bracket equal to zero, then solve the resulting equations for the roots.
- Set the brackets equal to each other and solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ The factors do not need to have equal values for their product to be zero. Set each bracket equal to zero to find the possible values of $x$.
- Set the constant term equal to zero and solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ The constant term alone does not identify the roots once the quadratic is factorised. Set each bracket equal to zero and solve both resulting equations.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the solutions to $(2x-3)(2x+5)=0$?

**Back:** $x=\frac{3}{2}$$x=-\frac{5}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $x=\frac{3}{2}$$x=-\frac{5}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The zero product property gives $2x-3=0$ or $2x+5=0$. Solving these equations produces $x=\frac{3}{2}$ and $x=-\frac{5}{2}$.
- $x=-\frac{3}{2}$$x=\frac{5}{2}$
  - _why:_ Both signs have been reversed when rearranging the brackets. Solving $2x-3=0$ and $2x+5=0$ correctly gives $x=\frac{3}{2}$ and $x=-\frac{5}{2}$.
- $x=3$$x=-5$
  - _why:_ This misses the division by the coefficient $2$ in each linear equation. The correct roots are $x=\frac{3}{2}$ and $x=-\frac{5}{2}$.
- $x=\frac{2}{3}$$x=-\frac{2}{5}$
  - _why:_ The fractions have been inverted instead of isolating $x$ by dividing the constants by $2$. The correct solutions are $x=\frac{3}{2}$ and $x=-\frac{5}{2}$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What two steps are required to factorise fully $6x^2-24$?

**Back:** Take out the highest common factor 6Use the difference of two squares on $(x^2-4)$

**Choices:**

- Take out the highest common factor 6Use the difference of two squares on $(x^2-4)$ ✔
  - _why:_ First factor out $6$, giving $6(x^2-4)$. Then use the difference of two squares because $x^2-4=x^2-2^2$, so the expression can be factorised completely.
- Take out the highest common factor 3Use the difference of two squares on $(2x^2-8)$
  - _why:_ The highest common factor is $6$, not $3$, since both terms divide by $6$. The correct first step gives $6(x^2-4)$ before applying the difference of two squares.
- Take out the highest common factor 6Use the sum of two squares on $(x^2-4)$
  - _why:_ The expression inside the bracket is a subtraction, not a sum, and $x^2-4$ is a difference of two squares. Use that identity after taking out the factor $6$.
- Take out the highest common factor 2Use the difference of two squares on $(3x^2-12)$
  - _why:_ Although $2$ is a common factor, it is not the highest common factor, so the expression is not fully simplified. Take out $6$ to obtain $6(x^2-4)$.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** The difference of two squares formula is $a^2-b^2=$ $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The difference of two squares formula is $a^2-b^2=$ $\boxed{(a-b)(a+b)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(a-b)(a+b)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The difference of two squares factors because $a^2-b^2$ is the product of the difference and sum of the square roots: $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$(a+b)(a+b)$
  - _why:_ This produces $(a+b)^2$, which includes a positive middle term rather than cancellation. The correct factorisation of a difference of squares is $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$(a-b)^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the difference creates $a^2-2ab+b^2$, not $a^2-b^2$. The correct identity uses two different brackets: $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$a(a-b)$
  - _why:_ This expands to $a^2-ab$, so it does not contain the required $-b^2$ term. The difference of two squares factors as $(a-b)(a+b)$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Factorise $x^2-4$.

**Back:** $$(x-2)(x+2)$

**Choices:**

- $$(x-2)(x+2)$ ✔
  - _why:_ This is a difference of two squares: $x^2-2^2=(x-2)(x+2)$. Expanding the brackets gives $x^2-4$, confirming the factorisation.
- $$(x-2)(x-2)$
  - _why:_ This treats the expression as a perfect square. Expanding gives $x^2-4x+4$, whereas the difference of two squares factorises as $(x-2)(x+2)$.
- $$(x+2)(x+2)$
  - _why:_ This introduces a positive middle term: expanding gives $x^2+4x+4$. Since $x^2-4$ is a difference of squares, the correct factors are $(x-2)(x+2)$.
- $$(x-4)(x+1)$
  - _why:_ Although the constants multiply to $-4$, expanding produces $x^2-3x-4$, including an unwanted middle term. Opposite signs with equal values give $(x-2)(x+2)$.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** To factorise by grouping for $3x(x-4y)-y(x-4y)$, you take out the repeated bracket to get $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To factorise by grouping for $3x(x-4y)-y(x-4y)$, you take out the repeated bracket to get $\boxed{(3x-y)(x-4y)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(3x-y)(x-4y)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms contain the identical factor $(x-4y)$. Taking it outside leaves $3x-y$, so the complete factorisation is $(3x-y)(x-4y)$.
- $$(3x+y)(x-4y)$
  - _why:_ The second original term is subtracted, so taking out $(x-4y)$ leaves $-y$, not $+y$. The correct factorisation is $(3x-y)(x-4y)$.
- $$(3x-y)(x+4y)$
  - _why:_ The repeated bracket is $(x-4y)$, and its sign must remain unchanged when extracted. Replacing it with $(x+4y)$ gives the wrong expansion.
- $$(3x-1)(x-4y)$
  - _why:_ After extracting $(x-4y)$, the remaining terms are $3x$ and $-y$. Dropping the variable from $-y$ is invalid, so the first bracket must be $(3x-y)$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the fastest way to check if a factorisation is correct in an exam?

**Back:** Expand the brackets to see if you return to the original expression.

**Choices:**

- Expand the brackets to see if you return to the original expression. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding multiplies out every term and collects like terms, allowing direct comparison with the original expression. An exact match confirms that the factorisation is correct.
- Factorise the brackets to see if you obtain a different expression.
  - _why:_ Factorising the brackets again does not independently check the work and may repeat the same mistake. Expanding them should reproduce the original expression exactly.
- Substitute one value to see if both expressions give the same result.
  - _why:_ Testing one substituted value may miss an algebraic error because two different expressions can agree at a particular value. Expanding provides a complete check against the original.
- Add the bracket terms to see if you obtain the original expression.
  - _why:_ Adding the contents of brackets ignores the multiplication represented by adjacent factors, so it cannot verify a factorisation. Expand the brackets and compare with the original expression.

## Card 22 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] is a value of the variable that makes an equation true.

**Back:** A root is a value of the variable that makes an equation true.

**Choices:**

- root ✔
  - _why:_ A root is an input value that satisfies an equation, meaning substitution makes the two sides equal. Quadratic equations can have zero, one or two real roots.
- factor
  - _why:_ A factor is an expression or number multiplied by another to form a product, not a value satisfying an equation. Such a satisfying value is called a root.
- coefficient
  - _why:_ A coefficient is the numerical multiplier of a variable, such as $3$ in $3x$. A variable value that makes an equation true is called a root.
- constant
  - _why:_ A constant is a fixed term without a variable, rather than a value chosen to satisfy an equation. The satisfying value of the variable is a root.
