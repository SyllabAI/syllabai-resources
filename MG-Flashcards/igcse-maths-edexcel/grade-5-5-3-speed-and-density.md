## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a compound measure, what does per usually mean?

**Back:** Divided by.

**Choices:**

- Divided by. ✔
  - _why:_ In a compound measure, “per” indicates division. For example, miles per hour means the number of miles divided by the number of hours.
- Multiplied by.
  - _why:_ Multiplication combines quantities rather than forming a rate. In compound measures, “per” means divided by, as in distance divided by time for speed.
- Added to.
  - _why:_ Adding quantities does not produce a compound rate. The word “per” means divided by, such as pounds divided by kilogram in a price rate.
- Subtracted from.
  - _why:_ Subtraction finds a difference rather than a rate. In a compound measure, “per” means divided by, so one quantity is divided by another.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What calculation gives average speed?

**Back:** Distance ÷ time.

**Choices:**

- Distance ÷ time. ✔
  - _why:_ Average speed is found by dividing the total distance travelled by the total time taken, using compatible units for distance and time.
- Distance × time.
  - _why:_ Multiplying distance by time does not calculate a rate. Average speed is the total distance divided by the total time taken.
- Time ÷ distance.
  - _why:_ Reversing the division gives time per unit of distance, not distance per unit of time. Average speed is distance divided by time.
- Distance + time.
  - _why:_ Adding distance and time combines quantities with incompatible units and cannot produce speed. Average speed is calculated as distance divided by time.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** If speed is in mph, time must be in [ ___ ]; if speed is in m/s, time must be in [ ___ ].

**Back:** If speed is in mph, time must be in hours; if speed is in m/s, time must be in seconds.

**Choices:**

- If speed is in mph, time must be in hours; if speed is in m/s, time must be in seconds. ✔
  - _why:_ Miles per hour requires time measured in hours, while metres per second requires time measured in seconds, so the units match each speed.
- If speed is in mph, time must be in minutes; if speed is in m/s, time must be in seconds.
  - _why:_ Using minutes with miles per hour creates inconsistent units. Time must be in hours for mph and in seconds for m/s.
- If speed is in mph, time must be in hours; if speed is in m/s, time must be in minutes.
  - _why:_ Hours match mph, but minutes do not match metres per second. For m/s, the time must be converted into seconds.
- If speed is in mph, time must be in seconds; if speed is in m/s, time must be in hours.
  - _why:_ These time units are both mismatched with the stated speed units. Mph requires hours, whereas m/s requires seconds.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Convert 4 hours 15 minutes into hours.

**Back:** 4.25 hours.

**Choices:**

- 4.25 hours. ✔
  - _why:_ Fifteen minutes is $15 \div 60=0.25$ hours. Adding this to 4 hours gives a total of 4.25 hours.
- 4.15 hours.
  - _why:_ Writing 15 minutes as 0.15 hours treats time as base 100. Since an hour has 60 minutes, 15 minutes is 0.25 hours, giving 4.25 hours.
- 4.5 hours.
  - _why:_ This treats 15 minutes as half an hour. Fifteen minutes is one quarter of 60 minutes, so the correct total is 4.25 hours.
- 4.75 hours.
  - _why:_ This converts 15 minutes into three quarters of an hour instead of one quarter. Dividing 15 by 60 gives 0.25, so the total is 4.25 hours.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** 3 hours 30 minutes is [ ___ ], not [ ___ ].

**Back:** 3 hours 30 minutes is 3.5 hours, not 3.30 hours.

**Choices:**

- 3 hours 30 minutes is 3.5 hours, not 3.30 hours. ✔
  - _why:_ Thirty minutes is half of 60 minutes, so it equals 0.5 hours. Decimal time is not written by simply placing the minutes after a decimal point.
- 3 hours 30 minutes is 3.3 hours, not 3.30 hours.
  - _why:_ Dividing 30 by 100 instead of 60 produces 0.3 hours. Thirty minutes is $30 \div 60=0.5$ hours, so the total is 3.5 hours.
- 3 hours 30 minutes is 3.30 hours, not 3.5 hours.
  - _why:_ This reverses the correct and incorrect conversions. Thirty minutes is half an hour, so the time is 3.5 hours rather than 3.30 hours.
- 3 hours 30 minutes is 3.5 hours, not 3.03 hours.
  - _why:_ The first conversion is correct, but 3.03 hours is not the tempting place-value error. Writing the minutes directly gives 3.30 hours, which is still incorrect.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** A car travels 250 miles in 4 hours 30 minutes. What is its average speed to 1 d.p.?

**Back:** 55.6 mph.

**Choices:**

- 55.6 mph. ✔
  - _why:_ Four hours 30 minutes is 4.5 hours, so the average speed is $250 \div 4.5=55.555\ldots$ mph, which rounds to 55.6 mph.
- 58.8 mph.
  - _why:_ This result comes from treating 4 hours 30 minutes as 4.25 hours. Thirty minutes is 0.5 hours, so $250 \div 4.5$ rounds to 55.6 mph.
- 58.1 mph.
  - _why:_ This result treats 4 hours 30 minutes as 4.3 hours by using decimal place value incorrectly. The journey lasts 4.5 hours, giving 55.6 mph.
- 62.5 mph.
  - _why:_ This ignores the additional 30 minutes and divides 250 miles by only 4 hours. Using the full 4.5 hours gives an average speed of 55.6 mph.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What formula gives time from distance and speed?

**Back:** Time = distance ÷ speed.

**Choices:**

- Time = distance ÷ speed. ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging $\text{speed}=\text{distance}\div\text{time}$ gives time as distance divided by speed. Compatible units then determine the time unit.
- Time = distance × speed.
  - _why:_ Multiplying distance by speed gives incorrect compound units and does not calculate duration. Time is found by dividing distance by speed.
- Time = speed ÷ distance.
  - _why:_ This reverses the required division and gives the reciprocal-type quantity. To calculate journey time, divide the distance by the speed.
- Time = distance + speed.
  - _why:_ Adding distance and speed combines incompatible units, so it cannot give a duration. The correct relationship is time equals distance divided by speed.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Nadia leaves at 08:10 and drives 63 miles at 28 mph. What is her arrival time?

**Back:** 10:25.

**Choices:**

- 10:25. ✔
  - _why:_ The journey takes $63 \div 28=2.25$ hours, and 0.25 hours is 15 minutes. Adding 2 hours 15 minutes to 08:10 gives 10:25.
- 10:35.
  - _why:_ This treats 0.25 hours as 25 minutes rather than converting it using 60 minutes per hour. The journey lasts 2 hours 15 minutes, giving 10:25.
- 10:10.
  - _why:_ This omits the extra 15 minutes represented by 0.25 hours. The travel time is 2 hours 15 minutes, so 08:10 becomes 10:25.
- 09:55.
  - _why:_ This subtracts the 15-minute part instead of adding it to the two full hours. Adding the complete 2 hours 15 minutes gives 10:25.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** Whole-journey average speed = [ ___ ] ÷ [ ___ ].

**Back:** Whole-journey average speed = total distance ÷ total time.

**Choices:**

- Whole-journey average speed = total distance ÷ total time. ✔
  - _why:_ Average speed measures the total distance covered per unit of time across the entire journey, so all distances and all corresponding times must be combined.
- Whole-journey average speed = sum of speeds ÷ number of stages.
  - _why:_ This calculates the arithmetic mean of the stage speeds, which is only valid in special cases such as equal times. Average speed uses total distance divided by total time.
- Whole-journey average speed = total time ÷ total distance.
  - _why:_ This reverses the speed formula and gives time per unit distance instead. Whole-journey average speed is total distance divided by total time.
- Whole-journey average speed = total distance ÷ average time.
  - _why:_ Using an average of the stage times fails to represent the duration of the whole journey. The denominator must be total time, not average time.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is a journey at 60 mph then 40 mph not automatically 50 mph on average?

**Back:** Use total distance ÷ total time.

**Choices:**

- Use total distance ÷ total time. ✔
  - _why:_ The runner or vehicle may spend unequal times at the two speeds, so average speed must be calculated from the journey’s total distance and total time.
- Use total time ÷ total distance.
  - _why:_ This reverses the speed formula, producing time per unit distance rather than speed. The average speed is found using total distance divided by total time.
- Use the most frequent speed.
  - _why:_ The speed maintained most often is the mode, not the average speed for a journey. Average speed requires total distance divided by total time.
- Use the two speeds’ midpoint.
  - _why:_ Taking the midpoint assumes the two speeds apply for equal times. That is not automatic, so the reliable calculation is total distance divided by total time.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** If speed stays constant, how do time and distance change?

**Back:** They increase in the same ratio.

**Choices:**

- They increase in the same ratio. ✔
  - _why:_ Since distance equals speed multiplied by time, a constant speed makes distance directly proportional to time. Multiplying time by any factor multiplies distance by the same factor.
- They increase in an inverse ratio.
  - _why:_ An inverse relationship would mean distance decreases as time increases. At constant speed, doubling the travel time doubles the distance, so they increase in the same ratio.
- They increase by the same amount.
  - _why:_ Equal numerical increases are not required because time and distance have different units. At constant speed, their proportional changes are equal, so they increase in the same ratio.
- They increase in different ratios.
  - _why:_ Different ratios would imply that the speed is changing. When speed remains constant, time and distance are directly proportional and therefore increase in the same ratio.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** If a runner slows down over a longer distance, what happens to a same-speed time estimate?

**Back:** It is too small; real time is longer.

**Choices:**

- It is too small; real time is longer. ✔
  - _why:_ A same-speed estimate assumes the runner maintains the earlier, faster pace. Slowing down means later sections take longer, so the estimate understates the real time.
- It is too large; real time is shorter.
  - _why:_ This reverses the effect of slowing down. Covering part of the route at a lower speed increases the real time, making a same-speed estimate too small.
- It is accurate; real time is unchanged.
  - _why:_ The estimate would only remain accurate if the assumed speed were maintained. Slowing down increases the time needed, so the same-speed estimate is too small.
- It is too large; real time is unchanged.
  - _why:_ This treats the reduction in speed as having no effect on journey time. A lower speed over part of the distance makes real time longer than the estimate.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What calculation gives density?

**Back:** Mass ÷ volume.

**Choices:**

- Mass ÷ volume. ✔
  - _why:_ Density measures how much mass is contained in each unit of volume. Dividing the object’s mass by its volume therefore gives its density.
- Volume ÷ density.
  - _why:_ This is not a valid rearrangement of the density relationship and has unsuitable units. Density is calculated by dividing an object’s mass by its volume.
- Mass × volume.
  - _why:_ Multiplying confuses the operation in the density formula and gives incorrect units. Density measures mass per unit volume, so mass must be divided by volume.
- Volume ÷ mass.
  - _why:_ This gives volume per unit mass, which is the reciprocal of density. Density is mass per unit volume, calculated as mass divided by volume.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What formula gives volume from mass and density?

**Back:** Volume = mass ÷ density.

**Choices:**

- Volume = mass ÷ density. ✔
  - _why:_ Starting from density equals mass divided by volume, multiply by volume and divide by density. This leaves volume equal to mass divided by density.
- Volume = density ÷ mass.
  - _why:_ This reverses the required division and does not follow from density equals mass divided by volume. Rearranging correctly gives volume equals mass divided by density.
- Volume = mass × density.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is a common rearrangement error. Since mass equals density multiplied by volume, isolating volume requires dividing mass by density.
- Volume = density × mass.
  - _why:_ Changing the multiplication order does not fix the incorrect operation. To isolate volume from mass equals density times volume, divide mass by density.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** Mass = [ ___ ] × [ ___ ].

**Back:** Mass = density × volume.

**Choices:**

- Mass = density × volume. ✔
  - _why:_ Density is mass divided by volume. Multiplying both sides by volume gives mass equal to density multiplied by volume.
- Mass = density × area.
  - _why:_ Area does not account for an object’s third dimension, so it cannot determine mass from density. Mass equals density multiplied by the object’s volume.
- Mass = volume × mass.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly includes mass on both sides and omits density, so it cannot calculate an unknown mass. The required product is density multiplied by volume.
- Mass = pressure × volume.
  - _why:_ Pressure describes force per unit area and is not used to calculate mass from volume. The relevant material property is density, so mass equals density times volume.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** Pressure = [ ___ ] ÷ [ ___ ].

**Back:** Pressure = force ÷ area.

**Choices:**

- Pressure = force ÷ area. ✔
  - _why:_ Pressure measures how concentrated a force is over a surface. Dividing the perpendicular force by the area over which it acts gives the pressure.
- Pressure = area ÷ force.
  - _why:_ This reverses the pressure formula and produces the reciprocal units. Pressure measures force distributed over each unit of area, so it is force divided by area.
- Pressure = force ÷ volume.
  - _why:_ Dividing force by volume describes force per cubic unit rather than force acting on a surface. Pressure is force divided by the contact area.
- Pressure = mass ÷ area.
  - _why:_ Mass is not itself a force, although weight can be calculated from mass and gravitational field strength. Pressure requires force divided by area.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** In a liquid mixture question, what should you track separately?

**Back:** Masses and volumes.

**Choices:**

- Masses and volumes. ✔
  - _why:_ Each liquid’s mass and volume must be tracked separately before the totals are combined, because mixture density is total mass divided by total volume.
- Densities and volumes.
  - _why:_ Densities cannot be combined as though they were additive quantities. Track the masses and volumes separately, then divide the total mass by the total volume.
- Masses and densities.
  - _why:_ Tracking density instead of volume leaves no denominator for the final calculation. The masses and volumes must be totalled separately to calculate mixture density.
- Temperatures and volumes.
  - _why:_ Temperature is not one of the quantities needed in this mixture calculation. Track each liquid’s mass and volume, then combine their respective totals.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** Final mixture density = [ ___ ] ÷ [ ___ ].

**Back:** Final mixture density = total mass ÷ total volume.

**Choices:**

- Final mixture density = total mass ÷ total volume. ✔
  - _why:_ Density is mass per unit volume, so the combined mass of all the liquids must be divided by their combined volume.
- Final mixture density = total volume ÷ total mass.
  - _why:_ This reverses the density formula and would give volume per unit mass. Mixture density is found by dividing total mass by total volume.
- Final mixture density = total density ÷ total volume.
  - _why:_ Densities are not added to form a meaningful total density. First find total mass, then divide it by the total volume of the mixture.
- Final mixture density = total mass ÷ total density.
  - _why:_ Density cannot be used as the denominator when it is the quantity being calculated. Divide the mixture’s total mass by its total volume.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Why can’t you add liquid densities directly to find mixture density?

**Back:** Density depends on mass and volume.

**Choices:**

- Density depends on mass and volume. ✔
  - _why:_ Density is the ratio of mass to volume. Different liquid volumes contribute different masses, so both quantities must be totalled before calculating the mixture density.
- Density depends on distance and time.
  - _why:_ Distance and time determine speed, not density. Density depends on mass and volume, which must be combined separately before finding their ratio.
- Density depends on force and area.
  - _why:_ Force divided by area defines pressure, not density. Density depends on mass and volume, so adding densities directly ignores how much of each liquid is present.
- Density depends on mass and acceleration.
  - _why:_ Mass multiplied by acceleration determines force, not density. Density depends on mass and volume, and the mixture uses the totals of both quantities.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** 150 ml at 0.8 g/ml is mixed with 250 ml at 1.2 g/ml. What is the mixture density?

**Back:** 1.05 g/ml.

**Choices:**

- 1.05 g/ml. ✔
  - _why:_ The masses are 150 × 0.8 = 120 g and 250 × 1.2 = 300 g. Therefore, the density is 420 ÷ 400 = 1.05 g/ml.
- 1.00 g/ml.
  - _why:_ This is the simple mean of the two densities, which wrongly assumes equal volumes. Using the actual masses and total volume gives 420 ÷ 400 = 1.05 g/ml.
- 0.95 g/ml.
  - _why:_ This results from pairing each density with the other liquid’s volume. Using 150 ml at 0.8 g/ml and 250 ml at 1.2 g/ml gives 1.05 g/ml.
- 1.20 g/ml.
  - _why:_ This keeps the density of the larger portion and ignores the less dense liquid. The combined mass is 420 g over 400 ml, giving 1.05 g/ml.
