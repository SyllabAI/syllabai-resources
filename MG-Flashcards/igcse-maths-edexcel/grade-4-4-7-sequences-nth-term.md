## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a sequence, what does the word term refer to?

**Back:** A single number or item in the list.

**Choices:**

- A single number or item in the list. ✔
  - _why:_ A term is one individual number or item in a sequence, such as the third number or the fifth shape. The complete list is made up of separate terms.
- The rule used to generate the list.
  - _why:_ This describes a sequence rule, not a term. A term is one individual number or item in the list, whereas the rule explains how terms are produced.
- The difference between consecutive numbers.
  - _why:_ The difference between consecutive terms can be part of a sequence rule, especially for arithmetic sequences. A term itself is one single number or item in the list.
- The complete sequence from start to finish.
  - _why:_ A sequence is the complete ordered list, not one term. A term is a single number or item occupying a particular position in that list.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the purpose of a term-to-term rule?

**Back:** It describes how to calculate the next term from the current one.

**Choices:**

- It describes how to calculate the next term from the current one. ✔
  - _why:_ A term-to-term rule tells you which operation or operations to apply to a known term to obtain the next term in the sequence.
- It identifies the position of each term in the sequence.
  - _why:_ The position is represented by the term number, usually $n$. A term-to-term rule instead describes how to calculate the next term from the current one.
- It calculates every term directly from the term number.
  - _why:_ A formula for the $n$th term calculates a term directly from its position. A term-to-term rule moves from the current term to the next one.
- It gives the first term of the sequence only.
  - _why:_ The first term is starting information rather than a term-to-term rule. The rule describes how to calculate each next term from the current term.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a rule like "multiply by 2, then subtract 1", you must perform the [ ___ ] before the subtraction.

**Back:** For a rule like "multiply by 2, then subtract 1", you must perform the multiplication before the subtraction.

**Choices:**

- multiplication ✔
  - _why:_ The wording gives the operations in order: first multiply by 2, then subtract 1. Therefore, multiplication must be performed before the subtraction.
- addition
  - _why:_ Addition is not one of the stated operations, and it does not come before subtraction in this rule. The first operation is multiplication, followed by subtraction.
- division
  - _why:_ Dividing would change the rule because the instruction specifically says to multiply by 2. Multiplication is the operation that must be performed before subtraction.
- squaring
  - _why:_ Squaring is a different operation from multiplying by 2, so inserting it changes the sequence rule. The required first operation is multiplication.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** In the context of an $n$th term formula, what does the letter $n$ represent?

**Back:** The position (or term number) of the item.

**Choices:**

- The position (or term number) of the item. ✔
  - _why:_ In an $n$th term formula, $n$ tells you which position is being calculated. For example, $n=10$ means the formula gives the tenth term.
- The value of the item in the sequence.
  - _why:_ The value is the result produced by the formula, not what $n$ represents. In an $n$th term formula, $n$ is the position or term number.
- The difference between consecutive terms.
  - _why:_ The difference describes how terms may change, particularly in an arithmetic sequence. The letter $n$ represents the position or term number of the item.
- The number of terms in the whole sequence.
  - _why:_ A sequence may continue indefinitely, so $n$ does not usually count the whole sequence. It identifies the position of the particular term being found.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If the $n$th term is $3n + 4$, how do you find the 10th term?

**Back:** Substitute $n = 10$ into the formula: $3(10) + 4 = 34$.

**Choices:**

- Substitute $n = 10$ into the formula: $3(10) + 4 = 34$. ✔
  - _why:_ The 10th term is found by using $n=10$. Substituting gives $3(10)+4=30+4=34$, so the 10th term is 34.
- Substitute $n = 10$ into the formula: $3 + 10 + 4 = 17$.
  - _why:_ This treats multiplication as addition and ignores the product $3(10)$. The correct substitution is $3(10)+4=30+4=34$.
- Substitute $n = 10$ into the formula: $3(10 - 4) = 18$.
  - _why:_ The $+4$ must be added after calculating $3n$; it is not inside the brackets. Correct substitution gives $3(10)+4=34$.
- Substitute $n = 10$ into the formula: $3(10) + 4 = 30$.
  - _why:_ This calculates $3(10)$ but then fails to add the remaining 4. The full expression is $30+4=34$, so the 10th term is 34.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you check if a specific number (e.g., 31) is a term in a sequence given its $n$th term?

**Back:** Set the formula equal to the number.Solve for $n$.If $n$ is a positive whole number, it is a term.

**Choices:**

- Set the formula equal to the number.Solve for $n$.If $n$ is a positive whole number, it is a term. ✔
  - _why:_ Equating the $n$th term formula to the proposed number finds its position. A positive whole-number value of $n$ is a valid term position, so the number belongs to the sequence.
- Set the formula equal to the position.Solve for the number.If the answer is positive, it is a term.
  - _why:_ This reverses the test: the formula must equal the proposed number, and you solve for $n$. The result must be a positive whole number to give a valid position.
- Set the formula equal to zero.Solve for $n$.If $n$ is a whole number, it is a term.
  - _why:_ Setting the formula equal to zero checks when the sequence might have value zero, not whether the proposed number occurs. You must set it equal to that number and require a positive whole-number $n$.
- Substitute the number for $n$.Calculate the term.If the answer is whole, it is a term.
  - _why:_ The proposed number is a term value, not its position, so it should not replace $n$. Set the formula equal to the number and solve for a positive whole-number position.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $n = 6.5$ an invalid result when checking if a number is in a sequence?

**Back:** Term positions must be positive whole numbers (integers).

**Choices:**

- Term positions must be positive whole numbers (integers). ✔
  - _why:_ A term position must be $1,2,3,ldots$, so it must be a positive integer. Since $6.5$ is not a whole number, it cannot identify a term in the sequence.
- Term positions must be positive numbers (including decimals).
  - _why:_ Although $6.5$ is positive, term positions count individual places and cannot be fractional. They must be positive whole numbers, or positive integers.
- Term positions must be whole numbers (including zero).
  - _why:_ Whole numbers include zero, but sequence positions normally begin at 1, not 0. Valid positions must be positive whole numbers, and $6.5$ is still invalid because it is fractional.
- Term positions can be any integers (positive or negative).
  - _why:_ Negative integers do not represent ordinary term positions, which start at 1. Positions must be positive whole numbers, and decimals such as $6.5$ are not allowed.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** An [ ___ ] sequence is one where the same amount is added or subtracted each time.

**Back:** An arithmetic sequence is one where the same amount is added or subtracted each time.

**Choices:**

- arithmetic ✔
  - _why:_ Arithmetic sequences have a constant difference between consecutive terms, meaning the same fixed amount is added or subtracted at every step.
- exponential
  - _why:_ Exponential sequences change by repeatedly multiplying by the same factor, not by adding or subtracting a fixed amount. The correct term is arithmetic.
- increasing
  - _why:_ An increasing sequence rises but need not change by a fixed amount, and subtraction could make a sequence decrease. A constant difference defines an arithmetic sequence.
- alternating
  - _why:_ Alternating sequences switch between values or signs rather than necessarily having a constant difference. Adding or subtracting the same amount produces an arithmetic sequence.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** In an arithmetic sequence formula, the number multiplying $n$ is the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In an arithmetic sequence formula, the number multiplying $n$ is the common difference.

**Choices:**

- common difference ✔
  - _why:_ In a linear expression for an arithmetic sequence, the coefficient of $n$ equals the fixed change between consecutive terms, called the common difference.
- common ratio
  - _why:_ The common ratio is the multiplying factor in a geometric sequence. For an arithmetic sequence, the coefficient of $n$ is the common difference.
- first term
  - _why:_ The first term helps determine the constant adjustment, but it is not generally the coefficient of $n$. That coefficient is the common difference.
- constant adjustment
  - _why:_ The constant adjustment is the separate number added to the $n$ term. The number multiplying $n$ represents the common difference.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** If an arithmetic sequence decreases by 4 each time, what is the start of its $n$th term formula?

**Back:** $$-4n$

**Choices:**

- $$-4n$ ✔
  - _why:_ A decrease of 4 gives a common difference of $-4$. Therefore, the coefficient of $n$ is $-4$, so the formula starts with $-4n$.
- $$4n$
  - _why:_ Using a positive coefficient would produce a sequence that increases by 4. A decrease of 4 requires the negative coefficient, so the formula starts with $-4n$.
- $$n-4$
  - _why:_ Subtracting 4 as a constant does not make consecutive terms decrease by 4; the coefficient of $n$ would still be 1. The required start is $-4n$.
- $$-n-4$
  - _why:_ A coefficient of $-1$ makes the sequence decrease by only 1 each time. To decrease by 4, the coefficient must be $-4$, giving $-4n$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the first three terms of the sequence with $n$th term $n^2 + 2$?

**Back:** $n=1 \rightarrow 3$$n=2 \rightarrow 6$$n=3 \rightarrow 11$

**Choices:**

- $n=1 \rightarrow 3$$n=2 \rightarrow 6$$n=3 \rightarrow 11$ ✔
  - _why:_ Substituting $1$, $2$ and $3$ into $n^2+2$ gives $1+2=3$, $4+2=6$ and $9+2=11$.
- $n=1 \rightarrow 1$$n=2 \rightarrow 4$$n=3 \rightarrow 9$
  - _why:_ These values come from calculating only $n^2$ and forgetting to add 2. Including the constant gives the first three terms $3$, $6$ and $11$.
- $n=1 \rightarrow 2$$n=2 \rightarrow 3$$n=3 \rightarrow 6$
  - _why:_ These results incorrectly use inputs $0$, $1$ and $2$. Sequence positions begin at $n=1$, so the first three terms are $3$, $6$ and $11$.
- $n=1 \rightarrow 3$$n=2 \rightarrow 8$$n=3 \rightarrow 15$
  - _why:_ These values treat the expression as $n^2+2n$ rather than $n^2+2$. Adding a constant 2 gives $3$, $6$ and $11$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Find the $n$th term of the sequence: 8, 13, 18, 23.

**Back:** $$5n + 3$

**Choices:**

- $$5n + 3$ ✔
  - _why:_ The common difference is 5, so the formula begins $5n$. At $n=1$, subtracting 5 from the first term 8 gives the adjustment 3.
- $$5n + 8$
  - _why:_ Using 8 directly as the constant forgets that $5n$ already contributes 5 when $n=1$. The required adjustment is $8-5=3$, giving $5n+3$.
- $$5n - 3$
  - _why:_ The sign of the adjustment has been reversed. Since $5n$ gives 5 at $n=1$, 3 must be added to reach 8, giving $5n+3$.
- $$8n + 5$
  - _why:_ This confuses the first term with the common difference. Consecutive terms rise by 5, so the coefficient is 5, and the correct formula is $5n+3$.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the first negative term of a decreasing sequence, you should solve the inequality $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To find the first negative term of a decreasing sequence, you should solve the inequality $\boxed{n\text{th term} < 0}$.

**Choices:**

- $$n\text{th term} < 0$ ✔
  - _why:_ Negative values are precisely those below zero. Solving this inequality identifies the positions where the sequence is negative, and the smallest valid integer $n$ gives the first negative term.
- $$n\text{th term} > 0$
  - _why:_ This inequality finds positive terms rather than negative ones. To locate the first negative term, solve $n\text{th term}<0$ and choose the smallest valid integer $n$.
- $$n\text{th term} = 0$
  - _why:_ Solving an equation equal to zero only finds a zero term, which may not even occur. Negative terms are found using $n\text{th term}<0$.
- $$n\text{th term} \leq 0$
  - _why:_ Including equality can identify a zero term before the sequence becomes negative. The strict inequality $n\text{th term}<0$ is needed to find the first genuinely negative term.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the adjustment (the constant term) when deriving an arithmetic $n$th term?

**Back:** Compare the first term of the sequence to the result of substituting $n = 1$ into the $dn$ part of the formula.

**Choices:**

- Compare the first term of the sequence to the result of substituting $n = 1$ into the $dn$ part of the formula. ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficient $d$ gives the common-difference part. Comparing its value at $n=1$ with the actual first term reveals the constant needed to make the formula match.
- Compare the first term of the sequence to the result of substituting $n = 0$ into the $dn$ part of the formula.
  - _why:_ Sequence indexing normally begins at $n=1$, not $n=0$. The adjustment is found by comparing the first term with the value of $dn$ at $n=1$.
- Use the first term of the sequence itself as the constant added to the $dn$ part of the formula.
  - _why:_ This overlooks the contribution already made by $dn$ when $n=1$. The correct constant is the difference between the actual first term and that contribution.
- Compare the second term of the sequence to the result of substituting $n = 1$ into the $dn$ part of the formula.
  - _why:_ This mismatches the sequence position with the value of $n$, producing an adjustment that is one common difference too large. Compare the first term using $n=1$ instead.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** When calculating the $n$th term of a decreasing sequence, a common mistake is forgetting that the common difference is [ ___ ].

**Back:** When calculating the $n$th term of a decreasing sequence, a common mistake is forgetting that the common difference is negative.

**Choices:**

- negative ✔
  - _why:_ A decreasing arithmetic sequence has a common difference below zero because each term is smaller than the previous one by a fixed amount.
- positive
  - _why:_ A positive common difference makes an arithmetic sequence increase rather than decrease. For a decreasing sequence, the fixed change between terms must be negative.
- zero
  - _why:_ A zero common difference produces a constant sequence in which every term is equal. A decreasing arithmetic sequence instead requires a negative common difference.
- variable
  - _why:_ An arithmetic sequence must have a fixed common difference, not one that varies between terms. For a decreasing arithmetic sequence, that fixed difference is negative.

**Front:** Find the $n$th term of the sequence: 34, 30, 26, 22.

**Back:** $$-4n + 38$

**Choices:**

- $$-4n + 38$ ✔
  - _why:_ The common difference is $-4$, so the coefficient of $n$ is $-4$. Since the first term occurs at $n=1$, the constant is $34+4=38$.
- $$-4n + 34$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the first term as the constant. Substituting $n=1$ gives $30$, not $34$; adjusting the intercept gives the correct term $-4n+38$.
- $$4n + 30$
  - _why:_ This makes the coefficient positive, so it generates an increasing sequence. The terms decrease by $4$, and using the first term gives the correct expression $-4n+38$.
- $$-4n + 42$
  - _why:_ This constant is $4$ too large because the indexing has been shifted by one place. At $n=1$ it gives $38$, whereas the correct formula is $-4n+38$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** When finding an $n$th term for a pattern of tiles, which part of the drawing usually represents the numerical adjustment (the constant)?

**Back:** The fixed part (the tiles that do not change).

**Choices:**

- The fixed part (the tiles that do not change). ✔
  - _why:_ The fixed tiles remain unchanged as the pattern number increases, so they provide the constant numerical adjustment in an expression of the form $an+b$.
- The growing part (the tiles added in each new pattern).
  - _why:_ The growing part determines the coefficient of $n$ because it changes with the pattern number. The numerical adjustment comes from the fixed part, which does not change.
- The first pattern (all the tiles shown at the start).
  - _why:_ The first pattern includes both growing and fixed tiles, so it does not by itself represent the constant. The adjustment is shown by the fixed part alone.
- The repeated part (the tiles multiplied by the pattern number).
  - _why:_ The repeated part is multiplied by $n$, so it determines the coefficient rather than the constant. The fixed tiles represent the numerical adjustment.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Find the $n$th term of the sequence: 3, 10, 17, 24.

**Back:** $$7n - 4$

**Choices:**

- $$7n - 4$ ✔
  - _why:_ The sequence increases by $7$, so the coefficient of $n$ is $7$. Since $7(1)=7$, subtracting $4$ produces the first term $3$.
- $$7n + 3$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats the first term as the constant. Substituting $n=1$ gives $10$, not $3$; subtracting $7$ from the first term gives $7n-4$.
- $$7n + 4$
  - _why:_ This uses the wrong sign for the adjustment, giving $11$ when $n=1$. To reduce $7$ to the first term $3$, the formula must subtract $4$.
- $$3n + 7$
  - _why:_ This swaps the first term and common difference, producing a sequence that increases by $3$. The actual common difference is $7$, so the formula is $7n-4$.
