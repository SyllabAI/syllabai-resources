## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the range describe about a set of data?

**Back:** How spread out the data values are.

**Choices:**

- How spread out the data values are. ✔
  - _why:_ The range measures the spread of the data by comparing its largest and smallest values. A larger range generally indicates that the values are more spread out.
- What the average of the data values is.
  - _why:_ This describes the mean, which indicates the central value obtained by sharing the total equally. The range instead describes how spread out the data values are.
- Which data value appears most often.
  - _why:_ This describes the mode, which identifies the value occurring most frequently. The range instead describes how spread out the data values are.
- Where the middle of the data values lies.
  - _why:_ This describes the median, which is the middle value after ordering the data. The range instead describes how spread out the data values are.

**Back:** The formula for the range is: $\text{range} = \boxed{\text{largest value}} - \text{smallest value}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\text{largest value}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The range is calculated by subtracting the smallest value from the largest value, giving the distance between the two extremes of the data set.
- $$\text{mean value}$
  - _why:_ Using the mean confuses a measure of central tendency with an extreme value. The correct first term is $\text{largest value}$, from which the smallest value is subtracted.
- $$\text{median value}$
  - _why:_ Using the median confuses the middle value with the upper extreme. The range is $\text{largest value} - \text{smallest value}$.
- $$\text{smallest value}$
  - _why:_ Starting with the smallest value would mean subtracting it from itself, always producing zero. The correct first term is $\text{largest value}$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How should repeated values be handled when putting a list in order?

**Back:** Write them every time they appear; they are essential for the mode, median, and mean.

**Choices:**

- Write them every time they appear; they are essential for the mode, median, and mean. ✔
  - _why:_ Each occurrence contributes to the frequency and total, and it can affect the middle position. Repeated values must therefore be retained when finding the mode, median, or mean.
- Write them only once in the list; duplicates do not affect any of the averages.
  - _why:_ Removing repeats changes frequencies, the number of values, and usually the total, producing unreliable averages. Write repeated values every time they appear.
- Write them at most twice in the list; further copies can always be ignored.
  - _why:_ Keeping only two copies arbitrarily changes the data whenever a value occurs more often. Every occurrence must be written because each one affects the averages.
- Write them only for the mode; they are irrelevant when calculating the median and mean.
  - _why:_ Repeated values affect more than the mode: they can change the median’s position and the mean’s total. Write them every time they appear.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the median if there is an even number of values?

**Back:** Identify the two middle valuesFind their mean (add them and divide by 2)

**Choices:**

- Identify the two middle valuesFind their mean (add them and divide by 2) ✔
  - _why:_ An even-sized ordered data set has two central values. Their mean gives the point halfway between them, which is the median of the data.
- Identify the two middle valuesFind their sum (add them without dividing)
  - _why:_ Adding the two middle values without dividing makes the result twice as large as their mean. The median is their sum divided by 2.
- Identify the two end valuesFind their mean (add them and divide by 2)
  - _why:_ Using the end values finds the midrange, not the median. For an even number of values, identify the two middle values and calculate their mean.
- Identify the two middle valuesFind their difference (subtract the smaller from the larger)
  - _why:_ The difference measures the gap between the middle values, not their central value. The median is found by adding them and dividing by 2.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Back:** The mean is calculated as: $\text{mean} = \frac{\boxed{\text{total of values}}}{\text{number of values}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\text{total of values}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The mean shares the combined total equally among all the values, so its numerator is the total obtained by adding every value in the data set.
- $$\text{number of values}$
  - _why:_ Using the count as both the numerator and denominator would always give 1. The numerator must be the total of values, divided by the number of values.
- $$\text{largest value}$
  - _why:_ The largest value is the maximum and does not represent the combined data. The mean uses the total of all values as its numerator.
- $$\text{range of values}$
  - _why:_ The range is the largest value minus the smallest and measures spread. The mean is calculated using the total of values as the numerator.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Back:** The mean is calculated as: $\text{mean} = \frac{\text{total of values}}{\boxed{\text{number of values}}}$.

**Choices:**

**Front:** How do you find a single missing value if the mean and all other values are known?

**Back:** Calculate the full total (mean $\times$ count)Subtract the sum of known values from that total

**Choices:**

- Calculate the full total (mean $\times$ count)Subtract the sum of known values from that total ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying the mean by the count reconstructs the total of every value. Removing the sum of the known values then leaves exactly the single missing value.
- Calculate the full total (mean $\div$ count)Subtract the sum of known values from that total
  - _why:_ Dividing the mean by the count reverses the relationship incorrectly and produces no useful total. The full total is mean multiplied by count, followed by subtracting the known sum.
- Calculate the full total (mean $\times$ count)Add the sum of known values to that total
  - _why:_ Adding the known values counts them twice because they are already included in the full total. Their sum must be subtracted from mean multiplied by count.
- Calculate the known total (mean $\times$ count)Divide the sum of known values by that total
  - _why:_ The given mean and count produce the full total, not merely the known total. Subtracting the known sum isolates the missing value; dividing the two totals does not.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it useful to write down the total in mean calculations?

**Back:** It helps secure method marks and is necessary for solving missing value questions.

**Choices:**

- It helps secure method marks and is necessary for solving missing value questions. ✔
  - _why:_ Writing the total shows the key step mean multiplied by count, which can earn method marks. A missing value is then found by subtracting the known values from this total.
- It helps secure accuracy marks and is necessary for solving range questions.
  - _why:_ The range only needs the largest and smallest values, so the total is unnecessary. Writing the total mainly demonstrates the mean method and supports missing-value calculations.
- It helps identify the middle position and is necessary for solving median value questions.
  - _why:_ Median questions depend on ordering the values and locating the middle, not calculating their total. The total is instead useful for method marks and missing-value mean questions.
- It helps identify the most frequent value and is necessary for solving mode value questions.
  - _why:_ The mode is identified by counting how often each value appears, so the total is not required. Totals are useful when showing mean methods and finding missing values.
