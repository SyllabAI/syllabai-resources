## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard formula for triangle area using base and height?

**Back:** $$A = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A triangle has half the area of a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height, so multiply base by height and divide by 2.
- $$A = \text{base} \times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of one half and gives the area of a rectangle. A triangle with the same base and perpendicular height has half that area, so the correct formula includes $\frac{1}{2}$.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{base} + \text{height})$
  - _why:_ Adding the base and height does not produce an area and mixes unlike measurements. The correct formula multiplies the base by the perpendicular height, then takes half the product.
- $$A = 2 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 2 makes the result twice the correct triangle area. The triangle occupies half of the corresponding base-height rectangle, so the factor must be $\frac{1}{2}$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In the standard triangle area formula, what does perpendicular height represent?

**Back:** The 90&deg; distance from the opposite vertex to the base.

**Choices:**

- The 90&deg; distance from the opposite vertex to the base. ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular height is the shortest distance from the vertex opposite the chosen base, meeting that base or its extension at a right angle.
- The 90&deg; distance from the base midpoint to the opposite vertex.
  - _why:_ The height is measured from the opposite vertex to the chosen base at 90 degrees; the foot of the perpendicular need not be the base midpoint.
- The sloping side distance from the opposite vertex to the base.
  - _why:_ A sloping side is not necessarily perpendicular to the base, so it cannot generally be used as the height. The correct height is the 90&deg; distance from the opposite vertex.
- The 90&deg; distance from the opposite vertex to the nearest side.
  - _why:_ Height is defined relative to the selected base, not whichever side happens to be nearest. It is the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to that base.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for triangle area using two sides and an angle.

**Back:** $$A = \frac{1}{2} ab \sin \theta$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{1}{2} ab \sin \theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ When two sides and their included angle are known, the perpendicular height is one side multiplied by $\sin \theta$, giving $A = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin\theta$.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2} ab \cos \theta$
  - _why:_ Cosine gives the component adjacent to the angle, not the perpendicular height needed for area. The correct formula uses $\sin \theta$ for the included angle.
- $$A = ab \sin \theta$
  - _why:_ This misses the triangle's factor of one half and therefore doubles the area. The two-side formula is $A = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin\theta$.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)\sin \theta$
  - _why:_ The two side lengths must be multiplied, not added, because one supplies the base and the other determines the height. The correct expression is $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin\theta$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Define the included angle in a triangle.

**Back:** The angle located between two given side lengths.

**Choices:**

- The angle located between two given side lengths. ✔
  - _why:_ The included angle is the angle formed where the two known sides meet. It lies between those side lengths and is the angle used in $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin\theta$.
- The angle located opposite two given side lengths.
  - _why:_ An angle opposite a side is not the included angle for the two-side area formula. The included angle is where the two given side lengths meet, between them.
- The angle located outside two given side lengths.
  - _why:_ An exterior angle lies outside the triangle and is not normally the included angle required by the formula. The required angle is the interior angle between the two given sides.
- The angle located beside two given side lengths.
  - _why:_ Being beside the sides is not a precise geometric condition and could describe several angles. The included angle is specifically the angle between the two given side lengths.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a right-angled triangle, $\sin \theta = \frac{\text{[...]}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$

**Back:** In a right-angled triangle, $\sin \theta = \frac{\boxed{\text{opposite}}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$

**Choices:**

- opposite ✔
  - _why:_ For a chosen angle in a right-angled triangle, sine is defined as opposite divided by hypotenuse, so the numerator is the side opposite $\theta$.
- adjacent
  - _why:_ Adjacent divided by hypotenuse defines cosine, not sine. For sine, the numerator is the side opposite $\theta$, so the correct term is opposite.
- hypotenuse
  - _why:_ Using hypotenuse in both numerator and denominator would make the ratio 1, which is not the sine rule. Sine uses opposite over hypotenuse.
- included angle
  - _why:_ An angle is not a side length and cannot be the numerator of this right-triangle ratio. Sine compares the opposite side with the hypotenuse.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a right-angled triangle, $\sin \theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{[...]}}$

**Back:** In a right-angled triangle, $\sin \theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\boxed{\text{hypotenuse}}}$

**Choices:**

- hypotenuse ✔
  - _why:_ Sine is the ratio of the opposite side to the hypotenuse, which is always the longest side and lies opposite the right angle.
- opposite
  - _why:_ Opposite belongs in the numerator of the sine ratio, not its denominator. The denominator is the hypotenuse, so $\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$.
- adjacent
  - _why:_ Adjacent over hypotenuse is the cosine ratio. Sine uses the opposite side over the hypotenuse, so the denominator here must be hypotenuse.
- right angle
  - _why:_ The right angle identifies the triangle's hypotenuse but is not a side that can appear in a trigonometric ratio. The required denominator is hypotenuse.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the height $h$ expressed in terms of side $b$ and included angle $\theta$?

**Back:** $$h = b \sin \theta$

**Choices:**

- $$h = b \sin \theta$ ✔
  - _why:_ The side $b$ is the hypotenuse of the right triangle formed by the height, and the height is opposite $\theta$. Therefore $h = b\sin\theta$.
- $$h = b \cos \theta$
  - _why:_ Cosine gives the component of $b$ adjacent to $\theta$, not the perpendicular height. Since the height is opposite the angle, use $h = b\sin\theta$.
- $$h = a \sin \theta$
  - _why:_ The stated side determining the height is $b$, so replacing it with $a$ uses the wrong side. Resolving $b$ perpendicular to the base gives $h = b\sin\theta$.
- $$h = b \tan \theta$
  - _why:_ Tangent relates opposite and adjacent sides, so using it with $b$ as though it were the adjacent side gives the wrong relationship. The perpendicular component is $b\sin\theta$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the requirement for using the formula $A = \frac{1}{2} ab \sin \theta$?

**Back:** The angle $\theta$ must be between sides $a$ and $b$.

**Choices:**

- The angle $\theta$ must be between sides $a$ and $b$. ✔
  - _why:_ The sine area formula uses the angle formed by the two known sides. That included angle determines the perpendicular component and must lie between $a$ and $b$.
- The angle $\theta$ must be opposite sides $a$ and $b$.
  - _why:_ An opposite angle does not form the perpendicular component between the two known sides. The formula requires the included angle, which is located between $a$ and $b$.
- The angle $\theta$ must be outside sides $a$ and $b$.
  - _why:_ The formula is based on the interior angle where the two sides meet, not an exterior angle. Therefore $\theta$ must be the included angle between $a$ and $b$.
- The angle $\theta$ must be perpendicular to sides $a$ and $b$.
  - _why:_ An angle cannot be perpendicular to both sides in the required sense. The sides and angle must form a triangle vertex, with $\theta$ between $a$ and $b$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the sine area formula valid for obtuse triangles?

**Back:** The sine of an obtuse angle is positive.

**Choices:**

- The sine of an obtuse angle is positive. ✔
  - _why:_ Sine is positive for angles between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, including obtuse angles. Therefore, the sine area formula produces a positive area for every interior triangle angle.
- The sine of an obtuse angle is negative.
  - _why:_ This confuses the second quadrant with quadrants where sine is negative. An obtuse angle lies between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, where sine is positive, so the area remains positive.
- The sine of an obtuse angle is greater than one.
  - _why:_ Sine values always lie between $-1$ and $1$. For an obtuse triangle angle, sine is positive but no greater than $1$, making the area formula valid.
- The sine of an obtuse angle is equal to zero.
  - _why:_ Sine equals zero at $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, not at an obtuse interior angle. Between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, sine is positive.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Back:** Degree (DEG) mode.

**Choices:**

- Degree (DEG) mode. ✔
  - _why:_ IGCSE triangle angles are normally measured in degrees, so the calculator must use DEG mode to evaluate trigonometric functions and inverse trigonometric functions correctly.

**Choices:**

- Rearrange the formula to isolate $\sin x$Apply inverse sine ($\sin^{-1}$) to the result ✔

- Rearrange the formula to isolate $\cos x$Apply inverse cosine ($\cos^{-1}$) to the result
  - _why:_ This substitutes cosine for the sine appearing in the triangle area formula. The correct method isolates $\sin x$ and then applies inverse sine to find $x$.
- Rearrange the formula to isolate $\tan x$Apply inverse tangent ($\tan^{-1}$) to the result
  - _why:_ Tangent is used for opposite-to-adjacent ratios, not in the sine area formula. Isolate $\sin x$ and apply inverse sine to obtain the angle.

  - _why:_ The angle is inside the sine function, so ordinary algebra cannot isolate $x$ directly. First isolate $\sin x$, then apply inverse sine to both sides.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** For any angle $x$ in a triangle, $\sin x$ has the same value as $\sin(\text{[...]})$.

**Back:** For any angle $x$ in a triangle, $\sin x$ has the same value as $\sin(\boxed{180^\circ - x})$.

**Choices:**

- $$180^\circ - x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Supplementary angles have equal sine values because their reference angles are equal in the first and second quadrants. Thus $\sin x = \sin(180^\circ-x)$.
- $$180^\circ + x$
  - _why:_ Adding $180^\circ$ moves the angle to a point where its sine has the opposite sign. The equal positive sine occurs at the supplementary angle $180^\circ-x$.
- $$90^\circ - x$
  - _why:_ This is the complementary angle, whose sine equals $\cos x$, not generally $\sin x$. The angle sharing the same sine is $180^\circ-x$.
- $$360^\circ - x$
  - _why:_ Reflecting an angle in $360^\circ$ reverses the sign of its sine in general. The supplementary angle $180^\circ-x$ has the same sine value.

**Choices:**

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** If two sides are in the ratio 3:2, how should they be expressed algebraically in the area formula?

**Back:** $$3k$ and $2k$ (where $k$ is a multiplier).

**Choices:**

- $$3k$ and $2k$ (where $k$ is a multiplier). ✔
  - _why:_ A ratio of $3:2$ means the sides share a common multiplier. Writing them as $3k$ and $2k$ preserves that ratio for any positive value of $k$.
- $$3k$ and $k$ (where $k$ is a multiplier).
  - _why:_ These expressions have ratio $3:1$, not $3:2$, because the second side has lost its factor of $2$. The sides should be $3k$ and $2k$.
- $$3k$ and $5k$ (where $k$ is a multiplier).
  - _why:_ Adding the ratio parts to obtain $5k$ is a common error, but it changes the ratio to $3:5$. The correct expressions are $3k$ and $2k$.
- $$2k$ and $3k$ (where $k$ is a multiplier).
  - _why:_ This reverses the stated order and represents the ratio $2:3$. To match a first side to second side ratio of $3:2$, use $3k$ and $2k$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the general rule for rounding during multi-step area calculations?

**Back:** Keep the full calculator value until the final step.

**Choices:**

- Keep the full calculator value until the final step. ✔
  - _why:_ Retaining the full calculator value prevents intermediate rounding errors from accumulating. Round only the final area to the accuracy requested in the question.
- Round the calculator value after every step.
  - _why:_ Rounding after every step can accumulate errors and alter the final area. Keep the full calculator value throughout, then round only the final answer.
- Round every calculator value to 2 decimal places.
  - _why:_ Automatically using 2 decimal places may discard useful precision and is not a general rule. Keep full calculator values until the final step, then use the requested accuracy.
- Round the first calculator value before continuing.
  - _why:_ Rounding the first intermediate value introduces an avoidable error into every later calculation. Keep the full calculator value until the final step.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Leading zeros only locate the decimal point and are not significant. Counting therefore begins with the first digit that is not zero.
- decimal
  - _why:_ The decimal point does not determine where significant figures begin. Counting starts at the first non-zero digit, whether it appears before or after the decimal point.
- even
  - _why:_ Significance is unrelated to whether a digit is even or odd. Significant figures are counted from the first non-zero digit in the number.
- repeated
  - _why:_ A digit does not need to appear more than once to be significant. Counting begins at the first non-zero digit, including any zeros that follow it.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What type of equation is typically formed when using the area formula with side lengths like $x$ and $x+6$?

**Back:** A quadratic equation (involving $x^2$).

**Choices:**

- A quadratic equation (involving $x^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying side lengths such as $x$ and $x+6$ produces $x(x+6)=x^2+6x$. Equating this area expression to a value therefore forms a quadratic equation.
- A linear equation (involving $x$ only).
  - _why:_ Treating the equation as linear overlooks the product $x(x+6)$, which creates an $x^2$ term. The resulting equation is therefore quadratic.
- A cubic equation (involving $x^3$).
  - _why:_ A cubic would require a product producing an $x^3$ term. Multiplying the two linear side expressions produces at most $x^2$, so the equation is quadratic.
- A constant equation (involving no $x$).
  - _why:_ The side lengths depend on $x$, so their product also contains $x$. Expanding $x(x+6)$ gives $x^2+6x$, producing a quadratic equation.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** If a question provides an obtuse angle and the area, but your calculator gives an acute angle $x$, how do you find the correct obtuse angle?

**Back:** Calculate $180^\circ - x$.

**Choices:**

- Calculate $180^\circ - x$. ✔
  - _why:_ Sine has the same positive value for supplementary angles. If the calculator returns the acute solution $x$, the required obtuse solution is its supplement, $180^\circ-x$.
- Calculate $180^\circ + x$.
  - _why:_ Adding $x$ produces an angle greater than $180^\circ$, not an obtuse interior angle of a triangle. The supplementary solution is $180^\circ-x$.
- Calculate $90^\circ - x$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting from $90^\circ$ finds a complementary angle, which remains acute. The angle sharing the same sine value is supplementary, so calculate $180^\circ-x$.
- Calculate $360^\circ - x$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting from $360^\circ$ gives a reflex angle, which cannot be an interior angle of a triangle. The correct obtuse solution is $180^\circ-x$.
