## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard form of a quadratic equation?

**Back:** $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$

**Choices:**

- $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ✔
  - _why:_ Standard quadratic form places all terms on one side in descending powers of $x$ and sets the resulting quadratic expression equal to zero.
- $$ax^2 + bx + c = 1$
  - _why:_ Setting the expression equal to one does not give the standard form. All terms must be collected so the equation is $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
- $$ax^2 + bx = c$
  - _why:_ Leaving the constant on the right means the equation has not been written in standard form. It should be rearranged to $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
- $$ax^3 + bx^2 + c = 0$
  - _why:_ A highest power of three makes this a cubic equation. A quadratic has highest power two and standard form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why must the coefficient $a$ be non-zero ($a \neq 0$) in a quadratic equation?

**Back:** If $a=0$, there is no $x^2$ term, making the equation linear rather than quadratic.

**Choices:**

- If $a=0$, there is no $x^2$ term, making the equation linear rather than quadratic. ✔
  - _why:_ The non-zero coefficient of $x^2$ is what makes the equation quadratic. Setting $a=0$ removes that term, leaving an equation of degree at most one.
- If $a=0$, there is no $x$ term, making the equation constant rather than quadratic.
  - _why:_ The coefficient $a$ multiplies $x^2$, not $x$. If $a=0$, the quadratic term disappears and the equation becomes linear rather than constant.
- If $a=0$, the coefficient of $x^2$ becomes $1$, making the equation monic rather than quadratic.
  - _why:_ Setting a coefficient to zero removes its term; it does not turn the coefficient into one. With no $x^2$ term, the equation is linear rather than monic.
- If $a=0$, there is no $x^3$ term, making the equation cubic rather than quadratic.
  - _why:_ A cubic equation requires a non-zero $x^3$ term, which is not created by setting $a=0$. Instead, the $x^2$ term vanishes and the equation becomes linear.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Where are the roots of a quadratic equation found on its graph?

**Back:** Where the parabola crosses the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- Where the parabola crosses the x-axis. ✔
  - _why:_ At an x-intercept, the graph has $y=0$. Therefore, the corresponding x-coordinates satisfy the quadratic equation and are its roots.
- Where the parabola crosses the y-axis.
  - _why:_ The y-intercept is found by setting $x=0$ and usually represents the constant term. Roots occur where $y=0$, so they lie on the x-axis.
- Where the parabola reaches its turning point.
  - _why:_ The turning point gives the maximum or minimum value of the quadratic, not generally its roots. Roots are located where the parabola crosses the x-axis.
- Where the parabola crosses its axis of symmetry.
  - _why:_ The axis of symmetry passes through the turning point and may lie between two roots, but it does not locate them. Roots occur at the x-axis intersections.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** Before identifying the coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$, the equation must be rearranged so that one side equals [ ___ ].

**Back:** Before identifying the coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$, the equation must be rearranged so that one side equals 0.

**Choices:**

- 0 ✔
  - _why:_ Standard quadratic form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$, so all terms must be collected on one side and the other side set equal to zero.
- 1
  - _why:_ Setting one side equal to one does not produce standard quadratic form. The equation must be rearranged so that one side equals zero.
- $$x$
  - _why:_ The variable $x$ is the unknown being solved for, not the required value of one side. Standard form sets one side equal to zero.
- $$c$
  - _why:_ The symbol $c$ is the constant coefficient within the quadratic expression. Standard form requires the entire expression to be set equal to zero.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Identify the coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$ for the equation $4x^2 = 7x + 2$.

**Back:** $a = 4$$b = -7$$c = -2$

**Choices:**

- $a = 4$$b = -7$$c = -2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging gives $4x^2-7x-2=0$. Comparing this with $ax^2+bx+c=0$ gives coefficients $a=4$, $b=-7$, and $c=-2$.
- $a = 4$$b = 7$$c = 2$
  - _why:_ These signs come from reading the unrearranged equation directly. Moving $7x$ and $2$ to the left changes both signs, giving $b=-7$ and $c=-2$.
- $a = 4$$b = -7$$c = 2$
  - _why:_ Only the $7x$ term has been given the sign change caused by moving it left. The constant also moves left, so $c=-2$, not $2$.
- $a = 4$$b = 7$$c = -2$
  - _why:_ Only the constant has been given the sign change caused by moving it left. The $7x$ term also moves left, so $b=-7$, not $7$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** State the quadratic formula used to solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

**Back:** $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

**Choices:**

- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ ✔
  - _why:_ This formula gives the solutions of any quadratic in standard form. The two signs before the square root account for the two possible roots.
- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2}$
  - _why:_ Omitting $a$ from the denominator changes the scale of both solutions. The complete denominator in the quadratic formula is $2a$, not merely $2$.
- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 + 4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ The discriminant uses subtraction because completing the square produces $b^2-4ac$. Replacing it with $b^2+4ac$ generally gives different, incorrect roots.
- $$x = \frac{b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ The linear coefficient must be negated before applying the plus-or-minus operation. Using $b$ instead of $-b$ changes both calculated roots.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the quadratic formula, the symbol $\pm$ indicates that there are usually [ ___ ] distinct solutions.

**Back:** In the quadratic formula, the symbol $\pm$ indicates that there are usually two distinct solutions.

**Choices:**

- two ✔
  - _why:_ The plus sign gives one value and the minus sign gives another. When the discriminant is positive, these produce two distinct real solutions.
- one
  - _why:_ One solution occurs only when the discriminant is zero, causing the plus and minus calculations to coincide. Usually, $b1$ produces two distinct solutions.
- three
  - _why:_ The symbol provides only two calculations: one using addition and one using subtraction. It therefore usually produces two distinct solutions, not three.
- four
  - _why:_ The plus-or-minus symbol represents two sign choices, not every combination of four operations. Consequently, a quadratic usually has two distinct solutions.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the discriminant?

**Back:** The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ found under the square root in the quadratic formula.

**Choices:**

- The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ found under the square root in the quadratic formula. ✔
  - _why:_ The discriminant is the quantity beneath the square root. Its sign determines whether a quadratic has two real roots, one repeated root, or no real roots.
- The expression $b^2 + 4ac$ found under the square root in the quadratic formula.
  - _why:_ The sign before $4ac$ has been reversed. The discriminant is $b^2-4ac$, and its value determines the number and type of roots.
- The expression $2a$ found under the fraction line in the quadratic formula.
  - _why:_ The denominator scales the result but does not determine the nature of the roots. The discriminant is $b^2-4ac$, found beneath the square root.
- The expression $-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}$ found above the fraction line in the quadratic formula.
  - _why:_ This is the whole numerator rather than the discriminant alone. The discriminant is specifically $b^2-4ac$, the expression inside the square root.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is [ ___ ], there are two real roots.

**Back:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is positive, there are two real roots.

**Choices:**

- positive ✔
  - _why:_ A positive discriminant has a positive square root, so the plus and minus cases in the quadratic formula produce two distinct real roots.
- zero
  - _why:_ A zero discriminant makes the square-root term vanish, producing one repeated root. Two distinct real roots occur when the discriminant is positive.
- negative
  - _why:_ A negative discriminant has no real square root, so it produces no real roots. Two real roots require a positive discriminant.
- non-positive
  - _why:_ A non-positive discriminant includes both zero and negative values, which give one repeated root or no real roots. It must be positive for two real roots.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is [ ___ ], there is one repeated root.

**Back:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is zero, there is one repeated root.

**Choices:**

- zero ✔
  - _why:_ When the discriminant equals zero, its square root is zero, so the plus and minus versions of the quadratic formula give the same repeated root.
- positive
  - _why:_ A positive discriminant has a non-zero real square root, so the quadratic formula gives two distinct real roots. One repeated root occurs when it is zero.
- negative
  - _why:_ A negative discriminant has no real square root, so there are no real roots. A repeated real root requires the discriminant to equal zero.
- non-zero
  - _why:_ A non-zero discriminant may be positive or negative, producing either two real roots or no real roots. Exactly zero produces one repeated root.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is [ ___ ], there are no real roots.

**Back:** If the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is negative, there are no real roots.

**Choices:**

- negative ✔
  - _why:_ A negative discriminant has no real square root, so the quadratic formula cannot produce real values of $x$. Therefore, the equation has no real roots.
- positive
  - _why:_ A positive discriminant has a positive real square root, giving two distinct real roots. No real roots occur when the discriminant is negative.
- zero
  - _why:_ A zero discriminant makes the square-root term zero and gives one repeated real root. No real roots occur only when the discriminant is negative.
- non-negative
  - _why:_ A non-negative discriminant includes zero and positive values, which produce one or two real roots. No real roots require a negative discriminant.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you use brackets for the numerator when typing the quadratic formula into a calculator?

**Back:** To ensure the entire numerator is divided by $2a$, rather than just the square root term.

**Choices:**

- To ensure the entire numerator is divided by $2a$, rather than just the square root term. ✔
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula divides the complete expression $-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}$ by $2a$. Brackets ensure the calculator applies the division to both numerator terms.
- To ensure only $-b$ is divided by $2a$, rather than the square root term.
  - _why:_ Dividing only $-b$ by $2a$ changes the quadratic formula and gives incorrect roots. Brackets make both $-b$ and the square-root term part of the numerator.
- To ensure the discriminant is divided by $2a$ before taking its square root.
  - _why:_ The discriminant must be evaluated inside the square root before the numerator is divided. Brackets around the numerator do not divide the discriminant by $2a$ first.
- To ensure just the square root term is divided by $2a$, rather than the entire numerator.
  - _why:_ The square-root term is not the only part divided by $2a$. The common calculator slip is omitting brackets and leaving $-b$ outside the division.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Contrast rounding to decimal places vs significant figures.

**Back:** Decimal places: count digits after the decimal point.Significant figures: count starting from the first non-zero digit.

**Choices:**

- Decimal places: count digits after the decimal point.Significant figures: count starting from the first non-zero digit. ✔
  - _why:_ Decimal places are determined by position relative to the decimal point, whereas significant figures begin at the first digit that contributes to the number’s value.
- Decimal places: count starting from the first non-zero digit.Significant figures: count digits after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two definitions. Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, while significant figures start at the first non-zero digit.
- Decimal places: count digits from the first digit shown.Significant figures: count digits after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Decimal places are not counted from the first digit of the whole number. They count digits after the decimal point, while significant figures start at the first non-zero digit.
- Decimal places: count all non-zero digits shown.Significant figures: count starting after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Significant figures do not begin automatically after the decimal point, and leading zeros are not significant. Decimal places count after the point; significant figures begin at the first non-zero digit.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a surd?

**Back:** An irrational root left in exact form, such as $\sqrt{2}$.

**Choices:**

- An irrational root left in exact form, such as $\sqrt{2}$. ✔
  - _why:_ A surd represents an irrational root exactly rather than replacing it with a rounded decimal. For example, $\sqrt{2}$ is exact and cannot be written as a rational number.
- A terminating decimal written in approximate form, such as $1.414$.
  - _why:_ A terminating decimal is rational, whereas a surd is an irrational root kept in exact form. For example, $\sqrt{2}$ is a surd rather than a decimal approximation.
- A rational root simplified to an integer, such as $\sqrt{4}$.
  - _why:_ Roots that simplify to integers are not surds because they are rational. A surd is an irrational root left exactly, such as $\sqrt{2}$.
- An irrational root rounded to a decimal, such as $1.41$.
  - _why:_ A rounded irrational decimal is only an approximation, not a surd. The surd keeps the irrational root in exact form, such as $\sqrt{2}$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify the surd $\sqrt{72}$ by extracting the largest square factor.

**Back:** $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The largest square factor of $72$ is $36$. Since $\sqrt{36}=6$, separating the factors gives $\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36}\sqrt{2}=6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 36\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ This treats $\sqrt{36}$ as $36$ instead of $6$. Extracting the square factor correctly gives $\sqrt{36 \times 2}=6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{9 \times 8} = 3\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ Although $72=9\times8$, simplifying only $\sqrt{9}$ and replacing $\sqrt{8}$ by $\sqrt{2}$ loses a factor of $2$. The result is $6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{4 \times 18} = 4\sqrt{18}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats $\sqrt{4}$ as $4$ rather than $2$. Fully extracting square factors from $\sqrt{72}$ gives $6\sqrt{2}$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify the expression $x = \frac{-10 \pm 6\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

**Back:** $$x = -5 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$x = -5 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms in the numerator must be divided by $2$. Thus $-10\div2=-5$ and $6\sqrt{2}\div2=3\sqrt{2}$, giving the stated expression.
- $$x = -5 \pm 6\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ This divides $-10$ by $2$ but leaves the surd term unchanged. The entire numerator is divided, so $6\sqrt{2}\div2=3\sqrt{2}$.
- $$x = -10 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ This divides the surd term by $2$ but leaves $-10$ unchanged. Both numerator terms must be divided, giving $-5 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$.
- $$x = -2 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ The constant term comes from $-10\div2$, which equals $-5$, not $-2$. Dividing the whole numerator gives $x=-5\pm3\sqrt{2}$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** In a geometry problem (e.g., finding area), why might you reject a mathematically correct solution from the formula?

**Back:** Physical lengths must be positive; a solution is rejected if it results in a negative side length.

**Choices:**

- Physical lengths must be positive; a solution is rejected if it results in a negative side length. ✔
  - _why:_ Side lengths represent physical distances, so they cannot be negative. An algebraically valid root must therefore be rejected when it gives a negative length.
- Physical lengths must be whole numbers; a solution is rejected if it results in a decimal side length.
  - _why:_ Lengths can be decimals, fractions or irrational values, so being non-integer does not invalidate a solution. The root should instead be rejected if it produces a negative length.
- Physical lengths must be less than the area; a solution is rejected if its value is greater than the area.
  - _why:_ Length and area have different units, so their numerical values cannot be compared this way. A solution is rejected when it gives a negative physical length.
- Physical lengths must be equal; a solution is rejected if it results in two different side lengths.
  - _why:_ Different side lengths are valid for shapes such as rectangles, so equality is not a general requirement. The relevant restriction is that every physical length must be positive.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** How is Pythagoras' Theorem used to form a quadratic equation?

**Back:** By substituting algebraic side lengths into $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ and expanding the brackets.

**Choices:**

- By substituting algebraic side lengths into $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ and expanding the brackets. ✔
  - _why:_ For a right-angled triangle, substituting expressions for the side lengths into Pythagoras' Theorem and expanding their squares commonly produces a quadratic equation.
- By substituting algebraic side lengths into $a + b = c$ and expanding the brackets.
  - _why:_ This omits the squares and incorrectly treats the hypotenuse as the sum of the shorter sides. Pythagoras' Theorem requires substitution into $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
- By substituting algebraic side lengths into $2a + 2b = c$ and expanding the brackets.
  - _why:_ The expression $2a + 2b$ resembles a rectangle's perimeter and is not Pythagoras' Theorem. The side expressions must be substituted into $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
- By substituting algebraic side lengths into $ab = c^2$ and expanding the brackets.
  - _why:_ Multiplying two side lengths confuses an area calculation with Pythagoras' Theorem. The squares of the shorter sides must be added using $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** To avoid inaccuracies, you should not [ ___ ] the square root value until the final step of the calculation.

**Back:** To avoid inaccuracies, you should not round the square root value until the final step of the calculation.

**Choices:**

- round ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping the square root in exact form during intermediate steps prevents rounding errors from accumulating. It should be rounded only when the final answer is required.
- keep
  - _why:_ The square root should be kept in exact form during the calculation, not avoided. Only the final numerical answer should be rounded to the required accuracy.
- use
  - _why:_ An exact square root value can and should be used in subsequent steps. The operation that must be delayed until the final step is rounding.
- simplify
  - _why:_ Simplifying a square root exactly does not reduce accuracy and can make later work easier. It is rounding to a decimal that should wait until the final step.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step to solve a problem involving the area of a compound shape with algebraic sides?

**Back:** Write an equation for the total area (e.g., sum of individual rectangle areas) and set it equal to the given value.

**Choices:**

- Write an equation for the total area (e.g., sum of individual rectangle areas) and set it equal to the given value. ✔
  - _why:_ Expressing each component's area algebraically and equating their total to the given area creates the equation that can then be rearranged into quadratic form.
- Write an equation for the total perimeter (e.g., sum of all exterior side lengths) and set it equal to the given value.
  - _why:_ This confuses area with perimeter and would model a different measurement. The component areas must be added and their total set equal to the given area.
- Multiply all the side lengths of the compound shape and set the product equal to the given value.
  - _why:_ Multiplying every side length does not give the area of a compound shape. Divide it into simpler shapes, add their area expressions and equate the total to the given area.
- Apply the quadratic formula to the algebraic side lengths before forming an equation for the total area.
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula cannot be used until a quadratic equation has been formed. First create an area equation from the individual parts and the given total.
