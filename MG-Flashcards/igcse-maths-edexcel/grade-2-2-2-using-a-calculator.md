## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** When asked for all figures on the calculator display, what should you write?

**Back:** Every digit shown on your own display, not a rounded answer.

**Choices:**

- Every digit shown on your own display, not a rounded answer. ✔
  - _why:_ Calculator models may display different numbers of digits, so you should copy every digit visible on your own screen rather than rounding the value.
- Every digit shown before the decimal point, not the decimal digits.
  - _why:_ Ignoring digits after the decimal point removes important precision. The instruction means copying every digit shown on your own calculator display without rounding.
- The answer rounded to three significant figures, not every displayed digit.
  - _why:_ Three significant figures is only appropriate when specifically requested. For all display figures, copy every digit visible on your own calculator screen.
- The answer rounded to two decimal places, not every displayed digit.
  - _why:_ Rounding to two decimal places changes the displayed value and loses digits. You must instead write every digit shown on your own calculator display.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** If a calculator shows an exact fraction or surd but a decimal display is needed, what key can you use?

**Back:** Use the decimal toggle: S⇔D, F⇔D or ≈.

**Choices:**

- Use the decimal toggle: S⇔D, F⇔D or ≈. ✔
  - _why:_ These calculator keys switch an exact fraction or surd into its decimal form, although the precise label varies between calculator models.
- Use the memory key: M+, M− or MR.
  - _why:_ Memory keys store, subtract or recall calculator values; they do not change an exact form into a decimal. Use S⇔D, F⇔D or ≈ instead.
- Use the editing key: DEL, AC or C.
  - _why:_ These keys delete entries or clear calculations rather than changing the answer format. Use the S⇔D, F⇔D or ≈ decimal toggle.
- Use the operation key: ×, ÷ or =.
  - _why:_ Operation and equals keys perform or complete calculations, but they do not toggle the displayed form. Use S⇔D, F⇔D or ≈ for a decimal.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** Squaring and square rooting [ ___ ].

**Back:** Squaring and square rooting undo each other.

**Choices:**

- undo each other ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring a number and then taking its square root returns the original non-negative number, so the two operations undo each other in this context.
- always double numbers
  - _why:_ Squaring means multiplying a number by itself, not doubling it. Squaring and square rooting are inverse operations, so they undo each other.
- give identical answers
  - _why:_ A number’s square and square root are generally different values. The correct relationship is that squaring and square rooting undo each other.
- both halve numbers
  - _why:_ Neither operation generally means dividing by two. Squaring and square rooting are inverse operations, meaning that they undo each other.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What multiplication does $1.25^2$ represent?

**Back:** $1.25 \times 1.25$.

**Choices:**

- $1.25 \times 1.25$. ✔
  - _why:_ An exponent of 2 means multiplying the base by itself once, so $1.25^2$ represents $1.25 \times 1.25$.
- $1.25 \times 2$.
  - _why:_ This treats squaring as doubling, which is a common misconception. An exponent of 2 means multiplying $1.25$ by itself: $1.25 \times 1.25$.
- $1.25 \times 1.2$.
  - _why:_ The second factor must be the complete base, not a shortened version of it. Therefore, $1.25^2$ represents $1.25 \times 1.25$.
- $1.25 \times 0.25$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses only the decimal part as the second factor. Squaring repeats the whole base, giving $1.25 \times 1.25$.

**Back:** 1.3

- 1.3 ✔
  - _why:_ The square root reverses squaring. Since $1.3^2=1.69$ and $1.3$ is positive, the principal square root is $\sqrt{1.69}=1.3$.
- 1.69
  - _why:_ This repeats the number under the square root instead of reversing the square. Since $1.3^2=1.69$, the principal square root is $1.3$.
- 0.13
  - _why:_ This introduces an unnecessary place-value shift. Square rooting reverses squaring, so $1.3^2=1.69$ directly gives $\sqrt{1.69}=1.3$.
- 13
  - _why:_ This incorrectly moves the decimal point one place to the right. Because $1.3 \times 1.3=1.69$, the square root is $1.3$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** In BIDMAS, [ ___ ] are done first; [ ___ ] are powers.

**Back:** In BIDMAS, Brackets are done first; indices are powers.

**Choices:**

- In BIDMAS, Brackets are done first; indices are powers. ✔
  - _why:_ BIDMAS begins with brackets, followed by indices. Indices are powers such as squares, cubes and other exponents.
- In BIDMAS, Indices are done first; brackets are powers.
  - _why:_ This reverses the first two BIDMAS concepts and misdefines powers. Brackets are evaluated first, while indices are powers such as squares and cubes.
- In BIDMAS, Multiplications are done first; indices are powers.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is not completed before brackets and indices. Brackets are done first in BIDMAS, while indices correctly refers to powers.
- In BIDMAS, Brackets are done first; divisions are powers.
  - _why:_ Brackets are correctly placed first, but division is an arithmetic operation rather than a power. Indices are the powers in BIDMAS.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** In $(4.25 - 0.36)^2$, what does the power 2 apply to?

**Back:** The whole bracket.

**Choices:**

- The whole bracket. ✔
  - _why:_ The brackets make $4.25 - 0.36$ a single grouped expression, so the result of the entire subtraction is squared.
- Only $0.36$.
  - _why:_ This overlooks the grouping created by the brackets. The power 2 applies to the whole bracket, so $4.25 - 0.36$ must be evaluated before squaring.
- Only $4.25$.
  - _why:_ The exponent is written outside the closing bracket, not directly after $4.25$. It therefore applies to the whole bracket rather than only the first number.
- Only the subtraction sign.
  - _why:_ An operation sign cannot be raised to a power. The power 2 applies to the whole bracket, meaning the result of $4.25 - 0.36$ is squared.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a fraction, the top part is the [ ___ ]; the bottom part is the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a fraction, the top part is the numerator; the bottom part is the denominator.

**Choices:**

- In a fraction, the top part is the numerator; the bottom part is the denominator. ✔
  - _why:_ The numerator appears above the fraction line and is divided by the denominator, which appears below the fraction line.
- In a fraction, the top part is the denominator; the bottom part is the numerator.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two standard terms. The numerator is the top part of a fraction, while the denominator is the bottom part.
- In a fraction, the top part is the dividend; the bottom part is the divisor.
  - _why:_ Dividend and divisor describe roles in a division calculation, not the named parts of a written fraction. Those parts are the numerator and denominator respectively.
- In a fraction, the top part is the numerator; the bottom part is the multiplier.
  - _why:_ The top term is correctly identified, but multiplier is not the name of the bottom part. The bottom part of a fraction is the denominator.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you not round partway through a calculator calculation?

**Back:** Early rounding can change the final answer.

**Choices:**

- Early rounding can change the final answer. ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding an intermediate value introduces an error that can be magnified by later operations. Keeping full calculator precision until the end produces the most accurate final answer.
- Early rounding always improves the final answer.
  - _why:_ Rounding does not automatically improve accuracy; it discards information and introduces error. Intermediate values should remain unrounded because early rounding can change the final answer.
- Calculators cannot store precise intermediate values.
  - _why:_ Calculators normally retain more digits internally than they display, so they can store precise intermediate results. Early rounding is avoided because it can alter the final answer.
- Only the final operation affects the answer.
  - _why:_ Every operation can affect accuracy, particularly if rounded intermediate values are reused. Early rounding may accumulate or magnify errors and therefore change the final answer.
