# The Cosine Rule Flashcards

## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** The included angle is the angle situated between [ ___ ].

**Back:** The included angle is the angle situated between two known sides.

**Choices:**

- two known sides ✔
  - _why:_ An included angle is formed by the two sides whose lengths are known, so it lies directly between those two known sides.
- two known angles
  - _why:_ This confuses the given angles with the lines forming an angle. The included angle lies between two known sides, which form its arms.
- a known side and an angle
  - _why:_ An angle is formed between two lines or sides, not between a side and another angle. The included angle is between two known sides.
- all three known sides
  - _why:_ Only two sides meet at any one vertex of a triangle, so an angle cannot lie between all three. It lies between two known sides.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** State the conditions required to use the Cosine Rule to find a missing side.

**Back:** Two sides and the included angle (SAS).

**Choices:**

- Two sides and the included angle (SAS). ✔
  - _why:_ The side form of the Cosine Rule requires two known sides and the angle between them, which is the side-angle-side or SAS arrangement.
- Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA).
  - _why:_ SSA gives an angle that is not between the two known sides, so the side form cannot be applied directly. The required information is SAS.
- One side and two angles (ASA).
  - _why:_ ASA is normally handled by finding the third angle and applying the Sine Rule. The Cosine Rule for a missing side directly requires SAS.
- Knowledge of all three sides (SSS).
  - _why:_ SSS is used with the rearranged Cosine Rule to calculate an angle, not a missing side. Finding a side directly requires SAS.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** State the Cosine Rule formula for finding a side $c$.

**Back:** $$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$

**Choices:**

- $$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The square of the side opposite $C$ equals the sum of the other squared sides minus twice their product multiplied by $\cos C$.
- $$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos C$$
  - _why:_ The cosine term has been added instead of subtracted. The correct formula is $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$.
- $$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\sin C$$
  - _why:_ This substitutes sine for cosine, perhaps confusing the rule with the triangle area formula. The final factor must be $\cos C$, not $\sin C$.
- $$$c = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$
  - _why:_ The left-hand side has not been squared. The rule calculates $c^2$ first, so the correct left-hand side is $c^2$.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** When finding a side length using the Cosine Rule, you must remember to [ ___ ] the result of $c^2$.

**Back:** When finding a side length using the Cosine Rule, you must remember to square root the result of $c^2$.

**Choices:**

- square root ✔
  - _why:_ The Cosine Rule initially produces $c^2$. Taking the positive square root gives $c$, the required side length.
- square
  - _why:_ Squaring again would produce $c^4$ rather than the side length. Since the calculated value is $c^2$, it must be square rooted.
- divide by two
  - _why:_ Dividing by two does not reverse the operation of squaring. To obtain $c$ from $c^2$, take the positive square root.
- cube root
  - _why:_ A cube root reverses cubing, but the Cosine Rule gives a squared side length. The required inverse operation is the square root.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the Cosine Rule simplify to if the angle $C = 90^\circ$?

**Back:** Pythagoras' Theorem ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$).

**Choices:**

- Pythagoras' Theorem ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ Since $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, the term $-2ab\cos C$ becomes zero, leaving $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, Pythagoras' Theorem.
- Sine Rule ($\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$).
  - _why:_ The Sine Rule relates sides to the sines of opposite angles and is not obtained here. Setting $\cos 90^\circ=0$ produces Pythagoras' Theorem.
- Area formula ($A = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$).
  - _why:_ This calculates a triangle's area and comes from sine, not from simplifying the Cosine Rule. At $90^\circ$, the rule becomes Pythagoras' Theorem.
- Pythagoras' Theorem ($c^2 = a^2 - b^2$).
  - _why:_ The squared shorter sides are added when $c$ is opposite the right angle. Since the cosine term vanishes, the result is $c^2=a^2+b^2$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** State the conditions required to use the Cosine Rule to find a missing angle.

**Back:** Knowledge of all three sides (SSS).

**Choices:**

- Knowledge of all three sides (SSS). ✔
  - _why:_ The rearranged Cosine Rule calculates an angle from the lengths of the opposite side and both adjacent sides, so all three sides must be known.
- Knowledge of two sides and the included angle (SAS).
  - _why:_ SAS is used to calculate the third side with the Cosine Rule, and the included angle is already known. Finding a missing angle directly requires SSS.
- Knowledge of two angles and one side (AAS).
  - _why:_ With AAS, the third angle is found using the angle sum and sides are found using the Sine Rule. The Cosine Rule angle form requires SSS.
- Knowledge of two sides and a non-included angle (SSA).
  - _why:_ SSA does not provide every side needed by the rearranged Cosine Rule and may create an ambiguous case. The required information is all three sides, SSS.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** State the rearranged Cosine Rule formula for finding the angle $C$.

**Back:** $$$\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging $c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$ isolates the cosine term, giving $\cos C=(a^2+b^2-c^2)/(2ab)$.
- $$$\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}{2ab}$$
  - _why:_ The $c^2$ term has the wrong sign after rearrangement. Moving it to the numerator gives $a^2+b^2-c^2$, not a sum of three squares.
- $$$\cos C = \frac{c^2 - a^2 - b^2}{2ab}$$
  - _why:_ This numerator is the negative of the required expression, so it would reverse the sign of the cosine. The numerator must be $a^2+b^2-c^2$.
- $$$\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{ab}$$
  - _why:_ The factor of $2$ has been lost when dividing by the coefficient of $\cos C$. The denominator must remain $2ab$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the formula $\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$, side $c$ is the side [ ___ ] angle $C$.

**Back:** In the formula $\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$, side $c$ is the side opposite angle $C$.

**Choices:**

- opposite ✔
  - _why:_ Standard triangle notation pairs each angle with the side directly across from it. Therefore side $c$ is opposite angle $C$.
- adjacent to
  - _why:_ The sides adjacent to angle $C$ are $a$ and $b$, which meet to form that angle. Side $c$ lies opposite angle $C$.
- forming
  - _why:_ Angle $C$ is formed by sides $a$ and $b$, not by side $c$. In standard notation, side $c$ is opposite angle $C$.
- containing
  - _why:_ A side does not contain an angle; the two sides meeting at a vertex form it. Side $c$ is the side opposite angle $C$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the actual angle $C$ once you have the value of $\cos C$?

**Back:** Use the inverse cosine function ($\cos^{-1}$).

**Choices:**

- Use the inverse cosine function ($\cos^{-1}$). ✔
  - _why:_ The inverse cosine reverses the cosine operation. Enter the calculated value of $\cos C$ and apply $\cos^{-1}$ to obtain angle $C$ in degrees.
- Use the inverse sine function ($\sin^{-1}$).
  - _why:_ Inverse sine is used when a sine value is known, not a cosine value. Apply the inverse cosine function, $\cos^{-1}$, to the calculated value of $\cos C$.
- Use the inverse tangent function ($\tan^{-1}$).
  - _why:_ Inverse tangent recovers an angle from its tangent, so using it on a cosine value gives the wrong angle. The required function is inverse cosine, $\cos^{-1}$.
- Use the cosine function ($\cos$).
  - _why:_ Applying cosine again does not undo the original cosine operation. To recover $C$ from $\cos C$, use the inverse cosine function, $\cos^{-1}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What should you check on your calculator before performing trigonometry calculations?

**Back:** Ensure it is in degrees mode.

**Choices:**

- Ensure it is in degrees mode. ✔
  - _why:_ IGCSE triangle angles are normally expressed in degrees, so the calculator must use degree mode for its trigonometric functions to produce the expected values.
- Ensure it is in radians mode.
  - _why:_ Radians measure angles using multiples of $\pi$, so trigonometric results will differ from degree-based IGCSE questions. The calculator should be set to degrees mode.
- Ensure it is in gradians mode.
  - _why:_ Gradians divide a full turn into 400 units and are not the angle units used in these questions. Set the calculator to degrees mode instead.
- Ensure it is in standard form mode.
  - _why:_ Standard form controls how numbers are displayed, not how angles are interpreted by trigonometric functions. The necessary check is that the calculator is in degrees mode.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** If an included angle is [ ___ ] (greater than $90^\circ$), the opposite side will usually be the longest side.

**Back:** If an included angle is obtuse (greater than $90^\circ$), the opposite side will usually be the longest side.

**Choices:**

- obtuse ✔
  - _why:_ An obtuse angle is greater than $90^\circ$ but less than $180^\circ$. In a triangle, the side opposite the largest angle is the longest side.
- acute
  - _why:_ An acute angle is less than $90^\circ$, not greater than it. The required term is obtuse, and the side opposite an obtuse angle is the triangle’s longest side.
- right
  - _why:_ A right angle is exactly $90^\circ$, whereas the sentence specifies an angle greater than $90^\circ$. Such an angle is described as obtuse.
- reflex
  - _why:_ A reflex angle exceeds $180^\circ$ and cannot be an interior angle of a triangle. An angle between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ is obtuse.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Why might you reject a solution like $x = -4$ when solving an algebraic Cosine Rule problem?

**Back:** Lengths cannot be negative.

**Choices:**

- Lengths cannot be negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A side length represents a physical distance, so it must be zero or positive. Therefore, a negative algebraic root cannot represent the required triangle side.
- Lengths cannot be irrational.
  - _why:_ Lengths may be irrational, such as $\sqrt{2}$, so irrationality is not a reason to reject a solution. The value $-4$ is rejected because lengths cannot be negative.
- Lengths cannot be non-integers.
  - _why:_ Lengths can be decimals, so being non-integer does not invalidate a side length. The solution $-4$ must instead be rejected because a length cannot be negative.
- Lengths cannot be the largest side.
  - _why:_ Lengths can be greater than the other individual sides, provided the triangle inequality is satisfied. The immediate problem with $-4$ is that lengths cannot be negative.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the area of a triangle given two sides and the included angle.

**Back:** $$$Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$$

**Choices:**

- $$$Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The area equals half the product of two side lengths and the sine of their included angle. Here, $C$ must be the angle between sides $a$ and $b$.
- $$$Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\cos C$$
  - _why:_ Using cosine confuses the area formula with the Cosine Rule. For two sides and their included angle, the area is $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$.
- $$$Area = ab\sin C$$
  - _why:_ Omitting the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ doubles the triangle’s area, effectively treating it like a parallelogram. The correct formula is $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$.
- $$$Area = \frac{1}{2}a^2b^2\sin C$$
  - _why:_ Squaring both side lengths gives dimensions of length to the fourth power, not area. The correct formula multiplies the sides once: $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the steps to find the perimeter of a triangle if given two sides, the area, and the fact that the included angle is acute.

**Back:** Use $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find the included angle.Use the Cosine Rule to find the third side.Sum all three sides.

**Choices:**

- Use $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find the included angle.Use the Cosine Rule to find the third side.Sum all three sides. ✔
  - _why:_ The area formula first gives the sine of the included angle; the acute condition selects the acute solution. The Cosine Rule then finds the missing side before all sides are added.
- Use $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find the third side.Use the Cosine Rule to find the included angle.Sum all three sides.
  - _why:_ The area formula with two known sides determines the included angle, not the third side directly. Find that angle first, use the Cosine Rule for the third side, then add all sides.
- Use $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find the included angle.Use the Sine Rule to find the third side.Sum all three sides.
  - _why:_ The Sine Rule cannot generally find the third side when only two sides and their included angle are available. After finding the angle from area, use the Cosine Rule, then sum the sides.
- Use $Area = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find the included angle.Use the Cosine Rule to find the third side.Sum the area and two sides.
  - _why:_ Adding the area to two lengths mixes incompatible units and does not produce a perimeter. Find the included angle, use the Cosine Rule for the third side, and add the three side lengths.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** Using inverse sine to find an angle can be [ ___ ] because there may be both acute and obtuse possibilities.

**Back:** Using inverse sine to find an angle can be ambiguous because there may be both acute and obtuse possibilities.

**Choices:**

- ambiguous ✔
  - _why:_ Sine has the same positive value for supplementary angles, since $\sin C = \sin(180^\circ-C)$. Consequently, inverse sine alone may not distinguish an acute angle from an obtuse one.
- inaccurate
  - _why:_ The issue is not that inverse sine is approximate; calculators can evaluate it accurately. It is ambiguous because supplementary acute and obtuse angles can have the same sine value.
- undefined
  - _why:_ Inverse sine is defined for inputs from $-1$ to $1$, including those arising here. The difficulty is ambiguity, because two supplementary angles can share the same sine.
- inefficient
  - _why:_ Inverse sine is commonly used and is not inherently inefficient. It can be ambiguous because an acute angle and its obtuse supplement have equal sine values.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you handle intermediate values in a multi-step Cosine Rule calculation to ensure accuracy?

**Back:** Keep full calculator values; only round at the very end.

**Choices:**

- Keep full calculator values; only round at the very end. ✔
  - _why:_ Retaining full calculator precision prevents rounding errors from accumulating across several steps. Round only the final requested measurement to the stated degree of accuracy.
- Keep rounded calculator values; round after every calculation.
  - _why:_ Rounding every intermediate result can accumulate errors and shift the final answer. Keep full calculator values throughout the working and round only the final result.
- Keep whole-number values; ignore all decimal places.
  - _why:_ Using only whole numbers discards substantial precision and can make the final side or angle inaccurate. Retain full calculator values and round only at the end.
- Keep estimated calculator values; adjust them at every step.
  - _why:_ Estimating intermediate values is useful for checking reasonableness but not for obtaining an accurate result. Use full calculator values during the calculation and round only the final answer.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the formula $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$, the sides $a$ and $b$ are the sides that [ ___ ] the angle $C$.

**Back:** In the formula $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$, the sides $a$ and $b$ are the sides that touch/enclose the angle $C$.

**Choices:**

- touch/enclose ✔
  - _why:_ In this labelling convention, angle $C$ lies between sides $a$ and $b$, so they are the two enclosing sides. The remaining side $c$ is opposite angle $C$.
- are opposite to
  - _why:_ This confuses the enclosing sides with the opposite side. Only $c$ is opposite angle $C$; sides $a$ and $b$ touch/enclose angle $C$.
- do not meet at
  - _why:_ Sides $a$ and $b$ meet at the vertex of angle $C$, rather than avoiding it. They are the sides that touch/enclose angle $C$.
- are perpendicular at
  - _why:_ Perpendicular sides would make $C$ a right angle, which is not required by the Cosine Rule. Sides $a$ and $b$ simply touch/enclose angle $C$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Contrast when to use the Sine Rule versus the Cosine Rule.

**Back:** Sine Rule: matching opposite pair (side and angle).Cosine Rule: SAS or SSS.

**Choices:**

- Sine Rule: matching opposite pair (side and angle).Cosine Rule: SAS or SSS. ✔
  - _why:_ The Sine Rule requires a known side and its opposite angle. The Cosine Rule is suited to two sides with the included angle, or all three sides.
- Sine Rule: SAS or SSS.Cosine Rule: matching opposite pair (side and angle).
  - _why:_ This reverses the two rules. A matching opposite side-angle pair indicates the Sine Rule, while SAS or SSS data indicates the Cosine Rule.
- Sine Rule: matching adjacent pair (side and angle).Cosine Rule: ASA or AAS.
  - _why:_ The Sine Rule depends on an opposite, not adjacent, side-angle pair. The Cosine Rule is selected for SAS or SSS, rather than ASA or AAS.
- Sine Rule: right-angled triangles only.Cosine Rule: non-right-angled triangles only.
  - _why:_ Neither rule is restricted in this way. Use the Sine Rule for a matching opposite pair and the Cosine Rule for SAS or SSS data.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** The Cosine Rule works for [ ___ ], not just right-angled ones.

**Back:** The Cosine Rule works for any triangle, not just right-angled ones.

**Choices:**

- any triangle ✔
  - _why:_ The Cosine Rule applies to acute, obtuse and right-angled triangles. For a right angle, $ccos 90^ccirc=0$, so the rule reduces to Pythagoras’ theorem.
- acute triangles
  - _why:_ Restricting the rule to acute triangles overlooks obtuse and right-angled cases. The Cosine Rule is valid for any triangle when the sides and angles are labelled consistently.
- obtuse triangles
  - _why:_ A negative cosine makes the rule useful for obtuse triangles, but it is not limited to them. The Cosine Rule works for any triangle.
- any non-right triangle
  - _why:_ This wrongly excludes right-angled triangles. The Cosine Rule works for any triangle and becomes Pythagoras’ theorem when the included angle is $90^ccirc$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Explain what 3 significant figures means for a final answer.

**Back:** The result is rounded to the first three non-zero digits.

**Choices:**

- The result is rounded to the first three non-zero digits. ✔
  - _why:_ Significant figures are counted beginning with the first non-zero digit. For three significant figures, retain three significant digits and use the following digit to round.
- The result is rounded to the first three decimal digits.
  - _why:_ This confuses significant figures with decimal places, which count digits after the decimal point. Three significant figures are counted from the first non-zero digit.
- The result is truncated after the first three non-zero digits.
  - _why:_ Truncation simply removes later digits, whereas the next digit must be checked to round correctly. The result should be rounded to the first three non-zero digits.
- The result is rounded to the first three digits, including leading zeros.
  - _why:_ Leading zeros only locate the decimal point and are not significant. Counting begins at the first non-zero digit, and the result is then rounded to three significant figures.
