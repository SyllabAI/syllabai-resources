## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard algebraic representation of an even integer?

**Back:** $$2n$ (where $n$ is an integer)

**Choices:**

- $$2n$ (where $n$ is an integer) ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying any integer $n$ by 2 produces an integer divisible by 2, so this expression represents every even integer.
- $$2n+1$ (where $n$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ Adding 1 to twice an integer always produces an odd integer. An even integer is represented by $2n$, where $n$ is an integer.
- $$\frac{n}{2}$ (where $n$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ Dividing an integer by 2 may produce a non-integer and does not guarantee an even result. Every even integer has the form $2n$.
- $$n+2$ (where $n$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ Adding 2 preserves whether $n$ is odd or even, so the result is not necessarily even. Multiplying an integer by 2 gives the required form $2n$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the two common algebraic forms for an odd integer?

**Back:** $$2n+1$ or $2n-1$

**Choices:**

- $$2n+1$ or $2n-1$ ✔
  - _why:_ Twice an integer is even, so adding or subtracting 1 makes it odd. These forms can represent every positive or negative odd integer.
- $$2n+2$ or $2n-2$
  - _why:_ Both expressions are divisible by 2 and therefore represent even integers. Odd integers are one more or one less than an even integer: $2n+1$ or $2n-1$.
- $$n+1$ or $n-1$
  - _why:_ The parity of these expressions depends on the chosen value of $n$, so they are not always odd. Guaranteed odd integers have the forms $2n+1$ or $2n-1$.
- $$3n+1$ or $3n-1$
  - _why:_ Multiples of 3 shifted by 1 can be odd or even depending on $n$. Using twice an integer and shifting by 1 guarantees an odd result.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What algebraic expressions represent three consecutive integers?

**Back:** $$n$, $n+1$, $n+2$

**Choices:**

- $$n$, $n+1$, $n+2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Consecutive integers differ by 1. Starting with an arbitrary integer $n$, the next two integers are therefore $n+1$ and $n+2$.
- $$n$, $n+2$, $n+4$
  - _why:_ These values increase by 2, so they represent three integers of the same parity rather than consecutive integers. Consecutive integers must increase by 1 each time.
- $$n-1$, $n+1$, $n+3$
  - _why:_ Although the expressions are arranged symmetrically around $n$, each adjacent pair differs by 2. The correct sequence is $n$, $n+1$, $n+2$.
- $$2n$, $2n+2$, $2n+4$
  - _why:_ These expressions represent consecutive even integers because their values differ by 2. Three consecutive integers instead have the form $n$, $n+1$, $n+2$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What algebraic expressions represent two consecutive even integers?

**Back:** $$2n$ and $2n+2$

**Choices:**

- $$2n$ and $2n+2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Every even integer can be written as $2n$, and the next even integer is 2 greater. Thus consecutive even integers differ by 2.
- $$2n$ and $2n+4$
  - _why:_ The expressions differ by 4, so there is another even integer, $2n+2$, between them. Consecutive even integers must differ by 2.
- $$n$ and $n+2$
  - _why:_ The expressions differ by 2 but are not guaranteed to be even because $n$ could be odd. Starting with $2n$ guarantees both integers are even.
- $$2n+1$ and $2n+3$
  - _why:_ Both expressions are odd because 1 or 3 is added to an even number. Consecutive even integers are represented by $2n$ and $2n+2$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What algebraic expressions represent two consecutive odd integers?

**Back:** $$2n+1$ and $2n+3$

**Choices:**

- $$2n+1$ and $2n+3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Adding 1 to the even integer $2n$ produces an odd integer, and adding a further 2 gives the next odd integer.
- $$2n$ and $2n+2$
  - _why:_ Both expressions are even, not odd, because they are divisible by 2. Consecutive odd integers have the form $2n+1$ and $2n+3$.
- $$2n+1$ and $2n+5$
  - _why:_ These are both odd, but they differ by 4, leaving another odd integer between them. Consecutive odd integers must differ by 2.
- $$n+1$ and $n+3$
  - _why:_ The expressions differ by 2, but their parity depends on $n$ and is not guaranteed to be odd. Adding 1 to $2n$ guarantees odd values.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is testing specific numbers (e.g., $n=1, 2, 3$) not considered a mathematical proof?

**Back:** Testing only shows the statement is true for specific casesAlgebraic proof shows it is true for all allowed values

**Choices:**

- Testing only shows the statement is true for specific casesAlgebraic proof shows it is true for all allowed values ✔
  - _why:_ Checking finitely many examples cannot rule out a failure at another allowed value. Algebra treats an arbitrary value and therefore establishes the statement throughout its stated domain.
- Testing shows the statement is true for all casesAlgebraic proof shows it is true for specific allowed values
  - _why:_ The roles have been reversed: numerical testing establishes only the cases checked, while a valid algebraic argument establishes the result for all allowed values.
- Testing always finds a counterexampleAlgebraic proof relies on chosen numerical examples
  - _why:_ Testing can support a conjecture but does not always find a counterexample, even when one exists. Algebraic proof uses general reasoning rather than relying on selected examples.
- Testing is invalid for positive integersAlgebraic proof avoids using integer values entirely
  - _why:_ Positive integers are valid mathematical inputs, so their use is not the problem. The limitation is that testing finitely many inputs cannot establish a claim for every allowed value.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the goal when proving an expression is a multiple of 6?

**Back:** Factorise the expression into the form $6(\text{expression})$

**Choices:**

- Factorise the expression into the form $6(\text{expression})$ ✔
  - _why:_ Writing the result as 6 multiplied by an integer-valued expression demonstrates directly that it is divisible by 6 and hence is a multiple of 6.
- Factorise the expression into the form $3(\text{expression})$
  - _why:_ A factor of 3 proves only that the result is divisible by 3; it may not be even. A factor of 6 is needed to establish divisibility by 6.
- Rewrite the expression into the form $6+(\text{expression})$
  - _why:_ Adding 6 does not generally make a quantity divisible by 6, since the remaining expression may have any remainder. The result must be written as a product with factor 6.
- Rewrite the expression into the form $(\text{expression})^6$
  - _why:_ A sixth power is not necessarily divisible by 6; for example, $5^6$ is not. The expression must instead be shown as 6 times an integer.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you finish a proof that results in $12n$ to ensure you get the mark?

**Back:** State that because $n$ is an integer, $12n$ must be a multiple of 12.

**Choices:**

- State that because $n$ is an integer, $12n$ must be a multiple of 12. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $n$ is an integer, multiplying it by 12 produces an integer divisible by 12. This final statement explicitly links the algebraic result to the required conclusion.
- State that because $n$ is an integer, $12n$ must be a factor of 12.
  - _why:_ The factor relationship has been reversed: $12n$ is generally a multiple of 12, not a factor of it. Stating divisibility by 12 completes the proof.
- State that because $n$ is an integer, $12n$ must be a positive integer.
  - _why:_ An integer need not be positive, so that claim introduces an unjustified restriction. Positivity is irrelevant; integer $n$ is sufficient to conclude that $12n$ is a multiple of 12.
- State that because $12$ is an integer, $n$ must be a multiple of 12.
  - _why:_ The fact that 12 is an integer places no such divisibility condition on $n$. Instead, integer $n$ ensures the product $12n$ is a multiple of 12.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What algebraic form should you reach to prove a result is a square number?

**Back:** $$m^2$ or $(\text{expression})^2$

**Choices:**

- $$m^2$ or $(\text{expression})^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A square number can be written as an integer multiplied by itself, so the final algebraic form must be $m^2$ or the square of an expression.
- $$m^2+1$ or $(\text{expression})^2+1$
  - _why:_ Adding 1 means the result is one greater than a square, not necessarily a square itself. The required form is $m^2$ or $(\text{expression})^2$.
- $$2m^2$ or $2(\text{expression})^2$
  - _why:_ Multiplying a square by 2 does not usually produce another square number. The result must be written directly as $m^2$ or $(\text{expression})^2$.
- $$m^3$ or $(\text{expression})^3$
  - _why:_ A cube has power 3, whereas a square has power 2. To prove the result is a square number, reach the form $m^2$ or $(\text{expression})^2$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** To prove a result is "4 more than a multiple of 8", what target form is needed?

**Back:** $$8k+4$ (where $k$ is an integer)

**Choices:**

- $$8k+4$ (where $k$ is an integer) ✔
  - _why:_ Every multiple of 8 has the form $8k$. Adding 4 gives exactly the numbers that are 4 more than a multiple of 8.
- $$8k$ (where $k$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ This represents a multiple of 8 itself, without the required extra 4. The target form for 4 more than a multiple of 8 is $8k+4$.
- $$8k+2$ (where $k$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ Adding 2 produces numbers that are 2 more than multiples of 8, not 4 more. The correct target form is $8k+4$.
- $$4k+4$ (where $k$ is an integer)
  - _why:_ This describes numbers 4 more than a multiple of 4, which includes values that are not 4 more than multiples of 8. The required form is $8k+4$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** When proving a sum is a square number, why is factorising often better than expanding?

**Back:** It leads directly to the squared bracket form $(n+a)^2$ rather than a long quadratic.

**Choices:**

- It leads directly to the squared bracket form $(n+a)^2$ rather than a long quadratic. ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising can reveal two identical brackets, such as $(n+a)(n+a)$, which immediately becomes $(n+a)^2$ and proves the result is a square.
- It leads directly to the linear bracket form $(n+a)$ rather than a long quadratic.
  - _why:_ A single linear bracket is not enough to show that an expression is a square. Factorising is useful because it can reveal the repeated bracket $(n+a)^2$.
- It leads directly to the difference bracket form $(n-a)(n+a)$ rather than a long quadratic.
  - _why:_ The form $(n-a)(n+a)$ represents a difference of two squares, not necessarily a square number. The useful target is the repeated bracket $(n+a)^2$.
- It leads directly to the cubed bracket form $(n+a)^3$ rather than a long quadratic.
  - _why:_ A cubed bracket proves a cube, not a square. Factorising helps when it produces two identical factors, giving the squared form $(n+a)^2$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Expand the identity for $(a-b)^2$.

**Back:** $$a^2 - 2ab + b^2$

**Choices:**

- $$a^2 - 2ab + b^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring $(a-b)$ gives $a^2-ab-ab+b^2$, which combines to $a^2-2ab+b^2$.
- $$a^2 - ab + b^2$
  - _why:_ The two cross-products, $-ab$ and $-ab$, combine to $-2ab$, not $-ab$. Therefore the expansion is $a^2-2ab+b^2$.
- $$a^2 + 2ab + b^2$
  - _why:_ This is the expansion of $(a+b)^2$; the minus sign between $a$ and $b$ makes the middle term negative. The correct result is $a^2-2ab+b^2$.
- $$a^2 - 2ab - b^2$
  - _why:_ The final term comes from multiplying $-b$ by $-b$, which gives positive $b^2$. Thus the expansion is $a^2-2ab+b^2$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the difference of two squares identity used in proofs?

**Back:** $$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$

**Choices:**

- $$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying $(a-b)(a+b)$ cancels the two cross-terms, leaving $a^2-b^2$. This identity is useful for factorising a difference of two squares.
- $$a^2 + b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$
  - _why:_ The product $(a-b)(a+b)$ gives $a^2-b^2$ because the cross-terms cancel. It does not factorise the sum $a^2+b^2$ in this way.
- $$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring $(a-b)$ introduces the extra middle term $-2ab$. The difference of two squares instead factorises into two different brackets, $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$a^2 - b^2 = (a+b)^2$
  - _why:_ Expanding $(a+b)^2$ gives $a^2+2ab+b^2$, including a positive middle term. The correct factorisation is $(a-b)(a+b)$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Prove that $2n+5$ is always odd for any integer $n$.

**Back:** $2n+4$ is a multiple of 2 (even)$2n+5$ is one more than an even number

**Choices:**

- $2n+4$ is a multiple of 2 (even)$2n+5$ is one more than an even number ✔
  - _why:_ Since $2n+4$ is divisible by 2, it is even. Adding 1 gives $2n+5$, which is one more than an even number and therefore odd.
- $2n+4$ is a multiple of 2 (even)$2n+5$ is one less than an even number
  - _why:_ The expression $2n+5$ is one more than $2n+4$, not one less. Being one more than an even number proves it is odd.
- $2n+5$ is a multiple of 2 (even)$2n+5$ is one more than an even number
  - _why:_ The first statement is false: $2n+5$ is not divisible by 2. Instead, $2n+4$ is even, and $2n+5$ is one more than it, so the result is odd.
- $2n+4$ is a multiple of 2 (even)$2n+5$ is two more than an even number
  - _why:_ The difference between $2n+5$ and $2n+4$ is 1, not 2. The correct proof uses the fact that $2n+5$ is one more than an even number.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** To prove an expression is a multiple of 12, it must be written in the form $\text{[...]}$ where $k$ is an integer.

**Back:** To prove an expression is a multiple of 12, it must be written in the form $\boxed{12k}$ where $k$ is an integer.

**Choices:**

- $$12k$ ✔
  - _why:_ An expression is a multiple of 12 when it equals 12 multiplied by an integer. Writing it as $12k$ guarantees divisibility by 12.
- $$12+k$
  - _why:_ Adding an integer to 12 does not generally produce a multiple of 12. The required form is $12k$, meaning 12 multiplied by an integer.
- $$k^{12}$
  - _why:_ Raising $k$ to the twelfth power does not ensure the result is divisible by 12, especially when $k=1$. A multiple of 12 has the form $12k$.
- $$k+12k$
  - _why:_ This simplifies to $13k$, which is a multiple of 13 rather than necessarily a multiple of 12. The correct form is $12k$.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expansion of $(a+b)^2$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The expansion of $(a+b)^2$ is $\boxed{a^2+2ab+b^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$a^2+2ab+b^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying $(a+b)(a+b)$ gives $a^2+ab+ab+b^2$, and the two middle terms combine to $2ab$.
- $$a^2+ab+b^2$
  - _why:_ There are two cross-products, $ab$ and $ab$, so they combine to $2ab$, not $ab$. The correct expansion is $a^2+2ab+b^2$.
- $$a^2-2ab+b^2$
  - _why:_ The negative middle term comes from expanding $(a-b)^2$. With a plus sign, both cross-products are positive, giving $a^2+2ab+b^2$.
- $$a^2+2ab-b^2$
  - _why:_ The final term is $b$ multiplied by $b$, so it is positive $b^2$. Therefore $(a+b)^2$ expands to $a^2+2ab+b^2$.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** A square number is an expression that can be written in the form $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** A square number is an expression that can be written in the form $\boxed{m^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$m^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A square number is obtained by multiplying an expression by itself, so it has the form $m \times m = m^2$.
- $$2m$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $m$ by 2 gives twice the expression, not its square. A square number results from multiplying $m$ by itself, giving $m^2$.
- $$m^3$
  - _why:_ The exponent 3 represents a cube because $m^3 = m \times m \times m$. A square number has exponent 2, so its form is $m^2$.
- $$m + 2$
  - _why:_ Adding 2 to $m$ increases it by a fixed amount but does not square it. A square number is formed by multiplying $m$ by itself, giving $m^2$.
