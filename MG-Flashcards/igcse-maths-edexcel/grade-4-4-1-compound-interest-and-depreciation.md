## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What multiplier decreases an amount by 12%?

**Back:** 0.88

**Choices:**

- 0.88 ✔
  - _why:_ A 12% decrease leaves 88% of the original amount. Converting 88% to a decimal gives the multiplier 0.88.
- 0.12
  - _why:_ Using 0.12 finds the amount removed, rather than the amount remaining. A 12% decrease leaves 88%, so the correct multiplier is 0.88.
- 1.12
  - _why:_ Adding 12% to 100% produces an increase multiplier. For a 12% decrease, subtract from 100% to obtain 88%, giving 0.88.
- 0.98
  - _why:_ This corresponds to a decrease of only 2%, since 98% remains. A 12% decrease leaves 88%, so the required multiplier is 0.88.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** An increase of 2.7% uses multiplier [ ___ ].

**Back:** An increase of 2.7% uses multiplier 1.027.

**Choices:**

- 1.027 ✔
  - _why:_ An increase of 2.7% means retaining 100% and adding 2.7%, giving 102.7%. Dividing by 100 converts this to the multiplier 1.027.
- 1.27
  - _why:_ This treats 2.7% as 27%, producing a 27% increase. The decimal form of 102.7% is 1.027, which is the correct multiplier.
- 0.973
  - _why:_ Subtracting 2.7% from 100% gives a decrease multiplier. An increase requires adding to 100%, so 102.7% becomes the multiplier 1.027.
- 0.027
  - _why:_ This represents only the 2.7% increase itself and omits the original 100%. Including the original amount gives 102.7%, or a multiplier of 1.027.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** Compound interest and depreciation use repeated [ ___ ], not repeated adding/subtracting.

**Back:** Compound interest and depreciation use repeated multiplication, not repeated adding/subtracting.

**Choices:**

- multiplication ✔
  - _why:_ Each period’s amount is found by multiplying the previous amount by the same growth or decay multiplier, so the change compounds over time.
- addition
  - _why:_ Repeated addition models a fixed numerical change, not a fixed percentage change. Compound interest and depreciation repeatedly multiply the current amount by a percentage multiplier.
- subtraction
  - _why:_ Repeated subtraction removes a fixed amount each period and therefore does not model percentage depreciation. The correct process is repeated multiplication by a decay multiplier.
- division
  - _why:_ Division is not the general repeated operation used for compound change. Each new value is calculated by multiplying the previous value by the appropriate multiplier.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is a 6% increase then a 10% decrease not an overall 4% decrease?

**Back:** The 10% is taken from the new amount.

**Choices:**

- The 10% is taken from the new amount. ✔
  - _why:_ After the 6% increase, the amount has changed, so the subsequent 10% decrease uses a different base. The percentage changes therefore cannot simply be subtracted.
- The 6% is taken from the new amount.
  - _why:_ The 6% increase is applied first, so it is calculated from the original amount. It is the later 10% decrease that is taken from the new amount.
- The 10% is taken from the original amount.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly assumes both percentages use the same base. After the increase, the 10% decrease is calculated from the new amount, so the net change is not -4%.
- The 6% is added after the 10% decrease.
  - _why:_ This reverses the stated order and does not explain why subtracting the rates fails. The key point is that the 10% uses the new amount as its base.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** With compound interest, future interest is calculated on the [ ___ ].

**Back:** With compound interest, future interest is calculated on the new, larger amount.

**Choices:**

- new, larger amount ✔
  - _why:_ Each interest payment is added to the balance, so the next period’s interest is calculated on both the principal and previously earned interest.
- original principal only
  - _why:_ Calculating every period’s interest only on the original principal describes simple interest. Compound interest uses the new, larger amount after previous interest has been added.
- interest earned only
  - _why:_ Compound interest is not calculated solely on the accumulated interest. It is calculated on the whole new balance, including the original principal and earlier interest.
- original, smaller amount
  - _why:_ The calculation base does not remain at the original amount. After interest is added, future interest is calculated on the new, larger amount.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Which formula gives final amount for principal $P$, rate $r$%, over $n$ years of compound interest?

**Back:** $A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$

**Choices:**

- $A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$ ✔
  - _why:_ The multiplier for an annual increase of r% is $1+\frac{r}{100}$. Raising it to power n applies the same compound increase for n years.
- $A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{100}\right)n$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by n does not repeatedly apply the annual percentage multiplier and resembles a simple-growth error. Compound interest requires the multiplier to be raised to power n.
- $A=P\left(1-\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$
  - _why:_ The minus sign creates a depreciation multiplier, causing the amount to decrease each year. Compound interest at a positive rate uses $1+\frac{r}{100}$.
- $A=P\left(1+\frac{100}{r}\right)^n$
  - _why:_ This reverses the percentage conversion and produces an excessively large multiplier. A rate of r% is written as $\frac{r}{100}$ before adding it to 1.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If $V=9000\times1.018^n$, what annual interest rate is used?

**Back:** 1.8%

**Choices:**

- 1.8% ✔
  - _why:_ The annual multiplier 1.018 represents 101.8% of the previous value. The increase above 100% is therefore 1.8%, giving an annual interest rate of 1.8%.
- 18%
  - _why:_ This misreads the decimal places in 1.018. An 18% rate would use a multiplier of 1.18, whereas 1.018 corresponds to an increase of 1.8%.
- 0.18%
  - _why:_ This divides the percentage increase by 10 unnecessarily. A multiplier of 1.018 is 0.018 above 1, and $0.018\times100=1.8\%$.
- 101.8%
  - _why:_ This gives the entire multiplier as a percentage rather than the interest rate. The multiplier is 101.8% of the previous value, so the increase is 1.8%.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find total interest, subtract the [ ___ ] from the final amount.

**Back:** To find total interest, subtract the original amount from the final amount.

**Choices:**

- original amount ✔
  - _why:_ The final amount contains both the initial investment and the interest earned. Removing the original amount leaves only the total interest accumulated.
- annual interest
  - _why:_ Subtracting one year’s interest does not isolate the total earned over all years. Total interest is the final amount minus the original amount invested.
- interest rate
  - _why:_ A percentage rate cannot be subtracted directly from a monetary final amount because the units differ. Subtract the original monetary amount to find total interest.
- yearly multiplier
  - _why:_ The multiplier is a scale factor, not a monetary amount, so subtracting it from the final balance is invalid. Total interest equals final amount minus original amount.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** £2700 is invested at 3% compound interest for 4 years. What is the final amount to the nearest penny?

**Back:** £3038.87

**Choices:**

- £3038.87 ✔
  - _why:_ Compound interest applies the 3% multiplier, 1.03, four times: £2700 × 1.03⁴ = £3038.87 to the nearest penny.
- £3024.00
  - _why:_ This applies 3% simple interest only once to the original investment. Compound interest uses £2700 × 1.03⁴, giving £3038.87.
- £3042.00
  - _why:_ This treats the annual increases as if each were calculated from a rounded or altered amount. Keeping full precision throughout gives £3038.87.
- £3132.00
  - _why:_ This uses an excessive overall increase, effectively adding more than four applications of 3%. The correct calculation is £2700 × 1.03⁴ = £3038.87.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** Fixed-rate depreciation formula: [ ___ ].

**Back:** Fixed-rate depreciation formula: $A=P\left(1-\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$.

**Choices:**

- $$A=P\left(1-\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$ ✔
  - _why:_ Fixed-rate depreciation repeatedly keeps the fraction $1-\frac{r}{100}$ of the value, so the starting amount $P$ is multiplied by this factor $n$ times.
- $$A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$
  - _why:_ The plus sign gives compound growth, not depreciation. For a value that falls by $r$ percent each period, the correct formula uses $1-\frac{r}{100}$.
- $$A=P\left(\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$
  - _why:_ This repeatedly keeps only the depreciation rate itself, rather than the percentage remaining. The correct multiplier is $1-\frac{r}{100}$, giving $A=P\left(1-\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$.
- $$A=P-n\left(\frac{r}{100}\right)$
  - _why:_ This subtracts a fixed quantity and omits repeated percentage multiplication. Fixed-rate depreciation is calculated with $A=P\left(1-\frac{r}{100}\right)^n$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What multiplier represents 14% depreciation each year?

**Back:** 0.86

**Choices:**

- 0.86 ✔
  - _why:_ A 14% decrease leaves 100% − 14% = 86%, and 86% written as a decimal is 0.86.
- 0.14
  - _why:_ This is the decimal representing the amount lost, not the amount remaining. A 14% depreciation leaves 86%, so the multiplier is 0.86.
- 1.14
  - _why:_ Adding the rate to 1 represents a 14% increase rather than a decrease. Depreciation uses 1 − 0.14, giving the multiplier 0.86.
- 0.96
  - _why:_ This corresponds to a 4% decrease, a common rate-copying error. Subtracting 14% from 100% gives 86%, so the correct multiplier is 0.86.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** If an item depreciates by 30%, why is the multiplier 0.70 rather than 0.30?

**Back:** The multiplier is the percentage left, not lost.

**Choices:**

- The multiplier is the percentage left, not lost. ✔
  - _why:_ After losing 30%, 70% remains. Converting 70% to a decimal gives the multiplier 0.70, which is used to calculate the new value.
- The multiplier is the percentage lost, not left.
  - _why:_ The lost percentage measures the reduction, but multiplication needs the fraction remaining. Losing 30% leaves 70%, so the multiplier is 0.70.
- The multiplier is the percentage increase, not decrease.
  - _why:_ Depreciation is a decrease, so the relevant multiplier is found by subtracting the loss from 100%. A 30% loss leaves 70%, giving 0.70.
- The multiplier is the original percentage, not changed.
  - _why:_ The original value is reduced, so its multiplier must be below 1. Subtracting the 30% loss from 100% gives the correct multiplier, 0.70.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** A car worth £16 000 loses 25%, then 15% in each of the next 2 years. What is it worth after 3 years?

**Back:** £8670

**Choices:**

- £8670 ✔
  - _why:_ Apply each depreciation successively: £16 000 × 0.75 × 0.85 × 0.85 = £8670. The later percentages reduce the already-depreciated value.
- £8800
  - _why:_ This is close but does not apply the two 15% reductions accurately to the changing value. Multiplying £16 000 by 0.75, 0.85 and 0.85 gives £8670.
- £9600
  - _why:_ This applies only the initial 25% loss and ignores both later depreciations. The three successive multipliers produce the correct value of £8670.
- £8400
  - _why:_ This treats the percentage losses as a larger combined reduction or applies one reduction incorrectly. The correct successive calculation is £16 000 × 0.75 × 0.85² = £8670.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** £280 000 increases by 4%, then 1.5%, then decreases by 3%. What is its final value?

**Back:** £286 966.40

**Choices:**

- £286 966.40 ✔
  - _why:_ The successive percentage changes are applied using their multipliers in order: increase by 4%, increase by 1.5%, then reduce by 3% to obtain the stated final value.
- £286 400.00
  - _why:_ This approximates the changes using a simplified net percentage rather than applying each multiplier successively. The stated compound calculation gives £286 966.40.
- £288 400.00
  - _why:_ This does not reduce the value correctly after the two increases, often caused by adding the 3% decrease instead. Applying all three changes gives £286 966.40.

  - _why:_ This is a likely arithmetic slip when combining the successive changes. Each percentage must be applied to the current value, producing £286 966.40.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** When finding unknown years $n$ from start, rate and final amount, what do you try at Grade 4?

**Back:** Try powers of the multiplier until you match or pass the target.

**Choices:**

- Try powers of the multiplier until you match or pass the target. ✔
  - _why:_ At Grade 4, test successive powers such as $1.03^n$ or $0.86^n$. Compare the resulting amount with the target until the required number of years is found.
- Try multiples of the percentage until you match or pass the target.
  - _why:_ Compound change is not found by repeatedly adding the percentage to the original amount. Test successive powers of the multiplier, which gives the correct number of years.
- Try factors of the original amount until you match or pass the target.
  - _why:_ The original amount is not repeatedly factorised to find time. The multiplier is raised to successive powers and compared with the target amount.
- Try differences of the final amount until you match or pass the target.
  - _why:_ Subtracting differences ignores the repeated percentage change on the changing balance. Try powers of the multiplier until the calculated amount matches or passes the target.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** £4800 grows at 2% compound interest to £5299.59. How many years?

**Back:** 5 years

**Choices:**

- 5 years ✔
  - _why:_ Solve by checking the compound multiplier: £4800 × 1.02⁵ = £5299.59 to the nearest penny, so the investment grows to the stated amount after 5 years.
- 4 years
  - _why:_ Four years gives £4800 × 1.02⁴, which is below £5299.59. The fifth application of the 1.02 multiplier reaches the target, so the answer is 5 years.
- 6 years
  - _why:_ Six years gives a value above the stated target. Checking successive powers shows that £4800 × 1.02⁵ = £5299.59, so the correct time is 5 years.
- 3 years
  - _why:_ Three applications of the 1.02 multiplier produce a value well below £5299.59. Five applications are needed to reach the target, giving 5 years.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** With 15% annual depreciation, how many years until the value is below half?

**Back:** 5 years

**Choices:**

- 5 years ✔
  - _why:_ Each year the value is multiplied by $0.85$. After 4 years, about $52.2\%$ remains, but after 5 years only about $44.4\%$ remains, which is below half.
- 4 years
  - _why:_ Rounding the value after 4 years too aggressively can suggest it is below half. However, $0.85^4\approx0.522$, so 5 years are needed for the value to fall below $50\%$.
- 6 years
  - _why:_ This overlooks that the threshold was already crossed one year earlier. Since $0.85^5\approx0.444$, the value is below half after 5 years, not 6 years.
- 7 years
  - _why:_ Dividing $100\%$ by $15\%$ treats depreciation as a simple linear decrease. Compound depreciation multiplies by $0.85$ annually, giving a value below half after 5 years.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** Simple interest is calculated only on the [ ___ ], not the growing balance.

**Back:** Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount, not the growing balance.

**Choices:**

- original amount ✔
  - _why:_ Simple interest is always calculated using the initial principal, or original amount. Unlike compound interest, previously earned interest is not included when calculating later interest.
- current balance
  - _why:_ Using the current balance would mean previously earned interest affects later interest, which describes compound interest. Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount.
- final amount
  - _why:_ The final amount includes the original principal and all accumulated interest, so it cannot be the basis for calculating that interest. Simple interest uses the original amount.
- annual interest
  - _why:_ Annual interest is the amount added each year, not the quantity on which the percentage is calculated. The simple-interest rate is applied to the original amount.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** 2.8% simple interest per annum for 5 years gives what total interest rate?

**Back:** 14%

**Choices:**

- 14% ✔
  - _why:_ Simple interest adds the same $2.8\%$ of the original amount each year. Over 5 years, the total interest rate is $2.8\%\times5=14\%$.
- 7.8%
  - _why:_ Adding the rate to the number of years gives $2.8+5=7.8$, but rates accumulate by multiplication. The total simple interest rate is $2.8\%\times5=14\%$.
- 13.2%
  - _why:_ This is approximately the result of subtracting compound depreciation factors, not calculating simple interest. Simple interest accumulates linearly, so $2.8\%\times5=14\%$.
- 14.8%
  - _why:_ This commonly results from an addition or place-value slip after multiplying. Five years of $2.8\%$ simple interest gives exactly $5\times2.8\%=14\%$.
