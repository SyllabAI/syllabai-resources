## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In the formula $v = u + at$, why is $v$ considered the subject?

**Back:** It is the variable isolated on one side of the equals sign.

**Choices:**

- It is the variable isolated on one side of the equals sign. ✔
  - _why:_ The subject is the variable written alone on one side of an equation. Here, $v$ is isolated on the left, while all other variables are on the right.
- It is the variable written first on one side of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ A variable’s position in the written formula does not determine the subject. The correct reason is that $v$ is isolated on one side of the equals sign.
- It is the variable with the greatest power on one side of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ The subject is not chosen by comparing powers of variables. Here, $v$ is the subject because it is isolated on one side of the equals sign.
- It is the variable calculated first from the values in the formula.
  - _why:_ The order in which values are calculated does not define the subject. A subject is the variable isolated on one side, which is $v$ in this formula.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the goal when asked to "make $x$ the subject" of a formula?

**Back:** To rearrange it so $x$ is alone on one side and the other side does not contain $x$.

**Choices:**

- To rearrange it so $x$ is alone on one side and the other side does not contain $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ Making $x$ the subject means completely isolating $x$. It must appear alone on one side, with an equivalent expression containing no $x$ on the other side.
- To rearrange it so $x$ is alone on one side and the other side also contains $x$.
  - _why:_ Leaving $x$ on both sides means it has not been fully isolated. The correct arrangement has $x$ alone on one side and no $x$ on the other.
- To rearrange it so $x$ is squared on one side and the other side does not contain $x$.
  - _why:_ Obtaining $x^2$ does not make $x$ itself the subject. Any remaining power must be undone so $x$ is alone and absent from the other side.
- To rearrange it so $x$ is in a fraction and the other side does not contain $x$.
  - _why:_ Placing $x$ inside a numerator or denominator does not necessarily isolate it. The goal is to have $x$ alone on one side and nowhere on the other.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the inverse operation of squaring a variable?

**Back:** Taking the square root.

**Choices:**

- Taking the square root. ✔
  - _why:_ Taking a square root reverses squaring because $csqrt{x^2}$ returns the magnitude of $x$. When solving equations, both positive and negative roots may need consideration.
- Dividing by two.
  - _why:_ The exponent $2$ does not mean that the variable was multiplied by $2$, so division by $2$ will not undo it. Squaring is reversed by taking the square root.
- Taking the reciprocal.
  - _why:_ A reciprocal reverses multiplication by a quantity or a negative exponent, not squaring. The inverse operation needed to undo a square is taking the square root.
- Taking the cube root.
  - _why:_ A cube root reverses raising a variable to the power $3$, not the power $2$. The operation that reverses squaring is taking the square root.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the inverse operation of square-rooting a variable?

**Back:** Squaring it.

**Choices:**

- Squaring it. ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring reverses the principal square-root operation: if $y=csqrt{x}$, then $y^2=x$. Therefore, squaring both sides removes a square root when rearranging a formula.
- Halving it.
  - _why:_ The root symbol does not mean division by $2$, so halving cannot undo it. A square root is removed by squaring the relevant expression or both sides.
- Reciprocating it.
  - _why:_ Taking a reciprocal changes $csqrt{x}$ into $1/csqrt{x}$ rather than recovering $x$. The inverse operation of square-rooting is squaring.
- Doubling it.
  - _why:_ A square root is not equivalent to dividing a value by $2$, so doubling does not reverse it. Squaring the square-rooted expression correctly returns the original value.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** If $x^2 = 25$ in a pure algebra problem, then $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If $x^2 = 25$ in a pure algebra problem, then $\boxed{x = \pm 5}$.

**Choices:**

- $$x = \pm 5$ ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring either $5$ or $-5$ gives $25$, so a pure algebraic equation requires both possible square roots: $x = \pm 5$.
- $$x = 5$
  - _why:_ Taking only the positive square root omits $x=-5$, which also satisfies the equation because $(-5)^2=25$. Therefore, $x = \pm 5$.
- $$x = -5$
  - _why:_ Taking only the negative square root omits $x=5$, which also satisfies the equation because $5^2=25$. Therefore, $x = \pm 5$.
- $$x = \pm 25$
  - _why:_ This fails to take the square root of $25$: squaring $25$ gives $625$, not $25$. The two solutions are $x = \pm 5$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When is it acceptable to ignore the negative root when rearranging a formula?

**Back:** When the variable represents a real-life physical quantity that cannot be negative (e.g., length, time).

**Choices:**

- When the variable represents a real-life physical quantity that cannot be negative (e.g., length, time). ✔
  - _why:_ Although algebra may produce two roots, context can exclude one. Quantities such as length and elapsed time cannot be negative, so only the physically meaningful positive root is retained.
- When the equation contains a squared variable that has two roots (e.g., $x^2=25$).
  - _why:_ Having two algebraic roots is precisely why both normally need to be stated. The negative root may be discarded only when the variable’s real-life meaning makes negative values impossible.
- When every value substituted into the formula is already positive (e.g., positive constants).
  - _why:_ Positive input values do not automatically rule out a negative solution after rearrangement. The negative root is ignored only if the target quantity itself cannot physically be negative.
- When the positive root is easier to use and simpler to write (e.g., a whole number).
  - _why:_ Convenience or simplicity is not a valid reason to reject a solution. A negative root can be excluded only when the context shows that the represented quantity cannot be negative.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If the target variable is in the denominator of a fraction, what is the first step?

**Back:** Multiply both sides by that denominator to clear the fraction.

**Choices:**

- Multiply both sides by that denominator to clear the fraction. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term on both sides by the denominator cancels it from the fraction. This moves the target variable out of the denominator while preserving the equation’s equality.
- Divide both sides by that denominator to clear the fraction.
  - _why:_ Dividing by the denominator introduces another denominator factor rather than cancelling the original one. Multiplying both sides by that denominator clears the fraction correctly.
- Multiply only the numerator by that denominator to clear the fraction.
  - _why:_ Changing only the numerator alters one expression without applying an equivalent operation to the whole equation. The denominator is cleared by multiplying both complete sides by it.
- Square both sides using that denominator to clear the fraction.
  - _why:_ Squaring does not directly cancel a denominator and can unnecessarily introduce extra solutions. The appropriate first step is to multiply both sides by the denominator.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you remove the fraction from the term $\frac{1}{2}at^2$ when rearranging?

**Back:** Multiply both sides of the equation by 2.

**Choices:**

- Multiply both sides of the equation by 2. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $2$ cancels the factor $cfrac{1}{2}$ because $2ctimescfrac{1}{2}=1$. Applying this to both sides preserves equality and leaves the term $at^2$.
- Divide both sides of the equation by 2.
  - _why:_ Dividing by $2$ introduces another factor of $cfrac{1}{2}$, making the term $cfrac{1}{4}at^2$. Multiplying both sides by $2$ cancels the original fraction.
- Multiply both sides of the equation by $cfrac{1}{2}$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by another $cfrac{1}{2}$ makes the coefficient $cfrac{1}{4}$ rather than removing it. The reciprocal operation is multiplication by $2$ on both sides.
- Square both sides of the equation to remove the 2.
  - _why:_ Squaring affects every term and turns the coefficient into $cfrac{1}{4}$ rather than cancelling it. Multiplying both sides by $2$ directly removes the factor $cfrac{1}{2}$.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must [ ___ ] the target terms, factorise the letter, and finally divide.

**Back:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must collect the target terms, factorise the letter, and finally divide.

**Choices:**

- collect ✔
  - _why:_ Collecting places all terms containing the target letter on the same side, allowing that letter to be taken out as a common factor.
- expand
  - _why:_ Expanding removes brackets but does not bring all terms containing the target letter together. The correct first step is to collect those terms on the same side.
- cancel
  - _why:_ Cancelling is only valid for common factors in a fraction, not separate terms in an equation. The target terms must first be collected together.
- substitute
  - _why:_ Substitution replaces a variable with a known expression or value, so it does not isolate a repeated letter. The required first step is to collect its terms.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must collect the target terms, [ ___ ] the letter, and finally divide.

**Back:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must collect the target terms, factorise the letter, and finally divide.

**Choices:**

- factorise ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising takes the repeated letter outside a bracket, changing an expression such as $ax+bx$ into $x(a+b)$ so the letter appears once.
- cancel

- expand
  - _why:_ Expanding would undo the useful common-factor form and leave the letter appearing several times. The collected expression must instead be factorised.
- square
  - _why:_ Squaring the letter changes the equation and is not an inverse operation required here. The repeated letter should be taken out as a common factor by factorising.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must collect the target terms, factorise the letter, and finally [ ___ ].

**Back:** To isolate a letter that appears more than once, you must collect the target terms, factorise the letter, and finally divide.

**Choices:**

- divide ✔
  - _why:_ After factorising, the target letter is multiplied by a coefficient or bracket. Dividing both sides by that factor leaves the target letter isolated.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplying would not remove the factor attached to the target letter. Once the letter has been factorised, divide by its remaining coefficient or bracket.
- subtract
  - _why:_ Subtraction removes an added term, not a factor multiplying the target letter. After factorising, the final step is division by that factor.
- add
  - _why:_ Addition reverses subtraction but cannot remove a factor multiplying the target letter. The factorised equation must finally be divided by the factor.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Which algebraic process converts $ax + bx$ into $x(a + b)$?

**Back:** Factorising (taking out a common factor).

**Choices:**

- Factorising (taking out a common factor). ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms contain the common factor $x$. Taking $x$ outside the bracket leaves the remaining coefficients $a+b$, giving $x(a+b)$.
- Expanding (multiplying out the brackets).
  - _why:_ Expanding performs the reverse transformation, converting $x(a+b)$ into $ax+bx$. The given conversion takes out the common factor $x$, so it is factorising.
- Simplifying (combining like terms).
  - _why:_ The terms $ax$ and $bx$ cannot generally be combined because their coefficients are different algebraic expressions. Rewriting them with common factor $x$ is factorising.
- Cancelling (removing common factors).
  - _why:_ Cancelling applies to common factors in a quotient and would remove a factor entirely. Here, $x$ is retained outside a bracket through factorising.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Make $k$ the subject of $m = n + kp$.

**Back:** $$k = \frac{m - n}{p}$

**Choices:**

- $$k = \frac{m - n}{p}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $n$ gives $m-n=kp$. Dividing both sides by $p$ then leaves $k$ alone, producing $k=\frac{m-n}{p}$.
- $$k = \frac{m + n}{p}$
  - _why:_ Adding $n$ repeats the original addition instead of reversing it. Subtract $n$ from both sides first, then divide by $p$ to obtain $k=\frac{m-n}{p}$.
- $$k = (m - n)p$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $p$ does not undo the multiplication in $kp$ and introduces an extra factor. After subtracting $n$, divide by $p$ instead.
- $$k = \frac{m}{p} - n$
  - _why:_ This divides only $m$ by $p$, although $p$ multiplies $k$ after the whole difference $m-n$ is formed. The entire numerator must be divided by $p$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Make $P$ the subject of $R^2 = P^2 + 5qt$.

**Back:** $$P = \pm\sqrt{R^2 - 5qt}$

**Choices:**

- $$P = \pm\sqrt{R^2 - 5qt}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $5qt$ gives $P^2=R^2-5qt$. Taking the square root requires both positive and negative roots, so $P=\pm\sqrt{R^2-5qt}$.
- $$P = \sqrt{R^2 - 5qt}$
  - _why:_ Taking only the positive square root loses one possible value of $P$. Since $P^2=R^2-5qt$, the complete answer is $P=\pm\sqrt{R^2-5qt}$.
- $$P = \pm\sqrt{R^2 + 5qt}$
  - _why:_ Adding $5qt$ is a sign error: it must be subtracted from both sides. This gives $P^2=R^2-5qt$, then $P=\pm\sqrt{R^2-5qt}$.
- $$P = \pm(R^2 - 5qt)$
  - _why:_ This omits the square-root operation when undoing the square. After obtaining $P^2=R^2-5qt$, take both square roots to get $P=\pm\sqrt{R^2-5qt}$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the result of factorising $2px - 2qx$ to make $x$ the subject?

**Back:** $$x(2p - 2q)$ (or $2x(p - q)$)

**Choices:**

- $$x(2p - 2q)$ (or $2x(p - q)$) ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms contain the common factor $2x$. Taking it outside the bracket leaves $p-q$, so the expression is $2x(p-q)$, equivalently $x(2p-2q)$.
- $$x(2p + 2q)$ (or $2x(p + q)$)
  - _why:_ The subtraction sign must remain when the common factor is extracted. Expanding this option gives $2px+2qx$, whereas the correct factorisation is $x(2p-2q)$.
- $$x(2p - 4q)$ (or $2x(p - 2q)$)
  - _why:_ Only one factor of 2 is common to both original terms. Expanding this option gives $2px-4qx$, so the correct factorisation is $x(2p-2q)$.
- $$x(2pq)$ (or $2x(pq)$)
  - _why:_ Factorising extracts common factors; it does not multiply $p$ and $q$ together. The shared factor is $2x$, leaving $p-q$, so the result is $2x(p-q)$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Make $x$ the subject of $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{3x}{x + 4}$.

**Back:** $$x = \frac{4a}{3b - a}$

**Choices:**

- $$x = \frac{4a}{3b - a}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Cross-multiplication gives $a(x+4)=3bx$. Expanding and collecting the terms containing $x$ gives $4a=x(3b-a)$, hence $x=\frac{4a}{3b-a}$.
- $$x = \frac{4a}{3b + a}$
  - _why:_ The $ax$ term changes sign when it is subtracted from both sides, producing $3bx-ax=x(3b-a)$. Thus the denominator is $3b-a$, not $3b+a$.
- $$x = \frac{a}{3b - 4a}$
  - _why:_ The 4 comes from expanding $a(x+4)$, so it multiplies $a$ in the numerator rather than appearing in the denominator. The correct result is $x=\frac{4a}{3b-a}$.
- $$x = \frac{4a}{a - 3b}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction in the collected coefficient of $x$. Since $3bx-ax=x(3b-a)$, the correct denominator is $3b-a$, giving $x=\frac{4a}{3b-a}$.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the "balance method" safer than just "moving" terms across the equals sign?

**Back:** It helps prevent sign errors by ensuring the same operation is applied to both sides.

**Choices:**

- It helps prevent sign errors by ensuring the same operation is applied to both sides. ✔
  - _why:_ Performing the same inverse operation on both sides preserves the equality. Writing that operation explicitly reduces the risk of accidentally changing a term’s sign incorrectly.
- It helps prevent fraction errors by ensuring every denominator is cancelled immediately.
  - _why:_ Cancelling every denominator immediately is neither always possible nor the defining safety feature. The balance method prevents sign errors by applying the same valid operation to both sides.
- It makes rearranging faster by allowing terms to change sides without an inverse operation.
  - _why:_ Terms cannot simply change sides without an inverse operation; that shorthand often causes sign mistakes. The balance method is safer because the same operation is explicitly applied to both sides.
- It ensures the unknown stays positive by keeping it on the left-hand side.
  - _why:_ Keeping an unknown on the left does not determine whether its value is positive. The method’s safety comes from applying identical operations to both sides, which prevents sign errors.
