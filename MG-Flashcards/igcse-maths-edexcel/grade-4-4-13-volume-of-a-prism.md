## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a 3D shape as a prism?

**Back:** It has the same 2D shape (cross-section) repeated throughout its length.

**Choices:**

- It has the same 2D shape (cross-section) repeated throughout its length. ✔
  - _why:_ A prism has a constant cross-section along its entire length, so every slice perpendicular to that length has the same shape and dimensions.
- It has the same 2D shape (cross-section) at each end only.
  - _why:_ Having identical shapes only at the two ends does not guarantee a prism, because the shape could change between them. The cross-section must remain the same throughout its length.
- It has a changing 2D shape (cross-section) throughout its length.
  - _why:_ This describes a solid whose cross-section changes along its length, such as a pyramid. In a prism, the same cross-section is repeated throughout the entire length.
- It has the same volume in every direction throughout its length.
  - _why:_ A constant volume does not define a type of three-dimensional shape. A prism is defined by having the same two-dimensional cross-section throughout its length.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The volume of any prism is calculated using the formula: $V = \text{[...]} \times \text{length}$.

**Back:** The volume of any prism is calculated using the formula: $V = \boxed{\text{area of cross-section}} \times \text{length}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\text{area of cross-section}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A prism is formed by extending a constant cross-section through a length, so its volume equals the cross-sectional area multiplied by that length.
- $$\text{perimeter of cross-section}$
  - _why:_ Perimeter measures the distance around the cross-section rather than the space inside it. Volume requires the area of the cross-section multiplied by the prism’s length.
- $$\text{total surface area}$
  - _why:_ The area of a prism’s surface is not used directly to calculate its volume. The required factor is the area of its constant cross-section.
- $$\text{width of cross-section}$
  - _why:_ Using the cross-sectional width alone omits another dimension and produces square rather than cubic units. The area of the cross-section must be multiplied by the length.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** The volume of any prism is calculated using the formula: $V = \text{area of cross-section} \times \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The volume of any prism is calculated using the formula: $V = \text{area of cross-section} \times \boxed{\text{length}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\text{length}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying the cross-sectional area by the distance through which that cross-section extends gives the prism’s volume in cubic units.
- $$\text{perimeter}$
  - _why:_ The perimeter describes the boundary of the cross-section and is not the distance through which it extends. The required multiplier is the prism’s length.
- $$\text{width}$
  - _why:_ Width may be one dimension within the cross-section, so multiplying by it can count a cross-sectional dimension twice. The correct multiplier is the prism’s length.
- $$\text{height}$
  - _why:_ Height may be needed when finding the cross-sectional area, but it is not necessarily the extrusion distance. The area must be multiplied by the prism’s length.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the length of a prism represent in the volume formula?

**Back:** The direction the cross-section has been stretched.

**Choices:**

- The direction the cross-section has been stretched. ✔
  - _why:_ The length is the extrusion distance: it measures how far the unchanged cross-section continues through the prism, perpendicular to the cross-sectional face.
- The distance around the edge of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ The distance around the cross-section is its perimeter, not the prism’s length. Length measures the direction in which the cross-section has been stretched.
- The distance across the height of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ The perpendicular height may be used to calculate the cross-sectional area. The prism’s length instead measures the direction through which that cross-section extends.
- The direction of the longest edge of the prism.
  - _why:_ The longest edge is not automatically the length used in the prism formula. The relevant length is the direction in which the cross-section has been stretched.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the volume of a cuboid?

**Back:** $$V = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height}$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cuboid has a rectangular cross-section, so multiplying its area, length times width, by its height gives the product of all three perpendicular dimensions.
- $$V = \text{length} + \text{width} + \text{height}$
  - _why:_ Adding the three dimensions does not calculate three-dimensional space and does not produce cubic units. A cuboid’s volume is their product: length times width times height.
- $$V = \text{length} \times \text{width}$
  - _why:_ This multiplies only two dimensions, so it gives the area of a rectangular face rather than volume. The height must also be included.
- $$V = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ The factor of one half belongs to triangular prisms, not cuboids. A cuboid’s rectangular cross-section requires the full product of length, width and height.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When calculating a triangular cross-section, which side length must be avoided for height?

**Back:** The sloping side (unless it is perpendicular to the base).

**Choices:**

- The sloping side (unless it is perpendicular to the base). ✔
  - _why:_ A triangle’s height must meet the chosen base at a right angle. A sloping side can be used only when it is perpendicular to that base.
- The perpendicular side (even when it forms a right angle).
  - _why:_ The perpendicular side is exactly the measurement needed as the triangle’s height. The side to avoid is a sloping side unless it meets the base at a right angle.
- The horizontal base (unless it is the longest side).
  - _why:_ The base is required in the triangle area formula and is not automatically the height. The measurement to avoid as height is a non-perpendicular sloping side.
- The vertical side (even when it is perpendicular to the base).
  - _why:_ A vertical line can serve as the height when it is perpendicular to the selected base. The unreliable choice is a sloping side that is not perpendicular.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the area of a trapezium cross-section.

**Back:** $$A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$, where $a, b$ are parallel sides$h$ is perpendicular height

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$, where $a, b$ are parallel sides$h$ is perpendicular height ✔
  - _why:_ A trapezium’s area is the mean of its two parallel side lengths multiplied by their perpendicular separation, giving $\frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$.
- $$A = (a+b)h$, where $a, b$ are parallel sides$h$ is perpendicular height
  - _why:_ Omitting the factor of one half doubles the trapezium’s area. The correct formula averages the parallel sides before multiplying by the perpendicular height.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2}(a-b)h$, where $a, b$ are parallel sides$h$ is perpendicular height
  - _why:_ Subtracting the parallel sides finds their difference rather than their mean and does not give the trapezium’s area. Their lengths must be added, halved and multiplied by height.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2}(ab)h$, where $a, b$ are parallel sides$h$ is perpendicular height
  - _why:_ Multiplying the two parallel sides together is not part of the trapezium formula and gives incorrect dimensions after multiplying by height. Their sum must be halved instead.

- $$10$
  - _why:_ This confuses centimetres with a ten-part conversion. Since each centimetre is one hundredth of a metre, one metre equals 100 centimetres.
- $$1000$
  - _why:_ This copies the litre-to-cubic-centimetre conversion into a linear length conversion. One metre contains 100 centimetres, whereas one litre contains 1000 cubic centimetres.
- $$0.01$
  - _why:_ This reverses the conversion and treats metres per centimetre as centimetres per metre. One centimetre is 0.01 metres, but one metre is 100 centimetres.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the volume of a prism with a composite (e.g. L-shaped) cross-section?

**Back:** Split cross-section into simpler shapes (rectangles/triangles)Sum the individual areasMultiply total area by prism length

**Choices:**

- Split cross-section into simpler shapes (rectangles/triangles)Sum the individual areasMultiply total area by prism length ✔
  - _why:_ A composite cross-section can be divided into familiar shapes whose areas are added. Multiplying this total cross-sectional area by the constant prism length gives the volume.
- Split cross-section into simpler shapes (rectangles/triangles)Sum the individual perimetersMultiply total perimeter by prism length
  - _why:_ Using perimeter gives the lateral surface area rather than volume. The individual cross-sectional areas must be added, then multiplied by the prism length.
- Split cross-section into simpler shapes (rectangles/triangles)Sum the individual areasDivide total area by prism length
  - _why:_ Dividing area by length does not produce cubic units. Volume is found by multiplying the total cross-sectional area by the prism length.
- Split cross-section into simpler shapes (rectangles/triangles)Multiply the individual areasMultiply total area by prism length
  - _why:_ The areas of separate parts must be added because together they form the whole cross-section. Multiplying those areas together has no geometric meaning for this volume calculation.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** If the volume and two dimensions of a cuboid are known, how is the missing dimension found?

**Back:** Multiply the known dimensionsDivide the volume by that product

**Choices:**

- Multiply the known dimensionsDivide the volume by that product ✔
  - _why:_ For a cuboid, $V=lwh$. Multiplying the two known dimensions gives their product, and dividing the volume by it isolates the missing dimension.
- Add the known dimensionsDivide the volume by that total
  - _why:_ The cuboid volume formula multiplies dimensions rather than adding them. Multiply the two known dimensions, then divide the volume by their product to find the missing dimension.
- Multiply the known dimensionsMultiply the volume by that product
  - _why:_ Multiplying again makes the units and value too large. Since the known dimensions are factors of the volume, divide the volume by their product.
- Divide the known dimensionsMultiply the volume by that quotient
  - _why:_ Dividing the known dimensions does not rearrange $V=lwh$ correctly. Their product must divide the volume, leaving the missing dimension in linear units.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the side length of a cube if you only know the area of one face?

**Back:** Calculate the square root of the face area.

**Choices:**

- Calculate the square root of the face area. ✔
  - _why:_ Each face of a cube is a square with area $s^2$. Taking the square root of the face area therefore gives the side length $s$.
- Calculate the cube root of the face area.
  - _why:_ A cube root is used to recover a side length from a cube's volume, not from a face area. Since face area is $s^2$, use the square root.
- Calculate the square of the face area.
  - _why:_ Squaring the face area increases the power and cannot produce a length. The face area equals $s^2$, so take its square root to obtain $s$.
- Calculate the quarter of the face area.
  - _why:_ Dividing by four would relate a square's perimeter to its side, not its area. For face area $s^2$, the side length is its square root.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the steps to find a cube's volume from its total surface area.

**Back:** Divide surface area by 6 to get one face areaFind the square root to get side lengthCube the side length ($s^3$)

**Choices:**

- Divide surface area by 6 to get one face areaFind the square root to get side lengthCube the side length ($s^3$) ✔
  - _why:_ A cube has six identical square faces, so dividing by six gives $s^2$. Its square root gives $s$, and cubing $s$ gives the volume.
- Divide surface area by 4 to get one face areaFind the square root to get side lengthCube the side length ($s^3$)
  - _why:_ Dividing by four counts only the lateral faces and ignores the top and bottom. A cube has six equal faces, so first divide the surface area by six.
- Divide surface area by 6 to get one face areaFind the cube root to get side lengthCube the side length ($s^3$)
  - _why:_ One face has area $s^2$, so its side is found using a square root, not a cube root. The resulting side length is then cubed.
- Divide surface area by 6 to get one face areaFind the square root to get side lengthSquare the side length ($s^2$)
  - _why:_ Squaring the side length returns the area of one face rather than the cube's volume. Volume requires three dimensions, so the side length must be cubed.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Why might a volume comparison not perfectly predict how many boxes fit in a van?

**Back:** Boxes must fit along specific dimensions (length, width, height) without awkward gaps.

**Choices:**

- Boxes must fit along specific dimensions (length, width, height) without awkward gaps. ✔
  - _why:_ Equal or suitable total volumes do not guarantee a practical arrangement. Each box must fit within the van’s individual dimensions, and unused gaps may remain.
- Boxes only need to have a smaller total volume than the van, regardless of their dimensions.
  - _why:_ Comparing total volumes alone ignores the arrangement of the boxes. The correct explanation is that boxes must fit along specific dimensions without leaving awkward gaps.
- Boxes can always be fitted by changing their dimensions while keeping their volume constant.
  - _why:_ This assumes rigid boxes can be reshaped, which is not normally possible. Boxes must fit along the van’s specific length, width and height without awkward gaps.
- Boxes will fill the van exactly if their combined volume equals the van’s volume.
  - _why:_ Equal combined volume does not ensure that the boxes tessellate inside the available space. Their specific dimensions may prevent them fitting without awkward gaps.

  - _why:_ Linear units measure one-dimensional length rather than volume. The correct term is cubic because volume involves three dimensions and uses units such as $cm^3$.
- decimal
  - _why:_ Decimal describes a way of writing numbers, not the dimension of a unit. Volume is measured in cubic units, such as $cm^3$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** A cube has a square face with area $36\text{ cm}^2$. What is its volume?

**Back:** $$216\text{ cm}^3$ (Side is $6$, and $6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216$).

**Choices:**

- $$216\text{ cm}^3$ (Side is $6$, and $6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216$). ✔
  - _why:_ The square face has side length $\sqrt{36}=6$ cm. Cubing this side length gives the cube’s volume: $6^3=216\text{ cm}^3$.
- $$36\text{ cm}^3$ (The face area is used directly as the volume).
  - _why:_ This confuses the two-dimensional face area with the three-dimensional volume. The side is $\sqrt{36}=6$ cm, so the volume is $6^3=216\text{ cm}^3$.
- $$1296\text{ cm}^3$ (Side is $36$, and $36 \times 36 = 1296$).
  - _why:_ This treats the face area as a side length and then squares it. The actual side is $\sqrt{36}=6$ cm, giving volume $6^3=216\text{ cm}^3$.
- $$18\text{ cm}^3$ (Side is $6$, and $6 + 6 + 6 = 18$).
  - _why:_ This adds the three dimensions instead of multiplying them. A cube’s volume is length times width times height, so $6\times6\times6=216\text{ cm}^3$.
