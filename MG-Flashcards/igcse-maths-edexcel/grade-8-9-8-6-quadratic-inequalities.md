## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the roots of a quadratic expression?

**Back:** The values of $x$ that make the expression equal to zero.

**Choices:**

- The values of $x$ that make the expression equal to zero. ✔
  - _why:_ A root is an input value for which the quadratic expression equals zero. Solving the corresponding quadratic equation therefore finds all of the expression’s roots.
- The values of $x$ that make the expression equal to one.
  - _why:_ Confusing roots with solutions to an equation whose right-hand side is one changes the problem. Roots specifically make the quadratic expression equal to zero.
- The values of $x$ that make the expression reach its turning point.
  - _why:_ The turning point has one x-coordinate and gives the quadratic’s maximum or minimum, not generally its roots. Roots are the x-values that make the expression zero.
- The values of $x$ that make the expression equal to its y-intercept.
  - _why:_ The y-intercept is the value of the expression when $x=0$, so it does not define the roots. Roots are values of $x$ that make the expression zero.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** How do the roots of a quadratic relate to its graph?

**Back:** They are the points where the curve crosses or touches the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- They are the points where the curve crosses or touches the x-axis. ✔
  - _why:_ Every point on the x-axis has $y=0$. Therefore, the roots occur where the quadratic graph crosses or just touches the x-axis.
- They are the points where the curve crosses or touches the y-axis.
  - _why:_ The graph meets the y-axis when $x=0$, which gives the y-intercept rather than the roots. Roots occur where the curve crosses or touches the x-axis.
- They are the points where the curve reaches its maximum or minimum.
  - _why:_ A maximum or minimum is the parabola’s turning point, not its roots. Roots are found where the curve crosses or touches the x-axis.
- They are the points where the curve crosses its line of symmetry.
  - _why:_ The curve meets its line of symmetry at the turning point, which need not lie on the x-axis. Roots occur where the curve crosses or touches the x-axis.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do the roots of a quadratic function help solve an inequality?

**Back:** They split the number line into regions where the expression is either positive or negative.

**Choices:**

- They split the number line into regions where the expression is either positive or negative. ✔
  - _why:_ The expression can change sign only at its roots. These boundary values divide the number line into regions where the quadratic remains positive or remains negative.
- They split the number line into regions where the expression is either increasing or decreasing.
  - _why:_ Increasing and decreasing behaviour is determined by the turning point, not by both roots. The roots divide the number line into regions where the expression is positive or negative.
- They split the number line into regions where the expression is either even or odd.
  - _why:_ Even and odd describe types of functions or integers, not the sign of a quadratic over intervals. Roots create regions where the expression is positive or negative.
- They split the number line into regions where the expression is either linear or quadratic.
  - _why:_ A quadratic expression remains quadratic throughout its domain; its algebraic type does not change at the roots. The roots instead separate positive and negative regions.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic with a positive leading coefficient forms a U-shaped curve called a [ ___ ].

**Back:** A quadratic with a positive leading coefficient forms a U-shaped curve called a parabola.

**Choices:**

- parabola ✔
  - _why:_ The graph of every quadratic function is a parabola. A positive leading coefficient makes its arms rise on both sides, producing a U-shaped curve.
- hyperbola
  - _why:_ A hyperbola is associated with reciprocal-type relationships, not quadratic functions. A quadratic with a positive leading coefficient produces an upward-opening parabola.
- ellipse
  - _why:_ An ellipse is a closed conic section rather than the graph of a quadratic function in this form. The U-shaped quadratic curve is called a parabola.
- circle
  - _why:_ A circle is a closed curve and is not the graph of a quadratic function $y=ax^2+bx+c$. Such a function forms a parabola.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** For an upward-opening parabola, where is the quadratic negative ($<0$)?

**Back:** Between the two roots.

**Choices:**

- Between the two roots. ✔
  - _why:_ An upward-opening parabola lies below the x-axis between two distinct roots. Since points below the x-axis have negative y-values, the quadratic is negative there.
- Outside the two roots.
  - _why:_ This reverses the sign regions for an upward-opening parabola. The outer tails lie above the x-axis, while the quadratic is negative between the two roots.
- At the two roots.
  - _why:_ At each root, the quadratic equals zero rather than being negative. For an upward-opening parabola with two roots, negative values occur strictly between them.
- To the right of the two roots.
  - _why:_ To the right of both roots, an upward-opening parabola is on its rising outer tail and is positive. It is negative between the two roots.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** For an upward-opening parabola, where is the quadratic positive ($>0$)?

**Back:** Outside the two roots (the two separate tails).

**Choices:**

- Outside the two roots (the two separate tails). ✔
  - _why:_ An upward-opening parabola lies above the x-axis on both outer tails. Therefore, the quadratic is positive to the left of the smaller root and right of the larger root.
- Between the two roots (the single central region).
  - _why:_ This reverses the signs for an upward-opening parabola. The curve lies below the x-axis between its roots and above it on the two outer tails.
- At the two roots (the two boundary points).
  - _why:_ At the roots, the quadratic equals zero, so it is not positive under a strict $>0$ inequality. Positive values occur outside the two roots.
- Inside the two roots (the region around the vertex).
  - _why:_ For an upward-opening parabola with two roots, the vertex and central region lie below the x-axis. The quadratic is positive outside the roots, on both tails.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the inequality sign is $\le$ or $\ge$, the final solution must [ ___ ] the boundary roots.

**Back:** If the inequality sign is $\le$ or $\ge$, the final solution must include the boundary roots.

**Choices:**

- include ✔
  - _why:_ The symbols $le$ and $ge$ allow equality as well as inequality. Because the expression equals zero at each boundary root, those roots must be included.
- exclude
  - _why:_ Excluding the roots is appropriate for strict signs $$, where equality is not allowed. The signs $le$ and $ge$ require the boundary roots to be included.
- reverse
  - _why:_ Reversing boundary roots is not a valid step in solving a quadratic inequality. The equality contained in $le$ or $ge$ means the roots must be included.
- ignore
  - _why:_ The roots cannot be ignored because they mark the boundaries between sign regions. With $le$ or $ge$, equality is allowed, so the roots are included.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it incorrect to write $x -2$ as one combined inequality?

**Back:** They represent two separate regions on the number line; they must be joined by "or".

**Choices:**

- They represent two separate regions on the number line; they must be joined by "or". ✔
  - _why:_ The inequalities describe values on opposite outer sides of two boundaries. A value can satisfy either region, so the two conditions are connected using “or”.
- They represent one continuous region on the number line; they must be joined by "and".
  - _why:_ This mistakes two outward-pointing inequalities for a single interval. The regions $x-2$ are separate and must be connected using “or”.
- They represent two separate regions on the number line; they must be joined by "and".
  - _why:_ Using “and” would require one value to be less than $-5$ and greater than $-2$ simultaneously, which is impossible. Separate alternative regions require “or”.
- They represent the same single region on the number line; they must be joined by "or".
  - _why:_ Although “or” is the correct connector, the regions are not the same: one lies below $-5$ and the other above $-2$. They are two separate regions.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What must you do to the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number?

**Back:** Reverse the direction of the sign.

**Choices:**

- Reverse the direction of the sign. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplication or division by a negative number reverses the order of the two quantities, so the inequality sign must point in the opposite direction.
- Keep the direction of the sign.
  - _why:_ Keeping the sign is appropriate only when multiplying or dividing by a positive number. With a negative multiplier or divisor, the inequality sign must be reversed.
- Remove the direction of the sign.
  - _why:_ An inequality sign cannot be removed during this operation. Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number requires reversing the sign’s direction.
- Square both sides of the sign.
  - _why:_ Squaring is not the required rule and can change which values satisfy an inequality. The correct step is to reverse the inequality sign.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To solve a quadratic inequality, first rearrange it into [ ___ ] (where one side is zero).

**Back:** To solve a quadratic inequality, first rearrange it into standard form (where one side is zero).

**Choices:**

- standard form ✔
  - _why:_ Standard form places all terms on one side, giving an inequality such as $ax^2+bx+c>0$. This allows the roots and sign regions to be identified.
- linear form
  - _why:_ A quadratic expression cannot generally be rewritten as a linear one without losing the $x^2$ term. It should first be rearranged into standard form.
- factorised form
  - _why:_ Factorised form can be useful later, but it is not always available. First rearrange into standard form with zero on one side.
- completed-square form
  - _why:_ Completed-square form is one possible solving method, but it is not the initial required arrangement. The inequality should first be put into standard form.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Solve $x^2 \le 64$.

**Back:** $$-8 \le x \le 8$

**Choices:**

- $$-8 \le x \le 8$ ✔
  - _why:_ The condition $x^2\le64$ means $|x|\le8$, so $x$ can be any value from $-8$ to $8$, including both endpoints.
- $$x \le -8$ or $x \ge 8$
  - _why:_ This selects values whose squares are at least $64$, reversing the required region. For $x^2\le64$, the solution lies between the roots: $-8\le x\le8$.
- $$0 \le x \le 8$
  - _why:_ Restricting the solution to non-negative values ignores numbers such as $-5$, whose square is also below $64$. The full solution is $-8\le x\le8$.
- $$-64 \le x \le 64$
  - _why:_ This uses $64$ itself as each boundary instead of taking its square roots. Since $\sqrt{64}=8$, the correct interval is $-8\le x\le8$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** Solving $x^2 \le 64$ by writing only $x \le 8$ is a mistake because it ignores [ ___ ] that also square to 64.

**Back:** Solving $x^2 \le 64$ by writing only $x \le 8$ is a mistake because it ignores negative values that also square to 64.

**Choices:**

- negative values ✔
  - _why:_ Both positive and negative numbers can have the same positive square. Values between $-8$ and $0$ must therefore be included alongside values between $0$ and $8$.
- fractional values
  - _why:_ Fractions are included in the interval, but they are not what the one-sided answer specifically misses. It ignores negative values, which can also square to positive numbers.
- values above 8
  - _why:_ Values above $8$ have squares greater than $64$, so they do not satisfy the inequality. The omitted valid solutions are the negative values down to $-8$.
- imaginary values
  - _why:_ This inequality is being solved over the real numbers, so imaginary values are irrelevant. The mistake is failing to include negative values whose squares are at most $64$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you solve a quadratic inequality that cannot be factorised?

**Back:** Use the quadratic formula to find the roots.Determine the region (inside/outside).Estimate values if integers are required.

**Choices:**

- Use the quadratic formula to find the roots.Determine the region (inside/outside).Estimate values if integers are required. ✔
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula finds roots even when factorisation is unavailable. The roots divide the number line into sign regions, after which any required integer solutions can be identified.
- Use the linear formula to find the roots.Determine the region (inside/outside).Estimate values if integers are required.
  - _why:_ A linear method cannot account for the $x^2$ term and will not find the quadratic’s roots. Use the quadratic formula, then select the appropriate sign region.
- Use the discriminant to find the roots.Determine the region (inside/outside).Round every root to the nearest integer.
  - _why:_ The discriminant indicates how many real roots exist but does not give their values by itself. Use the quadratic formula, and test integer boundaries rather than simply rounding roots.
- Use the constant term to find the roots.Select the region between the roots.Include every endpoint automatically.
  - _why:_ The constant term alone does not determine the roots. The required region may be inside or outside, and endpoints are included only for non-strict inequality signs.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Which integers satisfy $1.59 < x < 4.41$?

**Back:** 2, 3, and 4.

**Choices:**

- 2, 3, and 4. ✔
  - _why:_ The integers strictly greater than $1.59$ and strictly less than $4.41$ are $2$, $3$, and $4$. Neither decimal boundary is an integer.
- 1, 2, 3, and 4.
  - _why:_ Including $1$ comes from rounding the lower boundary down, but $1$ is not greater than $1.59$. The satisfying integers are $2$, $3$, and $4$.
- 2, 3, 4, and 5.
  - _why:_ Including $5$ comes from rounding the upper boundary up, but $5$ is not less than $4.41$. Only $2$, $3$, and $4$ satisfy both bounds.
- 2 and 3.
  - _why:_ Omitting $4$ incorrectly rounds the upper boundary down and excludes a valid integer. Since $4<4.41$, the solutions are $2$, $3$, and $4$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean if a quadratic has no real roots in the context of an inequality?

**Back:** The graph never crosses the x-axis; it is either always positive or always negative.

**Choices:**

- The graph never crosses the x-axis; it is either always positive or always negative. ✔
  - _why:_ With no real roots, the quadratic graph has no x-intercepts. Because it is continuous, it remains entirely above or entirely below the x-axis.
- The graph touches the x-axis once; it changes from positive to negative there.
  - _why:_ Touching the x-axis once describes a repeated real root, not no real roots, and the sign does not change there. With no roots, the graph never meets the x-axis.
- The graph crosses the x-axis twice; it is positive between the two roots.
  - _why:_ Crossing twice means the quadratic has two distinct real roots. With no real roots, it never crosses the x-axis and keeps the same sign for all real $x$.
- The graph never crosses the x-axis; it must always have positive values.
  - _why:_ No real roots means the sign is constant, but it does not guarantee that the graph is positive. It may lie entirely above or entirely below the x-axis.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you check a quadratic with no roots to see if it satisfies $f(x) > 0$?

**Back:** Test a single value (e.g., $x=0$); if it works, the inequality is true for all values of $x$.

**Choices:**

- Test a single value (e.g., $x=0$); if it works, the inequality is true for all values of $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic with no real roots cannot change sign because it never crosses the x-axis. If one tested value is positive, every real input gives a positive result.
- Test a single value (e.g., $x=0$); if it works, the inequality is true for positive values of $x$.
  - _why:_ The conclusion is unnecessarily restricted to positive inputs. Since a quadratic with no roots has a constant sign, one positive result establishes $f(x)>0$ for all real $x$.
- Find both real roots and test between them; if it works, the inequality is true for all values of $x$.
  - _why:_ A quadratic described as having no real roots has no real boundaries to find or test between. Test one input; its sign applies for all real values of $x$.
- Test several values (e.g., $x=0$); if they work, the inequality is true for those values of $x$ only.
  - _why:_ Testing several isolated values misses the significance of having no roots. The graph cannot change sign, so one positive test value proves the inequality for all real $x$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** State the three main steps to solve a quadratic inequality in an exam.

**Back:** Rearrange so one side is zero.Find the roots (boundary values).Identify the correct region (inside/outside).

**Choices:**

- Rearrange so one side is zero.Find the roots (boundary values).Identify the correct region (inside/outside). ✔
  - _why:_ The roots divide the number line into regions where the quadratic has different signs. After rearranging to zero, select the region that satisfies the original inequality.
- Rearrange so one side is zero.Find the turning point of the graph.Identify the correct region (inside/outside).
  - _why:_ The turning point does not usually provide the boundary values of the solution. After rearranging to zero, find the roots and then identify which region satisfies the inequality.
- Rearrange so $x^2$ is positive.Find the roots (boundary values).Always select the inside region.
  - _why:_ The solution is not always between the roots; it depends on the inequality sign and leading coefficient. Rearrange to zero, find the roots, then determine the correct inside or outside region.
- Rearrange so one side is zero.Find the axis of symmetry.Use it as the only boundary.
  - _why:_ The axis of symmetry is not generally a boundary of an inequality solution. The roots are the boundary values, and testing the resulting regions identifies the solution.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Solve $(x-2)(x-6) < 0$ by testing regions.

**Back:** $$2 < x < 6$

**Choices:**

- $$2 < x < 6$ ✔
  - _why:_ The factors have opposite signs between the roots 2 and 6, making their product negative. The strict inequality excludes both boundary values.
- $$x 6$
  - _why:_ Outside the roots, both factors have the same sign, so their product is positive. The product is negative only between the roots, giving $2 < x < 6$.
- $$2 \le x \le 6$
  - _why:_ Including 2 and 6 treats the inequality as non-strict. At either root the product equals zero, so the strict inequality gives $2 < x < 6$.
- $$-6 < x < -2$
  - _why:_ Negating the roots is a sign error: $(x-2)=0$ gives $x=2$, while $(x-6)=0$ gives $x=6$. The negative region is $2 < x < 6$.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** The coefficient of $x^2$ is called the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The coefficient of $x^2$ is called the leading coefficient.

**Choices:**

- leading coefficient ✔
  - _why:_ The leading coefficient is the coefficient of the highest-power term. In a quadratic expression, the highest power is $x^2$, so its coefficient is the leading coefficient.
- constant term
  - _why:_ The constant term is the term containing no variable, not the coefficient of $x^2$. In a quadratic, the coefficient of the highest-power term is the leading coefficient.
- linear coefficient
  - _why:_ The linear coefficient is the coefficient of the $x$ term, whose power is one. The coefficient of the quadratic $x^2$ term is the leading coefficient.
- independent variable
  - _why:_ The independent variable is the input variable, usually $x$, rather than a numerical coefficient. The coefficient multiplying $x^2$ is called the leading coefficient.
