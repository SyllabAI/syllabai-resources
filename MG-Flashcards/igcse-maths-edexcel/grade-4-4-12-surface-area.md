## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** Surface area is the total area of [ ___ ] of a 3D shape, measured in [ ___ ].

**Back:** Surface area is the total area of all outside faces of a 3D shape, measured in square units.

**Choices:**

- Surface area is the total area of all outside faces of a 3D shape, measured in square units. ✔
  - _why:_ Surface area combines the areas of every exterior face of a 3D shape. Because area is two-dimensional, it is expressed in square units, such as cm².
- Surface area is the total area of all visible faces of a 3D shape, measured in square units.
  - _why:_ A perspective drawing may hide exterior faces such as the back or bottom. Surface area includes all outside faces, not only visible ones, and is measured in square units.
- Surface area is the total area of all outside faces of a 3D shape, measured in cubic units.
  - _why:_ Cubic units measure volume, which is the space inside a solid. Surface area covers all outside faces and must therefore be measured in square units.
- Surface area is the total area of all inside faces of a 3D shape, measured in linear units.
  - _why:_ This confuses surface area with interior surfaces and length measurement. Surface area totals all outside faces and uses square units because each face has two dimensions.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a net help you do for surface area?

**Back:** Add the areas of all outside faces.

**Choices:**

- Add the areas of all outside faces. ✔
  - _why:_ A net lays every exterior face flat, making each face easy to identify and measure. Adding their individual areas gives the total surface area.
- Add the areas of only visible faces.
  - _why:_ Counting only visible faces omits exterior faces hidden in a 3D drawing. A net helps you add the areas of all outside faces.
- Multiply the areas of all outside faces.
  - _why:_ Surface area is a total, so the separate face areas must be added rather than multiplied. A net displays all outside faces for this addition.
- Add the lengths of all outside edges.
  - _why:_ Adding edge lengths calculates a total length, not an area. A net is used to find and add the areas of all outside faces.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** For a cuboid, which matching face pairs are included?

**Back:** Front/backTop/bottomLeft/right

**Choices:**

- Front/backTop/bottomLeft/right ✔
  - _why:_ Opposite faces of a cuboid are congruent rectangles. The front matches the back, the top matches the bottom, and the left matches the right.
- Front/topBack/bottomLeft/right
  - _why:_ Front and top faces are adjacent and usually have different dimensions, as are back and bottom. The matching pairs are front/back, top/bottom and left/right.
- Front/leftBack/rightTop/bottom
  - _why:_ This pairs adjacent side faces rather than opposite congruent faces. A cuboid’s matching pairs are front/back, top/bottom and left/right.
- Front/bottomTop/rightBack/left
  - _why:_ Each listed pair contains adjacent faces, which do not generally share the same dimensions. Matching cuboid faces are the three opposite pairs: front/back, top/bottom and left/right.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a cuboid, surface area = [ ___ ].

**Back:** For a cuboid, surface area = $2(lw+lh+wh)$.

**Choices:**

- $$2(lw+lh+wh)$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cuboid has two faces of each rectangular area: $lw$, $lh$ and $wh$. Adding both of every pair gives $2(lw+lh+wh)$.
- $$lw+lh+wh$
  - _why:_ This includes only one face from each matching pair, so it gives half the surface area. Doubling the sum gives $2(lw+lh+wh)$.
- $$2(l+w+h)$
  - _why:_ This adds lengths and resembles a perimeter calculation, so it cannot produce square units. Multiplying dimension pairs gives the correct surface area $2(lw+lh+wh)$.
- $$lwh$
  - _why:_ Multiplying all three dimensions calculates the cuboid’s volume in cubic units. Surface area is found from the six faces using $2(lw+lh+wh)$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is missed if you only add the visible faces in a 3D diagram?

**Back:** Hidden outside faces, such as the back or bottom.

**Choices:**

- Hidden outside faces, such as the back or bottom. ✔
  - _why:_ A 3D drawing often conceals exterior faces behind or beneath the solid. These hidden outside faces still contribute to the total surface area.
- Visible outside faces, such as the front or top.
  - _why:_ The visible faces are precisely the ones already included by this method. What gets missed are hidden outside faces, commonly the back or bottom.
- Interior measurements, such as the length or width.
  - _why:_ Length and width are dimensions used to calculate face areas, not overlooked faces. Adding only visible faces misses hidden outside faces such as the back or bottom.
- Hidden inside faces, such as the centre or middle.
  - _why:_ An ordinary solid does not have faces at its centre or middle. The omitted parts are hidden outside faces, such as the back or bottom.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** Triangle area = [ ___ ].

**Back:** Triangle area = $\frac{1}{2}\times \text{base}\times \text{height}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2}\times \text{base}\times \text{height}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A triangle with a given base and perpendicular height has half the area of the corresponding rectangle, so its area is $\frac{1}{2}\times \text{base}\times \text{height}$.
- $$\text{base}\times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ This calculates the area of the corresponding rectangle and makes the triangle’s area twice too large. The triangle requires the factor $\frac{1}{2}$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\times(\text{base}+\text{height})$
  - _why:_ Adding base and height produces a length rather than an area. The two perpendicular measurements must be multiplied, then halved: $\frac{1}{2}\times \text{base}\times \text{height}$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\times \text{base}\times \text{side}$
  - _why:_ Using a sloping side instead of the perpendicular height is a common mistake. Triangle area uses the base and its perpendicular height, multiplied and halved.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** Trapezium area = [ ___ ].

**Back:** Trapezium area = $\frac{1}{2}\times(\text{sum of parallel sides})\times \text{height}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2}\times(\text{sum of parallel sides})\times \text{height}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A trapezium’s area equals the average length of its two parallel sides multiplied by the perpendicular height, giving half their sum times the height.
- $$(\text{sum of parallel sides})\times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ This uses the sum rather than the average of the parallel sides, making the area twice too large. The formula needs the factor $\frac{1}{2}$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\times(\text{product of parallel sides})\times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ The parallel side lengths must be added, not multiplied; multiplying them also gives the wrong dimensions. Halve their sum and multiply by the perpendicular height.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\times(\text{sum of non-parallel sides})\times \text{height}$
  - _why:_ The area formula uses the two parallel sides because their average determines the trapezium’s effective width. The non-parallel sides are not used in this formula.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** In a prism, what happens to the cross-section along its length?

**Back:** It stays the same all the way through.

**Choices:**

- It stays the same all the way through. ✔
  - _why:_ A prism has a constant cross-section perpendicular to its length. Its cross-section remains the same shape and size at every point along the prism.
- It gets steadily larger all the way through.
  - _why:_ A solid that steadily widens does not have the constant cross-section required of a prism. In a prism, the cross-section stays the same throughout its length.
- It gets steadily smaller all the way through.
  - _why:_ A tapering solid, such as a pyramid or cone, has changing cross-sections and is not a prism. A prism’s cross-section stays the same throughout.
- It changes shape and size all the way through.
  - _why:_ Changing shape or size contradicts the defining property of a prism. Its cross-section must stay the same shape and size all the way through.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** For any prism, surface area = [ ___ ], where $P$ is the [ ___ ].

**Back:** For any prism, surface area = $2A+PL$, where $P$ is the perimeter of the cross-section.

**Choices:**

- For any prism, surface area = $2A+PL$, where $P$ is the perimeter of the cross-section. ✔
  - _why:_ The two identical cross-sections have total area $2A$. The rectangular side faces have combined area $PL$, because their total width is the cross-section’s perimeter.
- For any prism, surface area = $A+PL$, where $P$ is the perimeter of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ Using $A$ counts only one end face. A prism has two identical cross-sections, so their combined area is $2A$ and the correct formula is $2A+PL$.
- For any prism, surface area = $2A+PL$, where $P$ is the area of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ This confuses perimeter with area. In $PL$, $P$ must be the perimeter of the cross-section so that multiplying it by length gives the total side area.
- For any prism, surface area = $2A+2PL$, where $P$ is the area of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ This both doubles the side area and misidentifies $P$. The side rectangles total $PL$ once, and $P$ represents the perimeter of the cross-section, giving $2A+PL$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Why does $PL$ give the side-rectangle area of a prism?

**Back:** $P$ is the total width around the cross-section.$L$ is the prism length.

**Choices:**

- $P$ is the total width around the cross-section.$L$ is the prism length. ✔
  - _why:_ Unfolding the side faces produces rectangles whose combined width is the cross-section’s perimeter $P$. Their common length is $L$, so their total area is $P\times L$.
- $P$ is the total area of the cross-section.$L$ is the prism length.
  - _why:_ This confuses perimeter with area. $P$ is the total distance around the cross-section, and multiplying that combined width by the prism length $L$ gives the side area.
- $P$ is the total width around the cross-section.$L$ is the cross-section area.
  - _why:_ This misidentifies $L$ as an area. $L$ is the prism’s length, while $P$ is the total width around its cross-section, so $PL$ measures area.
- $P$ is the width of one side face.$L$ is the prism length.
  - _why:_ Using one face’s width would calculate only that face’s area. $P$ combines all widths around the cross-section, so $PL$ gives the area of every side rectangle.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Why does a triangular prism have 3 rectangular side faces?

**Back:** Each of the triangle’s 3 sides forms one rectangle.

**Choices:**

- Each of the triangle’s 3 sides forms one rectangle. ✔
  - _why:_ Each side of the triangular cross-section extends along the prism’s length to create one rectangular face. Since a triangle has three sides, there are three rectangles.
- Each of the triangle’s 2 sides forms one rectangle.
  - _why:_ This overlooks one side of the triangular cross-section. All three sides extend along the prism’s length, and each produces one rectangular side face.
- Each of the triangle’s 3 vertices forms one rectangle.
  - _why:_ This confuses vertices with edges. Rectangular faces are formed when the triangle’s three sides extend along the prism’s length, not when its vertices are counted.
- Each of the triangle’s 2 end faces forms one rectangle.
  - _why:_ The two end faces are triangles, not rectangles. The three rectangular side faces come from extending each of the triangle’s three sides along the prism’s length.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** In a trapezium prism, what is the trapezium area used for?

**Back:** The two identical end faces.

**Choices:**

- The two identical end faces. ✔
  - _why:_ The trapezium is the prism’s constant cross-section, appearing at both ends. Its area is therefore calculated twice when finding the prism’s total surface area.
- The four rectangular side faces.
  - _why:_ The side faces are rectangles formed by extending the trapezium’s sides along the prism. The trapezium area is used for the two identical end faces instead.
- The perimeter of the cross-section.
  - _why:_ Area and perimeter are different measurements. The trapezium area gives the two end-face areas, while its side lengths are added separately to find the cross-section’s perimeter.
- The length of the prism.
  - _why:_ The prism length is a separate linear measurement used to find rectangular side areas. The trapezium area represents each of the two identical end faces.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** In a trapezium, when is the perpendicular height included in perimeter?

**Back:** Only if it is an outside side length.

**Choices:**

- Only if it is an outside side length. ✔
  - _why:_ Perimeter includes only lengths forming the outside boundary. A perpendicular height is included only when it is also an actual outside side of the trapezium.
- Always, because it is an important measurement.
  - _why:_ The height is needed for the trapezium’s area formula, but that does not automatically place it on the perimeter. It is included only when it is an outside side length.
- Only if it is an internal vertical line.
  - _why:_ Internal construction lines are not part of a shape’s perimeter. The perpendicular height is counted only when it lies on the trapezium’s outside boundary as a side.
- Only if it is a parallel side length.
  - _why:_ A perpendicular height cannot be one of the trapezium’s parallel sides. It contributes to perimeter only if it is an outside side length, whether vertical or sloping.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the area of an L-shaped cross-section?

**Back:** Split into rectangles and add their areas.

**Choices:**

- Split into rectangles and add their areas. ✔
  - _why:_ An L-shape can be divided into non-overlapping rectangles. Calculate each rectangle’s area using length multiplied by width, then add the results for the total cross-sectional area.
- Multiply the perimeter by the prism length.
  - _why:_ Perimeter multiplied by prism length gives the area of the prism’s rectangular side faces, not the cross-section. Split the L-shape into rectangles and add their areas.
- Treat it as one large rectangle without subtraction.
  - _why:_ This includes the missing corner and therefore overestimates the area. The L-shape should be split into rectangles and their non-overlapping areas added together.
- Add all the outside side lengths together.
  - _why:_ Adding outside side lengths finds the perimeter, not the area. To find the L-shaped cross-section’s area, split it into rectangles and add their individual areas.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** For an L-shaped cross-section, which lines are excluded from the perimeter?

**Back:** Internal split lines.

**Choices:**

- Internal split lines. ✔
  - _why:_ Split lines are drawn only to divide the L-shape into simpler rectangles. They lie inside the shape, so they are not part of its outside boundary or perimeter.
- Outside horizontal lines.
  - _why:_ Horizontal direction does not exclude a line from the perimeter. Every segment on the outside boundary is counted; only internal split lines used to divide the shape are excluded.
- Outside vertical lines.
  - _why:_ Vertical outside edges still form part of the boundary and must be counted. The lines excluded from the perimeter are internal split lines drawn within the L-shape.
- The shortest boundary lines.
  - _why:_ Boundary length does not determine whether a segment is included. All outside edges, however short, contribute to perimeter; only internal split lines are left out.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** To convert cm² to m², divide by what?

**Back:** 10,000.

**Choices:**

- 10,000. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}$, squaring the conversion gives $1\text{ m}^2=10{,}000\text{ cm}^2$. Therefore, divide a value in cm² by 10,000.
- 100.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 100 uses the linear centimetre-to-metre conversion without squaring it. Area conversion requires $100^2=10{,}000$, so cm² must be divided by 10,000.
- 1,000.
  - _why:_ This treats the conversion as three factors of ten, which does not match the area scale. Because $1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}$, divide cm² by $100^2=10{,}000$.
- 1,000,000.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 1,000,000 applies the cubic conversion used for cm³ to m³. For square units, square 100, so cm² is divided by 10,000.

**Back:** $0.3\text{ m}$.

**Choices:**

- $0.3\text{ m}$. ✔

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a closed cylinder, surface area = [ ___ ].

**Back:** For a closed cylinder, surface area = $2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$.

**Choices:**

- $$2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$ ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has two circular ends, contributing $2\pi r^2$, and one curved surface, contributing $2\pi rh$. Adding these gives the total surface area.
- $$2\pi r^2+\pi rh$
  - _why:_ The curved surface has circumference $2\pi r$ multiplied by height $h$, so its area is $2\pi rh$, not half that amount. The complete formula is $2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$.
- $$\pi r^2+2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This counts only one circular end, but a closed cylinder has two. The two ends contribute $2\pi r^2$, making the full formula $2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$.
- $$2\pi r^2+2\pi h$
  - _why:_ The curved area must include the radius in the circumference, giving $2\pi rh$. Omitting $r$ loses a required length factor, so the correct formula is $2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the curved side area of a cylinder $2\pi rh$?

**Back:** Circumference $2\pi r$ multiplied by height $h$.

**Choices:**

- Circumference $2\pi r$ multiplied by height $h$. ✔
  - _why:_ Unwrapping the curved surface produces a rectangle. Its width is the cylinder’s circumference, $2\pi r$, and its height is $h$, so the area is $2\pi rh$.
- Diameter $2r$ multiplied by height $h$.
  - _why:_ The rectangle formed by unwrapping the curved surface has width equal to the full circumference, not the diameter. Using circumference $2\pi r$ gives the correct area $2\pi rh$.
- Area $\pi r^2$ multiplied by height $h$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying the circular area by height gives a volume-related expression, not the area of the curved surface. The curved side unwraps into a rectangle with width $2\pi r$ and height $h$.
- Circumference $2\pi r$ multiplied by radius $h$.
  - _why:_ The second dimension of the unwrapped rectangle is the cylinder’s height, not its radius. Therefore the curved area uses circumference $2\pi r$ multiplied by height $h$.
