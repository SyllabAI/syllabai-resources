## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In algebra, what is a coefficient?

**Back:** The number multiplying a variable (e.g., 4 in $4x$).

**Choices:**

- The number multiplying a variable (e.g., 4 in $4x$). ✔
  - _why:_ A coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable. In $4x$, the variable $x$ is multiplied by 4, so 4 is its coefficient.
- The number added to a variable (e.g., 4 in $x+4$).
  - _why:_ A number added to a variable is a constant term, not a coefficient. A coefficient multiplies the variable, such as 4 in $4x$.
- The power applied to a variable (e.g., 4 in $x^4$).
  - _why:_ The raised number in $x^4$ is an exponent or power, not a coefficient. A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable, such as 4 in $4x$.
- The letter representing an unknown (e.g., $x$ in $4x$).
  - _why:_ The letter $x$ is the variable, not the coefficient. The coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying that variable, which is 4 in $4x$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the most common mistake when expanding two brackets like $(x+4)(x-3)$?

**Back:** Only multiplying the first and last terms, missing the middle terms ($-3x$ and $4x$).

**Choices:**

- Only multiplying the first and last terms, missing the middle terms ($-3x$ and $4x$). ✔
  - _why:_ Every term in the first bracket must multiply every term in the second. Using only the first and last products omits $-3x$ and $4x$.
- Only adding the first and last terms, missing the constant terms ($4$ and $-3$).
  - _why:_ Expansion requires multiplication, not addition, and 4 and $-3$ are already visible constants. The classic error is multiplying only first and last terms, omitting $-3x$ and $4x$.
- Only multiplying the middle and last terms, missing the outside terms ($x^2$ and $-12$).
  - _why:_ Although omitting products is always an error, the standard misconception is to multiply only the first terms and last terms, thereby missing the two middle products $-3x$ and $4x$.
- Only simplifying the first and last terms, combining the middle terms ($-3x$ and $4x$).
  - _why:_ Combining $-3x$ and $4x$ after expansion is valid because they are like terms. The common mistake occurs earlier: multiplying only first and last terms and omitting both middle products.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What type of expression is usually produced when multiplying three linear brackets?

**Back:** A cubic expression (highest power is $x^3$).

**Choices:**

- A cubic expression (highest power is $x^3$). ✔
  - _why:_ Selecting the $x$ term from each of three linear brackets gives $x\cdot x\cdot x=x^3$, so their product is normally a cubic expression.
- A quadratic expression (highest power is $x^2$).
  - _why:_ A quadratic usually results from multiplying two linear brackets. Three linear brackets contribute three factors of $x$, giving a highest power of $x^3$, so the result is cubic.
- A quartic expression (highest power is $x^4$).
  - _why:_ A quartic would usually require four linear factors. Multiplying three linear brackets produces three factors of $x$, so the highest power is $x^3$ and the expression is cubic.
- A linear expression (highest power is $x$).
  - _why:_ A linear result has highest power $x$, but multiplying the leading terms from three linear brackets gives $x^3$. The resulting expression is therefore cubic.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the 3-step method for expanding triple brackets.

**Back:** Expand two brackets first.Simplify the resulting quadratic.Multiply the quadratic by the third bracket.

**Choices:**

- Expand two brackets first.Simplify the resulting quadratic.Multiply the quadratic by the third bracket. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying two linear brackets first produces a quadratic. After collecting its like terms, distribute each quadratic term across the remaining third bracket and simplify again.
- Add two brackets first.Simplify the resulting linear expression.Multiply the linear expression by the third bracket.
  - _why:_ The brackets are factors, so adding the first two changes the original expression. Expand two brackets by multiplication, simplify the quadratic, then multiply it by the third bracket.
- Expand two brackets first.Simplify the resulting quadratic.Add the quadratic to the third bracket.
  - _why:_ The final bracket is multiplied by the quadratic, not added to it. Expand two brackets, simplify their quadratic product, then distribute that quadratic across the third bracket.
- Expand one bracket first.Simplify the resulting linear expression.Multiply the linear expression by the other brackets.
  - _why:_ A single bracket cannot be expanded without another factor. First multiply two brackets to obtain a quadratic, simplify it, and then multiply that quadratic by the third bracket.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you determine the leading term ($x^3$) of $(2x+3)(x-1)(3x+2)$ without full expansion?

**Back:** Multiply the $x$ parts of all three brackets together: $2x \cdot x \cdot 3x = 6x^3$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the $x$ parts of all three brackets together: $2x \cdot x \cdot 3x = 6x^3$. ✔
  - _why:_ The highest-degree term comes from choosing the $x$ term in every bracket. Multiplying coefficients gives $2\cdot1\cdot3=6$, while $x\cdot x\cdot x=x^3$.
- Add the $x$ parts of all three brackets together: $2x + x + 3x = 6x$.
  - _why:_ The brackets are multiplied, so their leading terms must also be multiplied rather than added. This gives $2x\cdot x\cdot3x=6x^3$, the cubic leading term.
- Multiply the constant parts of all three brackets together: $3 \cdot -1 \cdot 2 = -6$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying the constants finds the constant term, not the leading term. The leading term comes from multiplying the three $x$ parts, giving $6x^3$.
- Multiply the $x$ parts of the first two brackets together: $2x \cdot x = 2x^2$.
  - _why:_ This stops after only two brackets and therefore finds the leading term of their quadratic product. Including the third bracket gives $2x\cdot x\cdot3x=6x^3$.

  - _why:_ An operation symbol cannot be added as a numerical term. Treat subtraction as adding a negative number, so a term such as $-3x$ keeps its sign during multiplication.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you rewrite a repeated bracket like $(x-2)^2$ before expanding?

**Back:** Write it as two identical brackets: $(x-2)(x-2)$.

**Choices:**

- Write it as two identical brackets: $(x-2)(x-2)$. ✔
  - _why:_ The power of $2$ means the entire bracket is multiplied by itself, so $(x-2)^2$ must be rewritten as $(x-2)(x-2)$ before expansion.
- Square each term separately: $x^2-2^2$.
  - _why:_ Squaring the terms separately ignores the cross-products that create the middle term. The bracket should be rewritten as $(x-2)(x-2)$ so every term is multiplied correctly.
- Write it as two opposite brackets: $(x-2)(x+2)$.
  - _why:_ Changing the sign creates a difference of two squares and represents a different expression. A repeated bracket must remain identical, giving $(x-2)(x-2)$.
- Multiply the bracket by the power: $2(x-2)$.
  - _why:_ An exponent indicates repeated multiplication, not multiplication by the exponent. Therefore, $(x-2)^2$ must be rewritten as $(x-2)(x-2)$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $(x-2)^2 = x^2 + 4$ incorrect?

**Back:** It squares terms individually instead of expanding $(x-2)(x-2)$, which misses the middle term ($-4x$).

**Choices:**

- It squares terms individually instead of expanding $(x-2)(x-2)$, which misses the middle term ($-4x$). ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding $(x-2)(x-2)$ produces $x^2-2x-2x+4=x^2-4x+4$. Squaring the terms individually omits the two cross-products that form $-4x$.
- It changes the subtraction to addition when expanding, so the constant term should be $-4$.
  - _why:_ The constants multiply to $(-2)(-2)=+4$, so the constant term is not the error. The actual mistake is omitting the middle term $-4x$.
- It squares the variable incorrectly, because the first term should remain $x$.
  - _why:_ The product $x \cdot x$ is correctly equal to $x^2$. The expression is wrong because it omits the cross-products $-2x-2x=-4x$.
- It expands the brackets correctly but writes the terms in the wrong order.
  - _why:_ Reordering terms cannot recover a missing term. Correct expansion gives $x^2-4x+4$, so $x^2+4$ is incomplete because the middle term $-4x$ is absent.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Back:** An equation that is true for every allowed value of the variable.

**Choices:**

- An equation that is true for every allowed value of the variable. ✔
  - _why:_ An identity remains true for every value for which its expressions are defined. Expanding or simplifying either side reveals that both sides represent the same expression.
- An equation that is true for one allowed value of the variable.
  - _why:_ An equation true only for particular values is an equation to solve, not an identity. An identity is true for every allowed value of the variable.
- An expression that has no variables and contains only constants.
  - _why:_ The presence or absence of variables does not define an identity. An identity is an equation whose two sides are equal for every allowed variable value.
- An equation that is false for every allowed value of the variable.
  - _why:_ An equation that never holds is a contradiction, not an identity. An identity is true for every allowed value of the variable.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** In what order should a final expanded expression usually be written?

**Back:** In descending powers of $x$: $x^3$, then $x^2$, then $x$, then the constant.

**Choices:**

- In descending powers of $x$: $x^3$, then $x^2$, then $x$, then the constant. ✔
  - _why:_ Standard algebraic form places the term with the highest exponent first and proceeds downwards. For a cubic, the usual order is $x^3$, $x^2$, $x$, then the constant.
- In ascending powers of $x$: the constant, then $x$, then $x^2$, then $x^3$.
  - _why:_ This is a possible mathematical arrangement but not the usual requested standard form. Expanded polynomials are normally written in descending powers: $x^3$, $x^2$, $x$, then the constant.
- In bracket order: terms from the first bracket, then the second, then the third.
  - _why:_ After expansion, terms are collected by their powers rather than retained according to their original brackets. The final expression should use descending powers of $x$.
- In coefficient order: the smallest coefficient first and the largest coefficient last.
  - _why:_ Polynomial terms are ordered by exponents, not by the sizes of their coefficients. A cubic is normally written as $x^3$, then $x^2$, then $x$, then the constant.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the constant term of $(x+3)(x+4)(x+2)$ using a shortcut?

**Back:** Multiply the numerical constants in each bracket: $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 24$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the numerical constants in each bracket: $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 24$. ✔
  - _why:_ A constant term is formed only by selecting the constant from every bracket. Their product is $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2=24$, so the expansion has constant term $24$.
- Add the numerical constants in each bracket: $3 + 4 + 2 = 9$.
  - _why:_ Adding the constants confuses multiplication of factors with collecting like terms. To form the constant term, select and multiply all three constants, giving $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2=24$.
- Multiply the first two constants only: $3 \cdot 4 = 12$.
  - _why:_ This omits the constant from the third bracket. The constant term requires one constant from every factor, so it is $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2=24$.
- Multiply the numerical constants and square the result: $(3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2)^2 = 576$.
  - _why:_ No square applies to the product of the constants, so squaring introduces an extra, unjustified operation. Multiplying the three constants once gives the constant term $24$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Define like terms.

**Back:** Terms that have the same variable part (e.g., $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$).

**Choices:**

- Terms that have the same variable part (e.g., $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ Like terms contain exactly the same variables raised to the same powers. Their numerical coefficients may differ, so $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$ are like terms.
- Terms that have the same coefficient (e.g., $3x$ and $3x^2$).
  - _why:_ Matching coefficients do not make terms like terms; the variable parts must match instead. For example, $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$ are like terms despite different coefficients.
- Terms that have the same total degree (e.g., $3x^2$ and $4xy$).
  - _why:_ Having the same total degree is insufficient because the variables and their powers may differ. Like terms must have exactly the same variable part, such as $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$.
- Terms that have the same variable (e.g., $3x$ and $-8x^2$).
  - _why:_ Sharing a variable is not enough when its power differs. Like terms require the same variables with the same powers, such as $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$.

  - _why:_ Different orders may produce different intermediate expressions, but correct expansion and collection of like terms give the same final answer. Multiplication is associative.
