## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is indicated by a dot placed above a single digit in a decimal?

**Back:** That digit repeats forever.

**Choices:**

- That digit repeats forever. ✔
  - _why:_ A dot above one digit marks that digit as recurring, so it continues indefinitely after the decimal point.
- That digit appears only once.
  - _why:_ A digit without recurring notation may appear only once. A dot above the digit instead indicates that it repeats forever.
- That digit must be rounded up.
  - _why:_ Rounding is determined by the digit to the right, not by a dot above a digit. The dot shows that the marked digit repeats forever.
- That digit has a place value of zero.
  - _why:_ The dot does not alter the digit’s place value or make it zero. It indicates that the marked digit repeats forever.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** How is a repeating block of digits (e.g. 0.3636...) shown using recurring notation?

**Back:** Dots are placed over the first and last digits of the repeating block.

**Choices:**

- Dots are placed over the first and last digits of the repeating block. ✔
  - _why:_ Marking the first and last digits identifies the boundaries of the entire repeating block, including every digit between the two dots.
- Dots are placed over the first and second digits of the repeating block.
  - _why:_ Using the first two digits does not show where a longer repeating block ends. Dots belong over the first and last digits of the block.
- Dots are placed over the middle and last digits of the repeating block.
  - _why:_ Starting the notation at a middle digit would omit part of the recurring block. The dots must mark its first and last digits.
- Dots are placed over the last two digits of the repeating block.
  - _why:_ Marking only the final two digits can falsely suggest that they alone recur. The first and last digits must be dotted to enclose the full block.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the notation $0.2\dot{4}\dot{9}$, only the digits [ ___ ] repeat.

**Back:** In the notation $0.2\dot{4}\dot{9}$, only the digits 4 and 9 repeat.

**Choices:**

- 4 and 9 ✔
  - _why:_ The dots over 4 and 9 mark the beginning and end of the recurring block, so the block 49 repeats while 2 does not.
- 2 and 4
  - _why:_ The digit 2 appears before the first dot and is therefore non-recurring. The dotted boundaries show that 4 and 9 form the repeating block.
- 2 and 9
  - _why:_ The dot above 9 does not mean that 2 also recurs. The two dots enclose 4 and 9 as the complete repeating block.
- 2, 4 and 9
  - _why:_ Including 2 mistakes the whole decimal part for the recurring block. Only the digits from the first dotted digit to the last dotted digit repeat, namely 4 and 9.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** When converting a fraction using long division, how do you know the decimal will repeat?

**Back:** When the same remainder appears for a second time.

**Choices:**

- When the same remainder appears for a second time. ✔
  - _why:_ A repeated remainder causes the subsequent division steps and quotient digits to repeat in the same cycle, producing a recurring decimal.
- When the same quotient digit appears for a second time.
  - _why:_ A quotient digit can occur twice without beginning a repeating cycle. Repetition is guaranteed when the same remainder appears again, because the division process then repeats.
- When a larger remainder appears for a second time.
  - _why:_ The size of a remainder does not determine whether the decimal recurs. The decimal repeats when exactly the same remainder appears for a second time.
- When a zero remainder appears for a second time.
  - _why:_ A zero remainder makes the division terminate rather than recur. A recurring decimal is identified when the same non-zero remainder appears again.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the core purpose of multiplying a recurring decimal by powers of 10 in algebra?

**Back:** To create two equations where the recurring tails are identical.

**Choices:**

- To create two equations where the recurring tails are identical. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by a suitable power of 10 shifts complete recurring blocks so the infinite tails align and can be eliminated by subtraction.
- To create two equations where the decimal points are identical.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 moves the decimal point rather than keeping it fixed. Its purpose is to make the recurring tails identical so subtraction removes them.
- To create two equations where the whole-number parts are identical.
  - _why:_ The whole-number parts normally become different after multiplication. The important feature is that the recurring tails become identical and therefore cancel under subtraction.
- To create two equations where the recurring blocks are different.
  - _why:_ Different recurring blocks would not cancel cleanly when the equations are subtracted. A suitable power of 10 is chosen to make the recurring tails identical.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is subtraction used after lining up the recurring tails of two equations?

**Back:** To cancel out the infinite repeating part of the decimal.

**Choices:**

- To cancel out the infinite repeating part of the decimal. ✔
  - _why:_ Once the recurring tails are aligned, subtracting one equation from the other removes every repeated decimal digit, leaving a finite equation that can be solved.
- To round off the infinite repeating part of the decimal.
  - _why:_ The method gives an exact fraction and does not use rounding. Subtraction cancels the identical infinite repeating tails completely.
- To double up the infinite repeating part of the decimal.
  - _why:_ Adding the equations would combine rather than eliminate their recurring tails. Subtraction is used because identical tails cancel out exactly.
- To shift left the infinite repeating part of the decimal.
  - _why:_ Multiplication by a power of 10 shifts the decimal digits. Subtraction has the different purpose of cancelling the aligned infinite repeating part.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the algebraic steps to prove $0.\dot{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.

**Back:** Let $x = 0.666\dots$Multiply by 10 ($10x = 6.666\dots$)Subtract ($9x = 6$) and simplify $x = \frac{6}{9}$

**Choices:**

- Let $x = 0.666\dots$Multiply by 10 ($10x = 6.666\dots$)Subtract ($9x = 6$) and simplify $x = \frac{6}{9}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 aligns the recurring tails. Subtracting the original equation gives $9x=6$, so $x=\frac{6}{9}=\frac{2}{3}$.
- Let $x = 0.666\dots$Multiply by 10 ($10x = 6.666\dots$)Subtract ($9x = 6$) and simplify $x = \frac{6}{10}$
  - _why:_ Dividing $9x=6$ by 10 is the slip; the coefficient of $x$ is 9. Dividing both sides by 9 gives $x=\frac{6}{9}=\frac{2}{3}$.
- Let $x = 0.666\dots$Multiply by 10 ($10x = 6.666\dots$)Subtract ($10x = 6$) and simplify $x = \frac{6}{10}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting $x$ from $10x$ gives $9x$, not $10x$. The correct equation is $9x=6$, which simplifies to $x=\frac{2}{3}$.
- Let $x = 0.666\dots$Multiply by 10 ($10x = 6.666\dots$)Subtract ($-9x = -6$) and simplify $x = -\frac{6}{9}$
  - _why:_ Dividing two negative quantities produces a positive result. From $-9x=-6$, the correct conclusion is $x=\frac{6}{9}=\frac{2}{3}$, not a negative fraction.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What constitutes a fraction in its simplest form?

**Back:** The numerator and denominator have no common factors other than 1.

**Choices:**

- The numerator and denominator have no common factors other than 1. ✔
  - _why:_ A fraction is fully simplified when no integer greater than 1 divides both its numerator and denominator, so no further cancellation is possible.
- The numerator and denominator have no common multiples other than 1.
  - _why:_ Simplification depends on common factors, not common multiples; non-zero integers have many common multiples. The numerator and denominator must share no factor greater than 1.
- The numerator and denominator are both prime numbers other than 1.
  - _why:_ The numerator and denominator do not both need to be prime; for example, $8/15$ is simplified. They only need to have no common factor greater than 1.
- The numerator and denominator have the same number of factors other than 1.
  - _why:_ The number of factors each value has is irrelevant to simplification. A fraction is simplest when the numerator and denominator have no common factor greater than 1.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** To convert $0.3\dot{8}$ to a fraction, which two multiples of $x$ should be compared to align the tails?

**Back:** $$100x$ and $10x$.

**Choices:**

- $$100x$ and $10x$. ✔
  - _why:_ There is one non-recurring digit and one recurring digit, so multiplying by 10 and 100 produces matching recurring tails that cancel when the equations are subtracted.
- $$10x$ and $x$.
  - _why:_ This comparison works when recurrence begins immediately after the decimal point. Here, the non-recurring 3 requires comparing $100x$ and $10x$ to align the recurring tails.
- $$1000x$ and $100x$.
  - _why:_ This shifts both decimals one place farther than necessary. Although the tails align, the standard minimal comparison is $100x$ and $10x$, based on one non-recurring and one recurring digit.
- $$100x$ and $x$.
  - _why:_ These multipliers differ by two decimal places, but the recurring block contains only one digit. Comparing $100x$ and $10x$ gives identical recurring tails for subtraction.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To align tails for a decimal with $n$ non-recurring digits and a block of $r$ recurring digits, compare $\text{[...]}$ and $10^n x$.

**Back:** To align tails for a decimal with $n$ non-recurring digits and a block of $r$ recurring digits, compare $\boxed{10^{n+r}x}$ and $10^n x$.

**Choices:**

- $$10^{n+r}x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $10^{n+r}$ shifts past both the $n$ non-recurring digits and one complete recurring block, allowing its tail to align with that of $10^n x$.
- $$10^r x$
  - _why:_ This accounts only for the recurring block and ignores the $n$ non-recurring digits. The required larger multiple is $10^{n+r}x$, which shifts past both sections.
- $$10^{nr}x$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $n$ and $r$ is a common exponent error. The shifts for the non-recurring digits and recurring block are added, so the correct expression is $10^{n+r}x$.
- $$10^{n-r}x$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the block length reduces the shift rather than moving past both sections. Their lengths must be added, making the correct larger multiple $10^{n+r}x$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** To align tails for a decimal with $n$ non-recurring digits and a block of $r$ recurring digits, compare $10^{n+r}x$ and $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To align tails for a decimal with $n$ non-recurring digits and a block of $r$ recurring digits, compare $10^{n+r}x$ and $\boxed{10^n x}$.

**Choices:**

- $$10^n x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $10^n$ moves the non-recurring digits before the decimal point. Its recurring tail then aligns with the tail produced by multiplying by $10^{n+r}$.
- $$10^r x$
  - _why:_ This uses the recurring block length rather than the number of non-recurring digits. The smaller multiple must be $10^n x$ so that the recurring tails align.
- $$10^{n-r}x$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the recurring length from the non-recurring length does not identify the starting point of recurrence. The correct smaller multiple is $10^n x$.
- $$10^{n+r-1}x$
  - _why:_ The off-by-one exponent shifts too far unless the recurring block happens to have length one. The general smaller multiple is $10^n x$, determined only by the non-recurring digits.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** For the decimal $0.\dot{4}\dot{7}$, why is the decimal multiplied by 100 instead of 10?

**Back:** The repeating block has 2 digits; multiplying by 100 shifts the tail to match.

**Choices:**

- The repeating block has 2 digits; multiplying by 100 shifts the tail to match. ✔
  - _why:_ The block 47 contains two digits, so multiplication by $10^2=100$ moves one full block before the decimal point and leaves the same recurring tail.
- The repeating block has 1 digit; multiplying by 100 shifts the tail to match.
  - _why:_ Treating 47 as a one-digit block misreads the recurrence notation. Both 4 and 7 repeat, so the block has 2 digits and requires multiplication by 100.
- The repeating block has 2 digits; multiplying by 10 shifts the tail to match.
  - _why:_ Multiplication by 10 shifts only one digit, reversing the order of the recurring tail. A two-digit block requires multiplication by 100 to reproduce the same tail.
- The repeating block has 100 digits; multiplying by 100 shifts the tail to match.
  - _why:_ The multiplier does not equal the number of repeating digits. The block has 2 digits, and $100=10^2$ shifts exactly those two digits to align the tail.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Is it necessary to separate the whole number (e.g., the 3 in $3.\dot{2}$) before using the algebra trick?

**Back:** No; let $x$ equal the entire decimal and use the standard method.

**Choices:**

- No; let $x$ equal the entire decimal and use the standard method. ✔
  - _why:_ The algebraic subtraction method works with the whole decimal, including its integer part. Setting $x=3.222\ldots$ and shifting the complete value still cancels the recurring tails correctly.
- Yes; convert the whole number into a recurring decimal before using the standard method.
  - _why:_ The integer part does not need to be rewritten as recurring. Let $x$ equal the entire decimal and apply the usual powers-of-ten multiplication and subtraction method directly.
- Yes; remove the whole number and discard it before using the standard method.
  - _why:_ Discarding the whole number changes the value and produces only the fractional part. The correct method lets $x$ equal the entire decimal, preserving both integer and recurring parts.
- No; let $x$ equal only the recurring digit and use the standard method.
  - _why:_ Setting $x$ equal only to a digit does not represent the original number. Instead, let $x$ equal the entire decimal and perform the standard shifting and subtraction steps.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** The final answer for a decimal greater than 1 (like $3.2\dot{6}\dot{3}$) is often an [ ___ ].

**Back:** The final answer for a decimal greater than 1 (like $3.2\dot{6}\dot{3}$) is often an improper fraction.

**Choices:**

- improper fraction ✔
  - _why:_ A decimal greater than 1 has a fractional representation whose numerator is larger than its denominator when written as a single fraction, so it is often an improper fraction.
- proper fraction
  - _why:_ A proper fraction is less than 1 because its numerator is smaller than its denominator. A decimal such as $3.2\dot{6}\dot{3}$ is greater than 1, so it gives an improper fraction.
- unit fraction
  - _why:_ A unit fraction has numerator 1 and is necessarily at most 1. A recurring decimal greater than 1 is instead commonly expressed as an improper fraction.
- integer
  - _why:_ A recurring decimal with a non-zero fractional part is not a whole number. When converted into one fraction, a value greater than 1 is generally an improper fraction.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the resulting fraction for the recurring digit $a$ in the decimal $0.\dot{a}$?

**Back:** $$\frac{a}{9}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{a}{9}$ ✔
  - _why:_ If $x=0.aaa\ldots$, then $10x=a.aaa\ldots$. Subtracting gives $9x=a$, so $x=\frac{a}{9}$.
- $$\frac{a}{10}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10 represents the terminating decimal $0.a$, not the recurring decimal $0.aaa\ldots$. Subtraction of $10x-x$ gives $9x=a$, so the fraction is $\frac{a}{9}$.
- $$\frac{a}{99}$
  - _why:_ A denominator of 99 corresponds to a two-digit repeating block. Here only one digit repeats, so subtracting $x$ from $10x$ gives the correct result $\frac{a}{9}$.
- $$\frac{9}{a}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the numerator and denominator after solving $9x=a$. Dividing both sides by 9 gives $x=\frac{a}{9}$, not its reciprocal.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the resulting fraction for the repeating block in $0.\dot{0}\dot{a}$?

**Back:** $$\frac{a}{99}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{a}{99}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The repeating block is $0a$, which has two digits. If $x=0.0a0a\ldots$, then $100x=0a.0a\ldots$, giving $99x=a$ and therefore $x=\frac{a}{99}$.
- $$\frac{a}{9}$
  - _why:_ A denominator of 9 applies to a one-digit repeating block. Here the repeating block is $0a$, so multiplying by 100 and subtracting gives $99x=a$ and $x=\frac{a}{99}$.
- $$\frac{a}{90}$
  - _why:_ This would fit a non-recurring zero followed by recurring $a$, written with only $a$ recurring. Both 0 and $a$ are marked as recurring here, so the fraction is $\frac{a}{99}$.
- $$\frac{a}{100}$
  - _why:_ A denominator of 100 represents the terminating decimal $0.0a$. Because the two-digit block $0a$ repeats indefinitely, subtraction produces a denominator of 99, giving $\frac{a}{99}$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How must recurring decimals be handled before performing multiplication or division?

**Back:** Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction first.

**Choices:**

- Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction first. ✔
  - _why:_ Fractions represent recurring decimals exactly, so multiplication or division can be completed without introducing rounding errors from an approximate decimal value.
- Round every recurring decimal to a fixed number of places first.
  - _why:_ Rounding replaces the recurring decimal with an approximation, which can make the final result inaccurate. Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction first to preserve its exact value.
- Convert every recurring decimal into a percentage first.
  - _why:_ Changing a recurring decimal into a percentage does not remove its recurring nature. It should be converted into an exact fraction before multiplication or division.
- Treat every recurring decimal as a terminating decimal first.
  - _why:_ A recurring decimal does not terminate, so cutting it off changes its value. Convert it into a fraction first so the calculation remains exact.

**Back:** When dividing fractions converted from recurring decimals, multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction.

**Choices:**

- reciprocal ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal, found by interchanging its numerator and denominator.
- numerator
  - _why:_ Using only the numerator ignores the denominator and changes the fraction’s value. Division requires multiplication by the reciprocal of the entire second fraction.
- denominator
  - _why:_ Using only the denominator is a common incomplete inversion error. The correct method is to swap both parts and multiply by the reciprocal.
- additive inverse
  - _why:_ The additive inverse changes a number’s sign and is used when dealing with subtraction. Fraction division instead requires the reciprocal of the second fraction.

**Choices:**

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Convert the fraction $\frac{7}{11}$ to recurring decimal notation.

**Back:** $$0.\dot{6}\dot{3}$

**Choices:**

- $$0.\dot{6}\dot{3}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing 7 by 11 gives $0.636363\ldots$, so the repeating block is 63. Dots above 6 and 3 mark the beginning and end of that block.
- $$0.\dot{3}\dot{6}$
  - _why:_ This represents $0.363636\ldots$, which equals $\frac{4}{11}$. Dividing 7 by 11 instead produces the repeating block 63, giving $0.\dot{6}\dot{3}$.
- $$0.6\dot{3}$
  - _why:_ This represents $0.63333\ldots$, where only 3 recurs. For $\frac{7}{11}$, both digits in the block 63 repeat, so both endpoints need dots.
- $$0.\dot{7}\dot{0}$
  - _why:_ This represents $0.707070\ldots$, not the result of dividing 7 by 11. Long division gives $0.636363\ldots$, written $0.\dot{6}\dot{3}$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it a common mistake to compare $100x$ and $x$ for the decimal $0.3\dot{8}$?

**Back:** The recurring tails will not match (one starts at the first decimal place, the other at the second).

**Choices:**

- The recurring tails will not match (one starts at the first decimal place, the other at the second). ✔
  - _why:_ The multiplier should be chosen so the repeating sections begin in corresponding positions before subtraction. Here, the recurring 8 begins after the non-recurring 3 in $x$.
- The recurring tails will match immediately (both start at the first decimal place).
  - _why:_ In $x=0.3888\ldots$, the first decimal digit is the non-recurring 3, while after multiplying by 100 the recurring tail starts immediately. Their starting positions therefore differ.
- Multiplying by 100 removes the recurring part (so $100x$ is a whole number).
  - _why:_ Multiplication by 100 shifts the decimal point two places but does not remove an infinite recurring tail. The issue is that the recurring tails begin in different positions.
- The non-recurring digit also repeats forever (so the entire decimal forms one recurring block).
  - _why:_ Only the 8 repeats in $0.3\dot{8}$; the 3 occurs once. Consequently, the repeating tail begins at the second decimal place in $x$, not the first.
