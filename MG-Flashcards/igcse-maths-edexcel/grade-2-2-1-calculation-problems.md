## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] is the cost of one item; a [ ___ ] is an amount per unit.

**Back:** A unit price is the cost of one item; a rate is an amount per unit.

**Choices:**

- A unit price is the cost of one item; a rate is an amount per unit. ✔
  - _why:_ A unit price gives the cost of a single item, while a rate compares an amount with one unit of another quantity, such as miles per hour.
- A total price is the cost of one item; a ratio is an amount per unit.
  - _why:_ A total price covers all items rather than one item, and a ratio does not necessarily express an amount per unit. The correct terms are unit price and rate.
- A rate is the cost of one item; a unit price is an amount per unit.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two definitions. A unit price is specifically the cost of one item, whereas a rate expresses an amount for each unit.
- A unit cost is the cost of one item; a total is an amount per unit.
  - _why:_ Although unit cost can describe one item's cost, a total is an overall amount, not an amount per unit. The required paired terms are unit price and rate.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** A price is in pence, but payment is in pounds. What must happen before subtracting?

**Back:** Convert to one unit, usually pence.

**Choices:**

- Convert to one unit, usually pence. ✔
  - _why:_ Both amounts must use the same unit before subtraction. Converting pounds to pence is usually convenient because it avoids calculations involving decimal amounts of pounds.
- Convert to different units, keeping pounds and pence.
  - _why:_ Amounts measured in pounds and pence cannot be subtracted directly as separate units. Convert both to one common unit, usually pence, before carrying out the subtraction.
- Round to whole pounds before subtracting.
  - _why:_ Rounding would discard the pence and make the calculated change inaccurate. Convert both amounts to one exact unit, usually pence, before subtracting.
- Subtract the numbers first, then convert.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the numbers before matching their units treats pounds and pence as equivalent. Convert to one unit, usually pence, and only then subtract.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Back:** £1 = 100p and £10 = 1000p.

**Choices:**

- £1 = 100p and £10 = 1000p. ✔
  - _why:_ There are 100 pence in each pound, so £1 equals 100p and multiplying by 10 gives £10 equal to 1000p.
- £1 = 10p and £10 = 100p.
  - _why:_ This uses 10 pence per pound instead of 100, making both conversions ten times too small. £1 is 100p and £10 is 1000p.
- £1 = 100p and £10 = 100p.
  - _why:_ The first conversion is correct, but the second fails to multiply by 10. Since each pound is 100p, £10 equals 1000p.
- £1 = 1000p and £10 = 10000p.
  - _why:_ This adds an extra factor of 10 to both conversions. One pound contains 100p, so ten pounds contains 1000p.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What calculation finds change after a purchase?

**Back:** Amount paid - total cost.

**Choices:**

- Amount paid - total cost. ✔
  - _why:_ Change is the portion of the payment left after paying the full cost, so it is calculated by subtracting the total cost from the amount paid.
- Total cost - amount paid.
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction and would usually produce a negative answer. Change is found using amount paid minus total cost.
- Amount paid + total cost.
  - _why:_ Adding combines the payment and cost instead of finding what remains. Change is the difference, calculated as amount paid minus total cost.
- Total cost ÷ amount paid.
  - _why:_ Division finds a ratio rather than the money remaining after a purchase. Change is found by subtracting the total cost from the amount paid.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If each item costs more and the same note is used, what happens to the change?

**Back:** Change decreases.

**Choices:**

- Change decreases. ✔
  - _why:_ With the amount paid fixed, a higher item cost leaves less money remaining. Therefore, the change decreases as the cost increases.
- Change increases.
  - _why:_ This reverses the relationship between cost and change. If the same note is used and the cost rises, less money remains, so change decreases.
- Change stays equal.
  - _why:_ Change stays unchanged only if the total cost and payment stay unchanged. A higher cost with the same payment means the change decreases.
- Change doubles.
  - _why:_ There is no doubling relationship between an increased price and the change. With a fixed payment, increasing the cost simply makes the change decrease.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a price is reduced, total cost [ ___ ] and change [ ___ ].

**Back:** If a price is reduced, total cost goes down and change goes up.

**Choices:**

- If a price is reduced, total cost goes down and change goes up. ✔
  - _why:_ Reducing a price lowers the amount that must be paid. If the payment stays the same, subtracting a smaller total cost leaves more change.
- If a price is reduced, total cost goes up and change goes down.
  - _why:_ This reverses both effects of a price reduction. A lower price makes the total cost go down and, for the same payment, makes the change go up.
- If a price is reduced, total cost goes down and change goes down.
  - _why:_ The total cost does fall, but the change does not fall with it. With the same payment, a lower cost leaves more money, so change goes up.
- If a price is reduced, total cost stays the same and change goes up.
  - _why:_ The change increases, but the total cannot stay unchanged when an included price is reduced. The total cost goes down, leaving more change from the same payment.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** In a bill with different item types, what do you do after finding each type's cost?

**Back:** Add the separate totals.

**Choices:**

- Add the separate totals. ✔
  - _why:_ Each item type has its own subtotal, found from its price and quantity. Adding these separate totals gives the complete bill.
- Multiply the separate totals.
  - _why:_ Multiplying subtotals does not represent the combined cost of different item types. Once each subtotal is found, add the separate totals to obtain the bill.
- Subtract the separate totals.
  - _why:_ Subtracting would find a difference between item-type costs rather than the full amount owed. The bill is found by adding all the separate totals.
- Divide the separate totals.
  - _why:_ Dividing the subtotals produces a comparison or ratio, not the total bill. Add the separate totals to find the overall cost of all items.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** When adding money, line up the [ ___ ] and use [ ___ ].

**Back:** When adding money, line up the decimal points and use two decimal places.

**Choices:**

- When adding money, line up the decimal points and use two decimal places. ✔
  - _why:_ Aligning decimal points places pounds above pounds and pence above pence. Money is written to two decimal places because one pound contains 100 pence.
- When adding money, line up the pound signs and use two decimal places.
  - _why:_ Pound signs can occupy different positions when amounts have different numbers of digits. The decimal points must be aligned, with money written to two decimal places.
- When adding money, line up the decimal points and use one decimal place.
  - _why:_ Using one decimal place would treat pence as tenths of a pound rather than hundredths. Align the decimal points and use two decimal places for money.
- When adding money, line up the decimal points and use three decimal places.
  - _why:_ Three decimal places would represent thousandths of a pound, which are not used for ordinary money calculations. Align decimal points and write amounts to two decimal places.

## Card 11 — `basic`

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** “As many as possible” usually means round [ ___ ] because you need [ ___ ] items.

**Back:** “As many as possible” usually means round down because you need whole items.

**Choices:**

- “As many as possible” usually means round down because you need whole items. ✔
  - _why:_ A decimal result represents a partly completed item, but only complete items can be counted. Rounding down gives the greatest whole number that does not exceed the available amount.
- “As many as possible” usually means round up because you need whole items.
  - _why:_ Rounding up would include an extra whole item without enough money or material to obtain it. Round down to find the greatest number of complete items available.
- “As many as possible” usually means round down because you need partial items.
  - _why:_ Rounding down is used precisely because partial items cannot be counted in this situation. The required items are whole, so the correct filled terms are down and whole.
- “As many as possible” usually means round nearest because you need whole items.
  - _why:_ Rounding to the nearest whole number can round up and claim an unaffordable extra item. To maximise complete items without exceeding the available amount, round down.
