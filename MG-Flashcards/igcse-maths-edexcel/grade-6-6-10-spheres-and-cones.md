## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What step must be taken first if a 3D geometry question provides a diameter but the formula requires $r$?

**Back:** Halve the diameter to find the radius ($r = \frac{d}{2}$).

**Choices:**

- Halve the diameter to find the radius ($r = \frac{d}{2}$). ✔
  - _why:_ The radius is half the diameter, so dividing the given diameter by 2 produces the value of $r$ required by sphere and cone formulae.
- Double the diameter to find the radius ($r = 2d$).
  - _why:_ Doubling confuses the relationship between radius and diameter. The diameter is twice the radius, so the diameter must be halved: $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- Square the diameter to find the radius ($r = d^2$).
  - _why:_ Squaring the diameter does not convert it into a radius and gives incompatible dimensions. The correct first step is to halve the diameter using $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- Divide the diameter by $\pi$ to find the radius ($r = \frac{d}{\pi}$).
  - _why:_ Dividing by $\pi$ is associated with rearranging some circumference formulae, not converting diameter to radius. The radius is found by halving: $r = \frac{d}{2}$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the volume of a cone.

**Back:** $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cone occupies one third of the volume of a cylinder with the same radius and perpendicular height, giving $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \pi r^2h$
  - _why:_ This is the volume of the corresponding cylinder because it omits the cone’s factor of one third. The cone’s volume is $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi rh^2$
  - _why:_ The radius must be squared because the circular base has area $\pi r^2$; the height is not squared. The correct formula is $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ The factor $\frac{4}{3}$ and the power $r^3$ belong to the volume of a sphere. A cone has volume $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the curved surface area of a cone.

**Back:** $$A = \pi rl$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \pi rl$ ✔
  - _why:_ The curved surface of a cone unwraps into a sector whose area simplifies to the product of $\pi$, the base radius $r$, and slant height $l$.
- $$A = \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This calculates the area of the cone’s circular base, not its curved surface. The curved surface area uses the slant height and is $A = \pi rl$.
- $$A = 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This resembles the curved surface area of a cylinder and incorrectly uses vertical height $h$. For a cone, use slant height $l$: $A = \pi rl$.
- $$A = \pi r(r + l)$
  - _why:_ This includes both $\pi rl$ and $\pi r^2$, so it gives the cone’s total surface area. The curved surface alone has area $A = \pi rl$.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the formula for the curved surface area of a cone ($\pi rl$), the symbol $l$ represents the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In the formula for the curved surface area of a cone ($\pi rl$), the symbol $l$ represents the slant height.

**Choices:**

- slant height ✔
  - _why:_ The symbol $l$ measures the distance from the edge of the circular base to the apex along the cone’s sloping outer surface.
- vertical height
  - _why:_ The vertical height is the perpendicular distance from the base centre to the apex and is usually labelled $h$. In $\pi rl$, $l$ represents the slant height.
- base radius
  - _why:_ The base radius is the distance from the base centre to its circumference and is represented by $r$. The symbol $l$ represents the cone’s slant height.
- base diameter
  - _why:_ The base diameter is twice the radius and is usually represented by $d$. In the curved surface area formula, $l$ instead represents the slant height.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the slant height ($l$) of a cone if you only know the vertical height ($h$) and radius ($r$)?

**Back:** Use Pythagoras' Theorem: $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$.

**Choices:**

- Use Pythagoras' Theorem: $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$. ✔
  - _why:_ The radius and vertical height form the perpendicular sides of a right-angled triangle, while the slant height is its hypotenuse, so $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$.
- Use Pythagoras' Theorem: $l^2 = h^2 - r^2$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting is used when a shorter side is unknown, but the slant height is the hypotenuse. Its square equals the sum: $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$.
- Use Pythagoras' Theorem: $l = r + h$.
  - _why:_ Pythagoras requires the squares of the perpendicular lengths to be added, not the lengths themselves. The correct relationship is $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$.
- Use Pythagoras' Theorem: $l^2 = (r + h)^2$.
  - _why:_ Squaring the sum introduces an extra $2rh$ term, so it does not apply to the right-angled cross-section. The correct relationship is $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the total surface area of a solid cone?

**Back:** Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($\pi r^2$)

**Choices:**

- Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($\pi r^2$) ✔
  - _why:_ A solid cone has a curved outer surface and one circular base. Adding their areas gives total surface area $\pi rl + \pi r^2$.
- Curved surface area ($2\pi rh$)Base area ($\pi r^2$)
  - _why:_ The expression $2\pi rh$ is the curved surface area of a cylinder, not a cone. A cone requires $\pi rl$ plus its base area $\pi r^2$.
- Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($2\pi r^2$)
  - _why:_ The curved area is correct, but $2\pi r^2$ counts two circular bases. A cone has only one base, so the total is $\pi rl + \pi r^2$.
- Curved surface area ($\pi rh$)Base area ($\pi r$)
  - _why:_ Using $h$ instead of $l$ ignores the slope of the cone, while $\pi r$ is only half a circumference, not an area. Use $\pi rl + \pi r^2$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Identify the difference between the vertical height and slant height of a cone.

**Back:** Vertical height: distance from base centre to apexSlant height: distance along the outside edge to apex

**Choices:**

- Vertical height: distance from base centre to apexSlant height: distance along the outside edge to apex ✔
  - _why:_ Vertical height is measured perpendicularly through the cone’s interior from the base centre, whereas slant height follows the sloping outer surface from the base edge.
- Vertical height: distance along the outside edge to apexSlant height: distance from base centre to apex
  - _why:_ These descriptions have been reversed. Vertical height runs from the base centre to the apex, while slant height runs along the outside edge to the apex.
- Vertical height: distance from base centre to circumferenceSlant height: distance from base centre to apex
  - _why:_ The radius ends at the base circumference and does not reach the apex. Vertical height joins the base centre to the apex, while slant height follows the outside edge.
- Vertical height: distance across the circular baseSlant height: distance around the circular base
  - _why:_ The diameter crosses the circular base and the circumference goes around it; neither describes the cone’s heights. The two heights run to the apex through the centre or along the edge.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** The formula for the volume of a sphere is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The formula for the volume of a sphere is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi \boxed{r^3}$.

**Choices:**

- $$r^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ A sphere’s volume depends on three dimensions, so its radius is cubed. The complete formula is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$r^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the radius produces an area-like quantity rather than a volume. A sphere’s volume is three-dimensional, so the missing term must be $r^3$.
- $$2r^3$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the cubed radius by 2 would make the calculated volume twice the correct value. The coefficient is already $\frac{4}{3}\pi$, so the term is $r^3$.
- $$d^3$
  - _why:_ Substituting the diameter directly without changing the coefficient makes the result eight times too large because $d^3 = 8r^3$. The required term is $r^3$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the surface area of a sphere.

**Back:** $$A = 4\pi r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$A = 4\pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A sphere’s surface area is four times the area of a circle with the same radius, so the formula is $A = 4\pi r^2$.
- $$A = \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the area of one circle, not the curved surface of a sphere. A sphere has surface area $A = 4\pi r^2$.
- $$A = 2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the curved surface area of a hemisphere, so it accounts for only half a sphere. A complete sphere has area $A = 4\pi r^2$.
- $$A = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This confuses surface area with the volume of a sphere and incorrectly uses cubic units. The surface area formula is $A = 4\pi r^2$.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** A solid hemisphere has a total surface area of [ ___ ].

**Back:** A solid hemisphere has a total surface area of $3\pi r^2$.

**Choices:**

- $$3\pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A solid hemisphere includes curved area $2\pi r^2$ and a circular base of area $\pi r^2$, giving a total of $3\pi r^2$.
- $$2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This includes only the hemisphere’s curved surface and omits its flat circular base. Adding the base area $\pi r^2$ gives $3\pi r^2$.
- $$4\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the surface area of a complete sphere, not a solid hemisphere. Including the hemisphere’s curved surface and base gives $3\pi r^2$.
- $$\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This gives only the area of the flat circular base and omits the curved surface. The total surface area is therefore $3\pi r^2$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the volume of a hemisphere?

**Back:** $$V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ A hemisphere is half a sphere, so halving the sphere’s volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ gives $V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This is the volume of a complete sphere, so it has not been halved. A hemisphere’s volume is $V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ The factor $\frac{1}{3}$ is associated with cones and does not give half a sphere. A hemisphere’s volume is $V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ Using $r^2$ produces square units, which measure area rather than volume. Volume requires $r^3$, giving $V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the total surface area of a solid hemisphere $3\pi r^2$ rather than $2\pi r^2$?

**Back:** It includes the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) plus the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$).

**Choices:**

- It includes the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) plus the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ A solid hemisphere has an exposed curved surface and an exposed circular base. Their areas add to $2\pi r^2 + \pi r^2 = 3\pi r^2$.
- It includes the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) but excludes the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ Excluding the base gives only $2\pi r^2$, which is the curved surface area. A solid hemisphere includes the base, making the total $3\pi r^2$.
- It includes the curved surface ($\pi r^2$) plus the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ The curved surface has been incorrectly treated as one circular area. It is $2\pi r^2$, and adding the base $\pi r^2$ gives $3\pi r^2$.
- It includes the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) plus the flat circular base ($2\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ The circular base has been doubled: its area is $\pi r^2$, not $2\pi r^2$. The correct total is therefore $3\pi r^2$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** When calculating the volume of a compound solid, what is the general rule?

**Back:** Add the volumes of the individual parts together.

**Choices:**

- Add the volumes of the individual parts together. ✔
  - _why:_ A compound solid is divided into non-overlapping familiar solids. Calculating each part’s volume and adding them gives the volume occupied by the complete solid.
- Subtract the volumes of the individual parts from each other.
  - _why:_ Subtraction is used only when a section has been removed to form a hollow or cut-out shape. Joined solid parts normally have their volumes added.
- Multiply the volumes of the individual parts together.
  - _why:_ Multiplying volumes produces inappropriate higher-powered units and does not represent combined space. The volumes of the non-overlapping individual parts should be added together.
- Use the volume of the largest individual part only.
  - _why:_ Ignoring the smaller parts underestimates the space occupied by the compound solid. The correct method is to calculate and add every individual part’s volume.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** When calculating the surface area of a compound solid, which faces are excluded?

**Back:** Joined faces (internal faces) are not counted; only outside faces are included.

**Choices:**

- Joined faces (internal faces) are not counted; only outside faces are included. ✔
  - _why:_ Joined faces lie inside the compound solid and are not exposed. Surface area measures only the faces forming the solid’s external boundary.
- Outside faces (external faces) are not counted; only joined faces are included.
  - _why:_ This reverses the rule: surface area measures exposed external faces, while joined faces are hidden inside the solid and must be excluded.
- Joined faces (internal faces) are counted twice; all outside faces are also included.
  - _why:_ Counting each joined face twice treats both contact surfaces as exposed, causing an overestimate. Internal joined faces are excluded, and only outside faces are counted.
- Curved faces (rounded faces) are not counted; only flat faces are included.
  - _why:_ Curved surfaces contribute to surface area whenever they are exposed. The distinction is between outside and internal faces, not between curved and flat faces.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you handle rounding in multi-step calculations for cones and spheres?

**Back:** Round only at the final answer to avoid inaccuracy; keep the full calculator value until then.

**Choices:**

- Round only at the final answer to avoid inaccuracy; keep the full calculator value until then. ✔
  - _why:_ Retaining full calculator values prevents small rounding errors from accumulating through later operations. Round once, to the required accuracy, after completing the calculation.
- Round after every calculation step to keep values simple; use each rounded result in the next step.
  - _why:_ Repeated intermediate rounding introduces errors that can accumulate and alter the final answer. Keep full calculator values and round only the final result.
- Round all intermediate values to 1 decimal place to ensure consistency; use these values in later steps.
  - _why:_ Consistent rounding is still premature rounding and can reduce the final answer’s accuracy. Intermediate values should remain unrounded until the calculation is complete.
- Round the given measurements before calculating to simplify the arithmetic; then keep the final value unchanged.
  - _why:_ Rounding the original measurements discards accuracy before the calculation begins, while leaving the final result unrounded ignores the requested precision. Round only at the end.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ An exact answer in terms of $\pi$ retains the symbol, such as $36\pi$, rather than replacing it with an approximate decimal value.

  - _why:_ Using 3.14 creates an approximate decimal answer rather than an exact answer in terms of $\pi$. The symbol $\pi$ must remain in the expression.

  - _why:_ Although $\frac{22}{7}$ is a common approximation, it is not exactly $\pi$. An answer in terms of $\pi$ must retain the symbol itself.
- Remove $\pi$ from the final expression and leave only its numerical coefficient.
  - _why:_ Removing $\pi$ changes the value of the expression rather than preserving an exact form. The final answer must include $\pi$ alongside its coefficient.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** When finding the radius of a sphere from its volume, you must use a [ ___ ] to undo the $r^3$ term.

**Back:** When finding the radius of a sphere from its volume, you must use a cube root to undo the $r^3$ term.

**Choices:**

- cube root ✔
  - _why:_ The sphere-volume formula contains $r^3$, so applying a cube root reverses the cubing operation and isolates the radius.
- square root
  - _why:_ A square root reverses squaring, not cubing. Since the sphere-volume formula contains $r^3$, the correct inverse operation is a cube root.
- cube
  - _why:_ Cubing would raise the expression to another power rather than isolate $r$. A cube root is required to undo the existing $r^3$ term.
- reciprocal
  - _why:_ Taking a reciprocal changes a quantity to its multiplicative inverse and does not undo a power of three. A cube root correctly isolates $r$.

**Back:** An object submerged in water displaces a volume of water equal to its own volume.

**Choices:**

- own volume ✔
  - _why:_ A fully submerged object occupies space that water can no longer occupy, so it displaces an amount of water equal to its own volume.
- own mass
  - _why:_ Mass measures the amount of matter, not the space occupied. A fully submerged object displaces water equal to its own volume.
- own weight
  - _why:_ Weight is a force and cannot equal a displaced volume. The volume of water displaced by a fully submerged object equals its own volume.
- surface area
  - _why:_ Surface area measures the exterior covering rather than the space occupied. Water displacement for a fully submerged object equals the object’s own volume.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the rise in water level in a rectangular container when an object is submerged.

**Back:** $$\text{rise} = \frac{\text{volume displaced}}{\text{base area of container}}$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{rise} = \frac{\text{volume displaced}}{\text{base area of container}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The displaced volume forms an additional rectangular prism. Since volume equals base area multiplied by height, dividing by the container’s base area gives the rise.
- $$\text{rise} = \text{volume displaced} \times \text{base area of container}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying volume by area does not produce a length and confuses rearrangement of the prism formula. The displaced volume must be divided by the container’s base area.
- $$\text{rise} = \frac{\text{base area of container}}{\text{volume displaced}}$
  - _why:_ Reversing the fraction gives units of inverse length rather than a height. The rise is the displaced volume divided by the container’s base area.
- $$\text{rise} = \frac{\text{volume displaced}}{\text{side area of container}}$
  - _why:_ The water rises across the horizontal base, not a vertical side. Therefore, divide the displaced volume by the base area of the container.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** The base area of a rectangular container is calculated as [ ___ ].

**Back:** The base area of a rectangular container is calculated as length $\times$ width.

**Choices:**

- length $\times$ width ✔
  - _why:_ The base is a rectangle, and a rectangle’s area is found by multiplying its two perpendicular base dimensions: length and width.
- length $+$ width
  - _why:_ Adding the length and width does not calculate area; doubling that sum would calculate perimeter. The rectangular base area is length multiplied by width.
- length $\times$ height
  - _why:_ Multiplying length by height gives the area of a vertical side, not the horizontal base. The base area is length multiplied by width.
- width $\times$ height
  - _why:_ Multiplying width by height gives the area of another vertical side. The rectangular container’s base uses its length and width dimensions.
