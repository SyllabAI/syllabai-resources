## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** Why are scale drawings used?

**Back:** To draw objects that are too big, too small, or too far away at actual size.

**Choices:**

- To draw objects that are too big, too small, or too far away at actual size. ✔
  - _why:_ Scale drawings represent objects whose actual dimensions are impractical to show on paper, while preserving all lengths in the same proportion.
- To draw objects that are the same size, the same shape, or close by at actual size.
  - _why:_ Objects that already fit conveniently do not require scaling. Scale drawings are used for objects that are too big, too small, or too far away to draw at actual size.
- To draw objects that are too heavy, too light, or too expensive at actual size.
  - _why:_ Scale drawings concern dimensions rather than weight or cost. They represent objects that are too big, too small, or too far away at a practical size.
- To draw objects that are too wide, too narrow, or too irregular at actual size.
  - _why:_ Width and shape alone do not explain the purpose of scaling. Scale drawings allow objects that are too big, too small, or too far away to be represented.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a scale drawing, every length is enlarged or reduced by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a scale drawing, every length is enlarged or reduced by the same multiplier.

**Choices:**

- same multiplier ✔
  - _why:_ Using the same multiplier for every length preserves the proportions and shape of the original object, which is essential for an accurate scale drawing.
- nearest measurement
  - _why:_ Rounding every length to a nearby measurement can distort the proportions. Every length must instead be enlarged or reduced using the same multiplier.
- largest dimension
  - _why:_ The largest dimension may help determine a convenient scale, but it does not transform every length. Each length is enlarged or reduced by the same multiplier.
- different multiplier
  - _why:_ Using different multipliers would stretch or compress the object unevenly and change its shape. An accurate scale drawing uses the same multiplier for every length.

**Choices:**

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a ratio compare in scale drawing questions?

**Back:** Two quantities of the same type, such as two lengths.

**Choices:**

- Two quantities of the same type, such as two lengths. ✔
  - _why:_ A ratio compares like quantities, such as one length with another length. Units should be made consistent before the ratio is formed.
- Two quantities of a different type, such as length and area.
  - _why:_ Comparing unlike quantities such as length and area does not form the scale ratio required here. A scale ratio compares two quantities of the same type.
- Two quantities of the same value, such as two equal lengths.
  - _why:_ The quantities do not need to have equal values; they need to be the same type. For example, a drawing length can be compared with a real length.
- Two quantities of the same unit, such as length and width.
  - _why:_ Matching units is necessary, but the defining requirement is that the quantities are of the same type. Units can first be converted before forming the ratio.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** An accurate scale drawing can be [ ___ ] to estimate real sizes.

**Back:** An accurate scale drawing can be measured to estimate real sizes.

**Choices:**

- measured ✔
  - _why:_ Accurately measuring a length on the scale drawing allows the corresponding real length to be estimated by applying the stated scale.
- counted
  - _why:_ Counting is suitable for separate objects, not continuous lengths. An accurate scale drawing is measured, and the scale is then used to estimate real sizes.
- folded
  - _why:_ Folding a drawing does not provide its dimensions and may reduce accuracy. The drawing should be measured before the scale is applied to estimate real sizes.
- traced
  - _why:_ Tracing copies the shape but does not determine any real dimensions. The scale drawing must be measured and its scale used to estimate real sizes.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** On the same scale drawing, if one drawn height is 3 times another, what happens to the real heights?

**Back:** One real height is also 3 times the other.

**Choices:**

- One real height is also 3 times the other. ✔
  - _why:_ Both drawn heights use the same scale multiplier, so their ratio is preserved. A 3:1 ratio on the drawing therefore gives a 3:1 ratio in reality.
- One real height is also 9 times the other.
  - _why:_ Squaring the factor of 3 confuses a length comparison with an area scale factor. Heights are lengths, so one real height is 3 times the other.
- One real height is also 6 times the other.
  - _why:_ Doubling the factor incorrectly applies the scale relationship twice. Ratios between corresponding lengths are preserved, so the real heights also differ by a factor of 3.
- One real height is also one-third of the other.
  - _why:_ Taking the reciprocal reverses which height is being compared. If one drawn height is 3 times another, the corresponding real height is also 3 times the other.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** When comparing two lengths on the same scale drawing, what should you compare first?

**Back:** Compare drawing length with drawing length first.

**Choices:**

- Compare drawing length with drawing length first. ✔
  - _why:_ Lengths on the same drawing share one scale, so comparing their drawn measurements directly gives the same ratio as the corresponding real lengths.
- Compare drawing length with real length first.
  - _why:_ This mixes scaled and actual measurements before the scale relationship has been established. First compare drawing length with drawing length, since both use the same scale.
- Compare real length with real length first.
  - _why:_ The real lengths may be the unknown quantities being investigated, so they cannot necessarily be compared first. Start by comparing the measurable drawing lengths.
- Compare drawing area with drawing length first.
  - _why:_ Area and length are different types of quantity and cannot form the required length ratio. First compare one drawing length with another drawing length.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you not compare a drawing length directly with a real length?

**Back:** First find the drawing multiplier, then apply it to the real measurement.

**Choices:**

- First find the drawing multiplier, then apply it to the real measurement. ✔
  - _why:_ A drawing length and a real length are expressed at different scales. Finding the drawing multiplier provides the conversion needed before applying it consistently to the measurement.
- First find the area multiplier, then apply it to the real measurement.
  - _why:_ An area multiplier is inappropriate when converting lengths and would usually involve squaring the scale factor. Find the drawing multiplier for lengths, then apply it to the real measurement.
- First find the unit difference, then add it to the real measurement.
  - _why:_ Scale conversion is multiplicative, not an additive adjustment based on units. Find the drawing multiplier and apply it to the real measurement.
- First find the drawing difference, then subtract it from the real measurement.
  - _why:_ Subtracting a difference assumes an additive relationship, but scale drawings use proportional multiplication. Find the drawing multiplier, then apply it to the real measurement.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** A van is 1.8 cm high and 5.4 cm long on a drawing. What multiplier compares length to height?

**Back:** 3, since $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$.

**Choices:**

- 3, since $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$. ✔
  - _why:_ The length-to-height multiplier is found by dividing the drawing’s length by its height. Since $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$, the van is three times as long as it is high.
- $\frac{1}{3}$, since $1.8 \div 5.4 = \frac{1}{3}$.
  - _why:_ Dividing height by length reverses the required comparison and gives $1.8 \div 5.4 = \frac{1}{3}$. Length compared with height is $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$.
- 3.6, since $5.4 - 1.8 = 3.6$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the dimensions finds their difference, not the multiplier comparing them. The multiplier is obtained by division: $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$.
- 7.2, since $5.4 + 1.8 = 7.2$.
  - _why:_ Adding the dimensions gives their total rather than their multiplicative comparison. Dividing length by height gives the required multiplier: $5.4 \div 1.8 = 3$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** A real van is 1.6 m high and 3 times as long. What is its real length?

**Back:** 4.8 m, since $1.6 \times 3 = 4.8$.

**Choices:**

- 4.8 m, since $1.6 \times 3 = 4.8$. ✔
  - _why:_ The van is three times as long as its height, so its length is found by multiplying: $1.6 \times 3 = 4.8$ m.
- 4.6 m, since $1.6 + 3 = 4.6$.
  - _why:_ Adding 3 treats the multiplier as an extra length in metres. “Three times as long” requires multiplication, giving $1.6 \times 3 = 4.8$ m.
- 3.2 m, since $1.6 \times 2 = 3.2$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 2 makes the van twice its height rather than three times its height. The required calculation is $1.6 \times 3 = 4.8$ m.
- 48 m, since $16 \times 3 = 48$.
  - _why:_ This loses the decimal place when multiplying $1.6$ by 3. The correct product is $4.8$, so the van’s real length is $4.8$ m.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** A written scale of $1:50$ means 1 unit on the drawing represents [ ___ ] of the same units in real life.

**Back:** A written scale of $1:50$ means 1 unit on the drawing represents 50 of the same units in real life.

**Choices:**

- 50 ✔
  - _why:_ In the ratio $1:50$, the first number refers to the drawing and the second to real life. Therefore, one drawing unit represents 50 real-life units.
- 5
  - _why:_ This makes the real measurement only five times the drawing measurement, usually by dropping a zero. A scale of $1:50$ means one drawing unit represents 50 real-life units.
- 49
  - _why:_ Subtracting 1 from 50 misinterprets the two parts of the scale as values to combine. The ratio directly states that one drawing unit represents 50 real-life units.
- 500
  - _why:_ This adds an extra factor of 10 to the scale and would describe $1:500$. At $1:50$, one drawing unit represents exactly 50 real-life units.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** For a written scale $1:n$, how do you change a drawing/model length into a real length?

**Back:** Multiply by the scale number $n$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply by the scale number $n$. ✔
  - _why:_ At scale $1:n$, every one unit on the drawing represents $n$ real units. A drawing or model length must therefore be multiplied by $n$.
- Divide by the scale number $n$.
  - _why:_ Dividing is used to change a real length into its smaller drawing length. To change a drawing or model length into the real length, multiply by $n$.
- Add the scale number $n$.
  - _why:_ Adding $n$ treats the scale number as a fixed length rather than a multiplier. At scale $1:n$, multiply the drawing length by $n$ to obtain reality.
- Subtract the scale number $n$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the scale number does not apply a scale factor and can even produce a negative length. The real length is the drawing length multiplied by $n$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** In scale questions, when should you convert units?

**Back:** Convert units at the end.

**Choices:**

- Convert units at the end. ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping both measurements in the same small unit makes the scale multiplication straightforward and avoids mixing units. Convert the resulting real measurement into the requested unit at the end.
- Convert units at the beginning.
  - _why:_ Converting before applying the scale factor can introduce unnecessary decimals and unit confusion. Keep consistent units during the scale calculation, then convert the final result at the end.
- Convert units halfway through.
  - _why:_ Converting halfway through risks applying the scale factor to measurements expressed in different units. Complete the scale calculation using consistent units and convert the answer at the end.
- Convert units before multiplying.
  - _why:_ A final conversion is necessary whenever the calculated unit differs from the unit requested. Complete the scale calculation first, then convert units at the end.

**Back:** The perimeter of a 2D shape is the total distance around its outside.

**Choices:**

- outside ✔
  - _why:_ The perimeter measures the complete distance around the outside boundary of a two-dimensional shape, rather than its interior region or the distance across it.
- inside
  - _why:_ The interior describes the region contained by a shape, not the path around its boundary. The correct term is outside because perimeter measures the external distance.
- area
  - _why:_ Area measures the amount of two-dimensional surface enclosed by a shape, while perimeter measures distance around it. The missing word should refer to the outside.
- diagonal
  - _why:_ A diagonal joins two non-adjacent vertices across a shape and does not trace its boundary. Perimeter is the total distance around the outside.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the perimeter formula for a rectangle with length $l$ and width $w$?

**Back:** $$P = \boldsymbol{2(l+w)}$

**Choices:**

- $$P = \boldsymbol{2(l+w)}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A rectangle has two sides of length $l$ and two sides of width $w$. Adding them gives $l+w+l+w$, which simplifies to $2(l+w)$.
- $$P = \boldsymbol{2(l-w)}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the width removes two side lengths instead of adding all four sides. The perimeter must add the two lengths and two widths, giving $2(l+w)$.
- $$P = \boldsymbol{l+w}$
  - _why:_ Adding one length and one width gives only half of the rectangle’s perimeter. Both pairs of equal sides must be included, so the formula is $2(l+w)$.
- $$P = \boldsymbol{lw}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying length by width calculates the rectangle’s area, not its perimeter. Perimeter requires adding the side lengths and is given by $2(l+w)$.
