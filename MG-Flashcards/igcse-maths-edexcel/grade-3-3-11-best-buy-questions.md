## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a best buy comparison, what must be kept the same for a fair comparison?

**Back:** The amount/unit of product.

**Choices:**

- The amount/unit of product. ✔
  - _why:_ Comparing the same amount or unit removes differences in pack size, allowing the prices to be judged fairly on an equivalent basis.
- The total price of product.
  - _why:_ The total price need not be equal because best-buy calculations compare what each quantity costs. The amount or unit of product must be kept the same.
- The shop/location of purchase.
  - _why:_ Using the same shop is unnecessary and does not account for different pack sizes. A fair comparison keeps the amount or unit of product the same.
- The packaging/type of product.
  - _why:_ Packaging can differ without affecting value, so matching it does not create a fair numerical comparison. The amount or unit of product must be kept the same.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why can the cheapest total price fail to be best value?

**Back:** It may give much less product.

**Choices:**

- It may give much less product. ✔
  - _why:_ A lower total price can simply reflect a smaller quantity. Comparing unit prices reveals whether the cheaper option actually provides more product for the money.
- It may have the lowest unit price.
  - _why:_ Having the lowest unit price would normally make an option the best value, not explain its failure. The cheapest total price can fail because it provides much less product.
- It may give much more product.
  - _why:_ Providing much more product would generally strengthen the case for good value. The real problem is that the cheapest option may provide much less product.
- It may cost less per unit.
  - _why:_ Costing less per unit is the criterion for better value, so this would support the cheapest option. It can fail when its lower price buys much less product.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** Unit price = [ ___ ] ÷ [ ___ ].

**Back:** Unit price = price ÷ amount.

**Choices:**

- Unit price = price ÷ amount. ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the total price by the amount gives the cost of one unit, enabling packs of different sizes to be compared on the same basis.
- Unit price = amount ÷ price.
  - _why:_ This reverses the required division and calculates an amount per unit of currency. Unit price is found by dividing price by amount.
- Unit price = price ÷ unit price.
  - _why:_ Dividing price by unit price would recover the amount, making this formula circular. Unit price must be calculated by dividing price by amount.
- Unit price = unit price ÷ amount.
  - _why:_ Dividing an already known unit price by the amount reduces it again and does not calculate unit cost. The required calculation is price divided by amount.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** After fair unit prices are found, which option is best value?

**Back:** The option with the lowest unit price.

**Choices:**

- The option with the lowest unit price. ✔
  - _why:_ Once every option is expressed for the same unit, the lowest unit price gives the greatest quantity for each pound or penny spent.
- The option with the highest unit price.
  - _why:_ A higher unit price means paying more for the same amount, so it represents worse value. The option with the lowest unit price is best.
- The option with the lowest total price.
  - _why:_ The lowest total price may belong to a much smaller pack and therefore need not be best value. The lowest unit price identifies the best option.
- The option with the largest product amount.
  - _why:_ The largest amount may also cost disproportionately more, so pack size alone cannot determine value. The option with the lowest unit price is best.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** 8 rolls cost £3.60. What is the cost per roll?

**Back:** 45p.

**Choices:**

- 45p. ✔
  - _why:_ Convert £3.60 to 360p, then divide by 8 rolls: $360 \div 8 = 45$. Therefore, each roll costs 45p.
- 450p.
  - _why:_ This is ten times too large because the pounds-to-pence conversion has been mishandled. Dividing 360p by 8 gives 45p per roll.
- 0.45p.
  - _why:_ This divides £3.60 by 8 but then labels the result as pence without converting £0.45 correctly. £0.45 equals 45p.
- 40p.
  - _why:_ This rounds or estimates instead of completing the exact division. Converting to 360p and dividing by 8 gives precisely 45p per roll.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Back:** For potatoes, rice or flour, use cost per kg.

**Choices:**

- kg ✔
  - _why:_ Using cost per kg gives a convenient standard unit for comparing the value of products usually sold in relatively large masses, such as potatoes, rice and flour.

  - _why:_ Cost per 100 g is normally used for smaller quantities such as cheese or sweets. For bulk foods such as potatoes, rice or flour, compare cost per kg.

  - _why:_ Millilitres measure volume and are suitable for liquids, not the mass of solid foods. Potatoes, rice and flour should be compared using cost per kg.

  - _why:_ Litres measure volume, so they are inappropriate for products sold by mass. Potatoes, rice and flour are most conveniently compared using cost per kg.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is wrong with comparing one option per kg and another per 100 g?

**Back:** The amounts are not the same.

**Choices:**

- The amounts are not the same. ✔
  - _why:_ Prices can be compared fairly only when they refer to equal quantities. A kilogram is ten times 100 g, so one price must first be converted.
- The products are not the same.
  - _why:_ Best-buy calculations can compare different brands or products when appropriate. The actual problem is that the prices refer to unequal amounts: 1 kg and 100 g.
- The weights are too large.
  - _why:_ The size of the weights is not the issue; either comparison unit can be used. The amounts must simply be converted so that they are the same.
- The costs must be added together.
  - _why:_ Adding the costs does not create a fair comparison and instead gives a combined price. Convert both prices to the same amount, such as 100 g.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** A 400 g jar costs £8.80. What is its cost per 100 g?

**Back:** £2.20 per 100 g.

**Choices:**

- £2.20 per 100 g. ✔
  - _why:_ There are four lots of 100 g in 400 g, so the price is divided by four: £8.80 ÷ 4 = £2.20 per 100 g.
- £4.40 per 100 g.
  - _why:_ Dividing by two only finds the cost per 200 g. Since 400 g contains four lots of 100 g, £8.80 must be divided by four, giving £2.20.
- £22.00 per 100 g.
  - _why:_ This results from mishandling the decimal or multiplying instead of reducing the price. Dividing £8.80 by the four 100 g portions gives £2.20 per 100 g.
- £35.20 per 100 g.
  - _why:_ Multiplying the price by four reverses the required scaling and produces the cost of 1600 g. Divide by four instead to obtain £2.20 per 100 g.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** When one amount is a simple multiple of another, what method can compare value?

**Back:** Scale to the same amount.

**Choices:**

- Scale to the same amount. ✔
  - _why:_ If one quantity is a simple multiple of the other, multiply or divide both its amount and price by the same number to create a fair comparison.
- Round to the same amount.
  - _why:_ Rounding changes values approximately and may distort a close best-buy comparison. The correct method is to scale both amount and price by the same factor.
- Add to the same amount.
  - _why:_ Adding an amount without changing the price proportionally breaks the relationship between quantity and cost. Scale the amount and price by the same number instead.
- Estimate to the same amount.
  - _why:_ Estimation is unnecessary when the quantities have a simple exact multiple and could give the wrong best buy. Scale exactly to the same amount instead.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** When scaling, multiply or divide the amount and price by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** When scaling, multiply or divide the amount and price by the same number.

**Choices:**

- same number ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying or dividing both quantity and price by the same number preserves the proportional relationship, producing an equivalent price for a different package size.
- different number
  - _why:_ Using different factors changes the price-to-quantity relationship, so the scaled result is not equivalent. Both the amount and price must be changed by the same number.
- larger number
  - _why:_ The factor need not be larger; scaling down requires division. What matters is applying the same number to both the amount and its corresponding price.
- price difference
  - _why:_ The difference between prices is not a scale factor and would not preserve proportionality. Multiply or divide the amount and price by the same number.

**Back:** With extra free, the amount changes, not the price.

**Choices:**

- amount ✔
  - _why:_ An extra-free offer gives more of the product while the customer pays the original price. Therefore, the amount changes but the price does not.
- price
  - _why:_ Confusing extra free with a price reduction changes the wrong quantity. The amount increases in an extra-free offer, while the stated price remains unchanged.
- brand
  - _why:_ The promotion does not require the product’s brand to change. Extra free means the amount supplied increases while the customer pays the same price.
- cost
  - _why:_ Treating extra free as money off confuses two different promotions. The amount of product changes, whereas the cost paid for the pack stays the same.
