## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a surd in terms of its numeric type?

**Back:** An irrational number expressed as an exact root.

**Choices:**

- An irrational number expressed as an exact root. ✔
  - _why:_ A surd is an exact root whose value is irrational, such as $\sqrt{2}$. It cannot be written exactly as a fraction of two integers.
- A rational number expressed as an exact root.
  - _why:_ A root with a rational value, such as $\sqrt{9}=3$, is not a surd. A surd is an irrational number expressed as an exact root.
- An integer number expressed as an exact root.
  - _why:_ Integers obtained from roots of perfect squares are rational, so they are not surds. A surd must be an irrational number left in exact root form.
- A decimal number expressed as an exact root.
  - _why:_ Writing a root as a decimal usually gives an approximation rather than an exact value. A surd is an irrational number expressed exactly using a root symbol.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $\sqrt{64}$ not considered a surd?

**Back:** 64 is a perfect square, so $\sqrt{64} = 8$ (a rational number).

**Choices:**

- 64 is a perfect square, so $\sqrt{64} = 8$ (a rational number). ✔
  - _why:_ Since $64=8^2$, its square root is the integer $8$. Surds are irrational roots, so a root that simplifies to a rational number is not a surd.
- 64 is a perfect cube, so $\sqrt{64} = 4$ (a rational number).
  - _why:_ Confusing square roots with cube roots gives $4$, since $\sqrt[3]{64}=4$. In fact, $64=8^2$, so $\sqrt{64}=8$ and is rational.
- 64 is an even number, so $\sqrt{64} = 32$ (a rational number).
  - _why:_ Halving an even number does not calculate its square root. Because $64$ is the perfect square $8^2$, $\sqrt{64}=8$, which is rational.
- 64 is a multiple of eight, so $\sqrt{64} = 16$ (a rational number).
  - _why:_ Being a multiple of eight does not determine a square root, and $16^2$ is $256$. Since $64=8^2$, the correct value is $8$, a rational number.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** State the rule for splitting a product inside a square root.

**Back:** $$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$

**Choices:**

- $$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A product under a square root can be split into the product of the individual square roots, assuming the quantities are non-negative in the IGCSE context.
- $$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$
  - _why:_ The product rule is often confused with addition, but square roots of factors must be multiplied. The correct identity is $\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$.
- $$\sqrt{ab} = a\sqrt{b}$
  - _why:_ Taking $a$ outside unchanged incorrectly treats it as a perfect-square factor. The correct general rule is $\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$; only $\sqrt{a^2b}=a\sqrt{b}$.
- $$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a+b}$
  - _why:_ Replacing multiplication inside the root with addition changes the value of the expression. A product splits according to $\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step to simplify a surd like $\sqrt{72}$?

**Back:** Find the largest perfect-square factor of the number.

**Choices:**

- Find the largest perfect-square factor of the number. ✔
  - _why:_ Using the largest perfect-square factor allows its square root to be taken immediately, leaving the simplest possible surd in one step.
- Find the largest prime-number factor of the number.
  - _why:_ A prime factor cannot usually be removed from a square root by itself. The useful factor is the largest perfect-square factor, such as $36$ in $72$.
- Find the nearest perfect-square number to the number.
  - _why:_ Using a nearby perfect square would change the original value rather than simplify it exactly. The first step is to find the largest perfect-square factor of the number.
- Find the decimal approximation of the number.
  - _why:_ A decimal approximation loses the exact surd form and does not perform algebraic simplification. Instead, identify the largest perfect-square factor inside the root.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $\sqrt{72}$ into the form $k\sqrt{2}$.

**Back:** $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Since $72=36\times2$ and $36$ is a perfect square, $\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36}\sqrt{2}=6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly evaluates $\sqrt{36}$ as $3$, confusing it with $\sqrt{9}$. Since $\sqrt{36}=6$, the correct simplification is $6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 36\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ This takes $36$ outside the root without finding its square root. The extracted coefficient must be $\sqrt{36}=6$, giving $6\sqrt{2}$.
- $$\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{6}$
  - _why:_ The factor remaining under the root is $2$, not $6$. Splitting $\sqrt{36\times2}$ gives $\sqrt{36}\sqrt{2}=6\sqrt{2}$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** While $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ is true, the expression $\sqrt{a+b}$ [ ___ ] $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$.

**Back:** While $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ is true, the expression $\sqrt{a+b}$ is not equal to $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$.

**Choices:**

- is not equal to ✔
  - _why:_ Square roots do not distribute over addition. For example, $\sqrt{9+16}=5$, whereas $\sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}=7$, so the expressions are not equal.
- is equal to
  - _why:_ This incorrectly applies the product rule to a sum. Square roots distribute over multiplication, but $\sqrt{a+b}$ is not equal to $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$.
- can be expanded to
  - _why:_ Treating a square root like an algebraic bracket creates a false expansion. The valid product rule does not extend to sums, so the expressions are not equal.
- is always twice
  - _why:_ There is no fixed factor of two between these expressions. For instance, $\sqrt{9+16}=5$ while $\sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}=7$, so they are not equal.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What are like surds?

**Back:** Surds that have the same root part (e.g. $3\sqrt{7}$ and $5\sqrt{7}$).

**Choices:**

- Surds that have the same root part (e.g. $3\sqrt{7}$ and $5\sqrt{7}$). ✔
  - _why:_ Like surds contain the same irrational root part, while their coefficients may differ. This allows their coefficients to be added or subtracted like algebraic terms.
- Surds that have the same coefficient (e.g. $3\sqrt{7}$ and $3\sqrt{5}$).
  - _why:_ Matching coefficients do not make surds like terms when their root parts differ. Like surds must share the same root part, such as $3\sqrt{7}$ and $5\sqrt{7}$.
- Surds that have different root parts (e.g. $3\sqrt{7}$ and $5\sqrt{3}$).
  - _why:_ Different root parts make the surds unlike, so their coefficients cannot be combined directly. Like surds have the same root part, such as multiples of $\sqrt{7}$.
- Surds that have the same value (e.g. $2\sqrt{3}$ and $\sqrt{12}$).
  - _why:_ Equivalent expressions may simplify to the same value, but that is not the definition of like surds. In simplified form, like surds have the same root part.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $12\sqrt{3} + 10\sqrt{3}$.

**Back:** $$22\sqrt{3}$

**Choices:**

- $$22\sqrt{3}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms contain the same root part, $\sqrt{3}$, so they are like surds. Adding their coefficients gives $(12+10)\sqrt{3}=22\sqrt{3}$.
- $$22\sqrt{6}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly adds the numbers inside identical roots as well as the coefficients. Like surds keep the root part unchanged, giving $(12+10)\sqrt{3}=22\sqrt{3}$.
- $$2\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This subtracts the coefficients instead of adding them, producing $12-10=2$. The operation shown is addition, so the correct result is $22\sqrt{3}$.
- $$120\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This multiplies the coefficients $12$ and $10$ even though the terms are being added. Like surds are combined by adding coefficients, giving $22\sqrt{3}$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you simplify a surd with a coefficient, such as $4\sqrt{45}$?

**Back:** Simplify the root: $\sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$Multiply by the coefficient: $4 \times 3\sqrt{5} = 12\sqrt{5}$

**Choices:**

- Simplify the root: $\sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$Multiply by the coefficient: $4 \times 3\sqrt{5} = 12\sqrt{5}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Since $45=9\times5$, $\sqrt{45}=\sqrt{9}\sqrt{5}=3\sqrt{5}$. Multiplying this by the outside coefficient $4$ gives $12\sqrt{5}$.
- Simplify the root: $\sqrt{45} = 9\sqrt{5}$Multiply by the coefficient: $4 \times 9\sqrt{5} = 36\sqrt{5}$
  - _why:_ This takes $9$ outside the root without square-rooting it. Since $\sqrt{9}=3$, $\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5}$ and therefore $4\sqrt{45}=12\sqrt{5}$.
- Simplify the root: $\sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$Add the coefficient: $4 + 3\sqrt{5} = 7\sqrt{5}$
  - _why:_ The coefficient $4$ multiplies the surd rather than adding to its coefficient. As $\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5}$, the expression becomes $4\times3\sqrt{5}=12\sqrt{5}$.
- Simplify the root: $\sqrt{45} = 5\sqrt{3}$Multiply by the coefficient: $4 \times 5\sqrt{3} = 20\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly removes $5$ from the root, although $25$ is not a factor of $45$. Using $45=9\times5$ gives $\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5}$ and the result $12\sqrt{5}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the result of $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a}$?

**Back:** $$a$

**Choices:**

- $$a$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying a square root by itself reverses the square-root operation: $\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}=(\sqrt{a})^2=a$, for values where the square root is defined.
- $$a^2$
  - _why:_ This squares $a$ rather than squaring its square root. Since $(\sqrt{a})^2=a$, the product $\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}$ simplifies to $a$.
- $$2a$
  - _why:_ The factor $2$ usually comes from adding two identical terms, not multiplying them. Multiplying $\sqrt{a}$ by itself gives $(\sqrt{a})^2=a$.
- $$\sqrt{2a}$
  - _why:_ This treats multiplication like addition inside the root. The correct rule gives $\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}=\sqrt{a^2}=a$, assuming $a$ is non-negative.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** Two expressions such as $a+\sqrt{b}$ and $a-\sqrt{b}$ are known as [ ___ ].

**Back:** Two expressions such as $a+\sqrt{b}$ and $a-\sqrt{b}$ are known as conjugates.

**Choices:**

- conjugates ✔
  - _why:_ Conjugates contain the same two terms but have opposite signs between them. Thus $a+\sqrt{b}$ and $a-\sqrt{b}$ form a conjugate pair.
- reciprocals
  - _why:_ Reciprocals multiply to $1$, whereas these expressions differ only in the sign between their terms. They are called conjugates, and their product eliminates the surd cross-terms.
- factors
  - _why:_ Factors are expressions multiplied together to form a product, so the term does not describe this sign-changing relationship. The two expressions are known as conjugates.
- coefficients
  - _why:_ Coefficients are numerical or algebraic multipliers of terms. Expressions with identical terms and opposite signs, such as $a+\sqrt{b}$ and $a-\sqrt{b}$, are conjugates.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the simplified result of the product $(a+\sqrt{b})(a-\sqrt{b})$?

**Back:** $$a^2 - b$

**Choices:**

- $$a^2 - b$ ✔
  - _why:_ The expressions are conjugates, so the middle terms $-a\sqrt{b}$ and $+a\sqrt{b}$ cancel. The remaining terms are $a^2-(\sqrt{b})^2=a^2-b$.
- $$a^2 + b$
  - _why:_ The final terms multiply to $\sqrt{b}\times(-\sqrt{b})=-b$, not $+b$. After the middle terms cancel, the correct product is $a^2-b$.
- $$a^2 - 2b$
  - _why:_ The two middle terms cancel rather than combining to produce another $b$. Since $(\sqrt{b})^2=b$, the conjugate product simplifies to $a^2-b$.
- $$a - b^2$
  - _why:_ This squares the wrong parts of the product. Multiplying the first terms gives $a^2$, while multiplying the surd terms gives $-b$, producing $a^2-b$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** When squaring a bracket like $(3-\sqrt{5})^2$, why is it helpful to write it as two identical brackets?

**Back:** To ensure the middle terms are included in the expansion.

**Choices:**

- To ensure the middle terms are included in the expansion. ✔
  - _why:_ Writing $(3-\sqrt{5})^2$ as $(3-\sqrt{5})(3-\sqrt{5})$ makes both cross-products visible. These middle terms are essential and combine to give $-6\sqrt{5}$.
- To ensure the middle terms cancel in the expansion.
  - _why:_ The middle terms cancel when multiplying conjugates, but these brackets are identical rather than conjugate. Here both cross-products are negative, so they combine to give $-6\sqrt{5}$.
- To ensure the surd term is removed before the expansion.
  - _why:_ The surd should not be removed before expanding, and the final result still contains a surd term. Two brackets help ensure both middle cross-products are included.
- To ensure the constant term is not squared in the expansion.
  - _why:_ The constant term must be squared: $3\times3=9$. Writing two identical brackets is helpful because it displays both middle cross-products, preventing them from being omitted.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Expand and simplify $(5+\sqrt{6})(5-\sqrt{6})$.

**Back:** $$25 - 6 = 19$

**Choices:**

- $$25 - 6 = 19$ ✔
  - _why:_ The brackets are conjugates, so the middle terms cancel. The remaining product is $5^2-(\sqrt{6})^2=25-6=19$.
- $$25 + 6 = 31$
  - _why:_ The surd terms have opposite signs, so their product is $\sqrt{6}\times(-\sqrt{6})=-6$, not $+6$. Therefore the result is $25-6=19$.
- $$10 - 6 = 4$
  - _why:_ This doubles $5$ instead of multiplying $5$ by itself. The constant product is $5^2=25$, so the conjugate product is $25-6=19$.
- $$25 - 36 = -11$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly squares $6$ rather than squaring $\sqrt{6}$. Since $(\sqrt{6})^2=6$, the correct calculation is $25-6=19$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the objective of rationalising a denominator?

**Back:** To rewrite the fraction so there is no surd in the denominator.

**Choices:**

- To rewrite the fraction so there is no surd in the denominator. ✔
  - _why:_ Rationalising changes an irrational denominator into a rational one without changing the fraction’s value. The resulting denominator therefore contains no surd.
- To rewrite the fraction so there is no surd in the numerator.
  - _why:_ Rationalising concerns the denominator, and the numerator may still contain a surd afterwards. Its objective is to produce an equivalent fraction with no surd in the denominator.
- To rewrite the fraction so the denominator is equal to 1.
  - _why:_ Rationalising does not generally make the denominator $1$; it makes the denominator rational. The required equivalent fraction has no surd in its denominator.
- To rewrite the fraction so there are no fractions in the expression.
  - _why:_ The expression usually remains a fraction after rationalising. The process specifically removes surds from the denominator while preserving the fraction’s original value.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you rationalise a fraction with a single surd denominator $\frac{k}{\sqrt{a}}$?

**Back:** Multiply both the numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{a}$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply both the numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{a}$. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{a}}$ preserves the fraction’s value, while the denominator becomes $\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}=a$, which is rational.
- Multiply only the numerator by $\sqrt{a}$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying only the numerator changes the value of the fraction and leaves the denominator irrational. Both parts must be multiplied by $\sqrt{a}$, giving denominator $a$.
- Multiply only the denominator by $\sqrt{a}$.
  - _why:_ Although this would make the denominator $a$, changing only the denominator changes the fraction’s value. Multiply both numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{a}$ instead.
- Multiply both the numerator and denominator by $a$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $a$ leaves the denominator as $a\sqrt{a}$, which still contains a surd. Multiplying both parts by $\sqrt{a}$ makes the denominator equal to $a$.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** To rationalise a denominator with two terms, such as $3+\sqrt{2}$, you must multiply the numerator and denominator by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** To rationalise a denominator with two terms, such as $3+\sqrt{2}$, you must multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate.

**Choices:**

- conjugate ✔
  - _why:_ The conjugate of $3+sqrt{2}$ is $3-sqrt{2}$. Multiplying them produces $9-2=7$, eliminating the surd from the denominator.
- reciprocal
  - _why:_ A reciprocal swaps the numerator and denominator but does not necessarily remove a surd. The correct multiplier is the conjugate, which uses the opposite sign between the two terms.
- denominator
  - _why:_ Multiplying by the denominator itself gives $(3+sqrt{2})^2$, which still contains a surd term. The conjugate $3-sqrt{2}$ creates a difference of two squares.
- numerator
  - _why:_ The numerator is unrelated to the method for eliminating a two-term surd denominator. Multiplying by the conjugate makes the denominator rational through the difference-of-squares identity.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $\frac{12}{\sqrt{3}}$ fully.

**Back:** $$\frac{12\sqrt{3}}{3} = 4\sqrt{3}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{12\sqrt{3}}{3} = 4\sqrt{3}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying top and bottom by $sqrt{3}$ gives $frac{12sqrt{3}}{3}$. Dividing the coefficient $12$ by $3$ then produces the fully simplified result $4sqrt{3}$.
- $$frac{12sqrt{3}}{3} = 12sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ The denominator becomes $3$, so the coefficient must also be divided by $3$. Failing to perform this division gives $12sqrt{3}$ instead of the correct $4sqrt{3}$.
- $$frac{12sqrt{3}}{6} = 2sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $sqrt{3}$ by itself gives $3$, not $6$. Rationalising therefore gives $frac{12sqrt{3}}{3}=4sqrt{3}$, rather than halving the correct coefficient.
- $$frac{12}{3} = 4$
  - _why:_ This treats $sqrt{3}$ as though it were $3$. Rationalising requires multiplying by $sqrt{3}$, giving $frac{12sqrt{3}}{3}=4sqrt{3}$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify the algebraic surd expression $(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y})$.

**Back:** $$x - y$

**Choices:**

- $$x - y$ ✔
  - _why:_ The expression is a product of conjugates, so it follows $(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2$. Thus $(sqrt{x})^2-(sqrt{y})^2=x-y$.
- $$x + y$
  - _why:_ The signs of the two cross terms are opposite, so they cancel; the final term is negative. Applying the difference-of-squares identity gives $x-y$, not $x+y$.
- $$x - 2sqrt{xy} + y$
  - _why:_ This is the expansion of $(sqrt{x}-sqrt{y})^2$, not a product of conjugates. Here the cross terms cancel completely, leaving the correct result $x-y$.
- $$sqrt{x} - sqrt{y}$
  - _why:_ This leaves the factors essentially unexpanded and ignores that each square root is multiplied by itself. Full expansion cancels the cross terms and gives $x-y$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Expand and simplify $(3p+\sqrt{q})^2$.

**Back:** $$9p^2 + 6p\sqrt{q} + q$

**Choices:**

- $$9p^2 + 6p\sqrt{q} + q$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$, the terms are $(3p)^2=9p^2$, $2(3p)(sqrt{q})=6psqrt{q}$ and $(sqrt{q})^2=q$.
- $$9p^2 + q$
  - _why:_ Squaring a binomial requires the cross term $2ab$. Here it is $2(3p)(sqrt{q})=6psqrt{q}$, so the complete result is $9p^2+6psqrt{q}+q$.
- $$9p^2 + 3psqrt{q} + q$
  - _why:_ The middle term must include a factor of $2$ because both cross-products occur. Therefore it is $6psqrt{q}$, giving $9p^2+6psqrt{q}+q$.
- $$9p^2 + 6psqrt{q} + q^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring $sqrt{q}$ gives $q$, not $q^2$. The first and middle terms are correct, but the full expansion ends with $+q$.
