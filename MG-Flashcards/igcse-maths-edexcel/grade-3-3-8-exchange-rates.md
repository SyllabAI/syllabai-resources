# Exchange Rates Flashcards

## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] is the type of money used in a country.

**Back:** A currency is the type of money used in a country.

**Choices:**

- currency ✔
  - _why:_ Currency is the general term for the money officially used within a particular country, such as pounds in the UK or euros in many European countries.
- exchange rate
  - _why:_ An exchange rate compares the value of two currencies, rather than naming the money used in a country. The correct term is currency, meaning a country’s type of money.
- denomination
  - _why:_ A denomination is a specific value of a note or coin, such as £5 or £10. The general type of money used in a country is called currency.
- interest rate
  - _why:_ An interest rate describes the cost of borrowing or the return on savings. It does not name a country’s money; the correct term is currency.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** An exchange rate tells you how much [ ___ ].

**Back:** An exchange rate tells you how much one currency is worth in another currency.

**Choices:**

- one currency is worth in another currency ✔
  - _why:_ An exchange rate compares the value of one currency with another, showing how much of one currency is equivalent to a given amount of the other.
- one country spends in another country
  - _why:_ This describes spending across borders, not the value comparison between currencies. An exchange rate tells you how much one currency is worth in another currency.
- one currency weighs compared with another currency
  - _why:_ Currencies are compared by value rather than physical weight. The correct meaning is how much one currency is worth in another currency.
- one currency earns as interest in another currency
  - _why:_ Interest is an amount gained from savings or charged on borrowing, not the definition of an exchange rate. The rate compares the value of currencies.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** In an exchange rate, what tells you whether to multiply or divide?

**Back:** The currency with the 1.

**Choices:**

- The currency with the 1. ✔
  - _why:_ The currency written beside 1 is the reference currency. Converting away from it usually involves multiplying, while converting back to it involves dividing.
- The currency with the larger number.
  - _why:_ The larger number is not what determines the operation, because exchange rates can be greater or less than 1. Use the currency with the 1 as the reference.
- The currency being converted into.
  - _why:_ The destination currency does not by itself decide whether to multiply or divide. The key is identifying which currency has the value 1 in the exchange rate.
- The currency with the smaller value.
  - _why:_ The smaller numerical value may not be the reference currency and cannot reliably determine the operation. The currency with the 1 gives the correct direction.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** If £1 = €1.18, how do you convert pounds to euros?

**Back:** Multiply the pounds by 1.18.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the pounds by 1.18. ✔
  - _why:_ Since £1 is equal to €1.18, each pound produces 1.18 euros. Multiplying the number of pounds by 1.18 gives the equivalent amount in euros.
- Divide the pounds by 1.18.
  - _why:_ Dividing would convert euros back into pounds when £1 = €1.18. To convert pounds into euros, multiply the pounds by 1.18.
- Add the pounds to 1.18.
  - _why:_ Exchange rates scale an amount rather than adding a fixed number. For pounds to euros, multiply the pounds by 1.18 to account for the value of each pound.
- Multiply the pounds by 0.18.
  - _why:_ The factor 0.18 is only the extra amount above one euro equivalent, not the full conversion factor. The pounds must be multiplied by 1.18.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** At £1 = €1.18, convert £280 to euros.

**Back:** €330.40

**Choices:**

- €330.40 ✔
  - _why:_ Using £1 = €1.18, calculate $280 \times 1.18 = 330.40$. Therefore, £280 is worth €330.40.
- €237.29
  - _why:_ This comes from dividing 280 by 1.18, which reverses the required direction. Since pounds are being converted into euros, calculate $280 \times 1.18 = 330.40$.
- €329.20
  - _why:_ This is a near miss caused by using an inaccurate multiplication result. The correct calculation is $280 \times 1.18 = 330.40$, so the answer is €330.40.
- €281.18
  - _why:_ Adding the exchange rate instead of multiplying treats the rate as a fixed extra amount. Each pound becomes €1.18, so $280 \times 1.18 = 330.40$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** When £1 equals a foreign amount, converting foreign currency back to pounds means [ ___ ] by the exchange rate.

**Back:** When £1 equals a foreign amount, converting foreign currency back to pounds means dividing by the exchange rate.

**Choices:**

- dividing ✔
  - _why:_ When £1 equals a foreign amount, the foreign currency is the multiplied side of the rate. To return to pounds, divide the foreign amount by the exchange rate.
- multiplying
  - _why:_ Multiplication converts pounds into the foreign currency when the rate is written as £1 equal to a foreign amount. Converting back to pounds requires dividing by the rate.
- adding
  - _why:_ Adding the exchange rate does not scale the foreign amount according to the currency relationship. To convert foreign currency back to pounds, divide by the exchange rate.
- subtracting
  - _why:_ Subtracting the exchange rate does not reverse a currency conversion because the rate represents a multiplier. The correct operation for returning to pounds is division.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If £1 = €1.17, how do you convert euros to pounds?

**Back:** Divide the euros by 1.17.

**Choices:**

- Divide the euros by 1.17. ✔
  - _why:_ Because £1 equals €1.17, euros are the foreign amount relative to pounds. Dividing the euros by 1.17 converts them back into pounds.
- Multiply the euros by 1.17.
  - _why:_ Multiplication by 1.17 converts pounds into euros, not euros into pounds. To change euros back to pounds, divide the euros by 1.17.
- Divide the euros by 0.17.
  - _why:_ The value 0.17 is only the difference between 1.17 and 1, not the exchange rate. The euros must be divided by the full rate, 1.17.
- Subtract 1.17 from the euros.
  - _why:_ Subtracting a fixed amount does not account for the proportional relationship between euros and pounds. Divide the euros by 1.17 to convert them correctly.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** At £1 = €1.17, convert €95 to pounds to the nearest penny.

**Back:** £81.20

**Choices:**

- £81.20 ✔
  - _why:_ Convert euros to pounds by dividing: $95 \div 1.17 = 81.1965\ldots$. Rounding to the nearest penny gives £81.20.
- £111.15
  - _why:_ This results from multiplying 95 by 1.17, which converts in the wrong direction. Since euros must become pounds, calculate $95 \div 1.17 = 81.1965\ldots$, giving £81.20.
- £80.95
  - _why:_ Subtracting the difference between 1.17 and 1 from 95 is not a valid exchange-rate conversion. Dividing by 1.17 and rounding to the nearest penny gives £81.20.
- £81.19
  - _why:_ The unrounded result is £81.1965..., which rounds up because the third decimal digit is 6. Therefore, the nearest-penny answer is £81.20, not £81.19.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What does giving money to the nearest penny require?

**Back:** Write it to two decimal places.

**Choices:**

- Write it to two decimal places. ✔
  - _why:_ A penny is one hundredth of a pound, so an amount given to the nearest penny must have exactly two digits after the decimal point.
- Write it to one decimal place.
  - _why:_ One decimal place gives the amount to the nearest ten pence, not the nearest penny. The amount must be written to two decimal places.
- Write it to three decimal places.
  - _why:_ Three decimal places gives thousandths of a pound, equivalent to tenths of a penny. The nearest penny requires two decimal places.
- Write it to the nearest whole number.
  - _why:_ Rounding to a whole number gives the nearest pound and discards the pence. The nearest penny requires the amount to be written to two decimal places.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Back:** Do not memorise “foreign to pounds means divide”; always check which currency has the 1.

**Choices:**

- the 1 ✔
  - _why:_ The currency beside 1 determines the direction of the exchange rate. Use the rate according to which currency represents one complete unit.
- the 0
  - _why:_ Exchange rates are normally expressed using 1 unit of one currency, not 0 units. Check which currency has the 1 before choosing multiplication or division.
- the larger number
  - _why:_ Choosing an operation from the larger number is unreliable because exchange-rate values vary. The correct method is to identify which currency has the 1.
- the smaller number
  - _why:_ The smaller number does not determine whether to multiply or divide. Instead, inspect the exchange-rate statement and identify which currency has the 1.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Before comparing prices in different currencies, what must you do?

**Back:** Convert so both prices are in the same currency.

**Choices:**

- Convert so both prices are in the same currency. ✔
  - _why:_ Prices can only be compared fairly when they use the same unit. Convert one price so that both amounts are expressed in the same currency.
- Convert so both prices have the same number.
  - _why:_ A conversion should preserve value, not force the numerical amounts to match. Both prices must instead be expressed in the same currency before comparison.
- Round so both prices are to the nearest pound.
  - _why:_ Rounding does not resolve the fact that the prices use different currencies and may also reduce accuracy. Convert both prices into the same currency first.
- Calculate so both prices use a different exchange rate.
  - _why:_ Using different exchange rates would make the comparison inconsistent. Apply the given exchange rate so both prices are expressed in the same currency.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Choices:**

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** A jacket costs €54 abroad and £49.99 in the UK. If £1 = €1.20, where is it cheaper?

**Back:** Abroad, because €54 = £45.

**Choices:**

- Abroad, because €54 = £45. ✔
  - _why:_ Since £1 equals €1.20, convert euros to pounds by dividing: €54 ÷ 1.20 = £45. This is less than the UK price of £49.99.
- The UK, because €54 = £64.80.
  - _why:_ Multiplying €54 by 1.20 applies the exchange rate in the wrong direction. Dividing by 1.20 gives £45, so the jacket is cheaper abroad.
- The UK, because 54 is greater than 49.99.
  - _why:_ Comparing 54 and 49.99 directly ignores that they represent different currencies. Converting €54 gives £45, which means the jacket is cheaper abroad.
- Neither, because the prices cannot be compared.
  - _why:_ The prices can be compared after converting them into the same currency. €54 is £45 at this rate, so the jacket is cheaper abroad.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** After two prices are in the same currency, how do you find the difference?

**Back:** Subtract the smaller price from the larger price.

**Choices:**

- Subtract the smaller price from the larger price. ✔
  - _why:_ The positive difference between two prices is found by taking the smaller amount away from the larger amount after both have been converted to the same currency.
- Subtract the larger price from the smaller price.
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction produces a negative result rather than the usual positive price difference. Subtract the smaller price from the larger price.
- Add the smaller price to the larger price.
  - _why:_ Adding finds the total cost of the two items, not the difference between their prices. The smaller price must be subtracted from the larger price.
- Divide the larger price by the smaller price.
  - _why:_ Division finds a ratio showing how many times one price is another. To find the monetary difference, subtract the smaller price from the larger price.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** City B gives €1 = £0.86. How many euros does £1 give?

**Back:** €1.16 approximately.

**Choices:**

- €1.16 approximately. ✔
  - _why:_ Since €1 costs £0.86, divide £1 by 0.86: $1 \div 0.86 \approx 1.1628$. This rounds to approximately €1.16.
- €0.86 approximately.
  - _why:_ Using 0.86 directly confuses pounds per euro with euros per pound. The rate must be inverted, giving $1 \div 0.86 \approx €1.16$.
- €1.14 approximately.
  - _why:_ This value may be confused with a competing quoted rate, but it is not the reciprocal of 0.86. Dividing 1 by 0.86 gives approximately €1.16.
- €1.20 approximately.
  - _why:_ This rounds the result too generously. The calculation gives $1 \div 0.86 \approx 1.1628$, which rounds to €1.16 to the nearest cent.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Which gives more euros per £1: £1 = €1.14 or €1 = £0.86?

**Back:** €1 = £0.86 gives more euros per £1.

**Choices:**

- €1 = £0.86 gives more euros per £1. ✔
  - _why:_ Invert €1 = £0.86 to obtain $£1 = €(1 \div 0.86) \approx €1.16$. Since €1.16 exceeds €1.14, this rate gives more euros.
- £1 = €1.14 gives more euros per £1.
  - _why:_ Comparing 1.14 directly with 0.86 ignores that the second quote is pounds per euro. Its reciprocal is about €1.16 per £1, which is greater than €1.14.
- Both rates give the same euros per £1.
  - _why:_ The rates look close but are not equal. Converting €1 = £0.86 gives approximately €1.16 per £1, which exceeds €1.14 per £1.
- €1 = £0.86 gives fewer euros per £1.
  - _why:_ This reverses the comparison after conversion. The quote €1 = £0.86 is approximately €1.16 per £1, so it gives more, not fewer, euros.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a greatest number of whole notes, if the number of notes is a decimal, [ ___ ].

**Back:** For a greatest number of whole notes, if the number of notes is a decimal, round down.

**Choices:**

- round down ✔
  - _why:_ Only complete notes can be received. Rounding down discards the unusable fractional part and gives the greatest whole number that does not exceed the available money.
- round up
  - _why:_ Rounding up would require enough money for an additional complete note. To find the greatest affordable number of whole notes, the decimal result must be rounded down.
- round to the nearest whole number
  - _why:_ Ordinary rounding can increase the answer when the decimal part is at least 0.5, producing more notes than can be afforded. The result must always be rounded down.
- keep the decimal
  - _why:_ A decimal number of notes would imply receiving part of a note, which is not allowed. Keep only the greatest whole number by rounding down.

- 102.2 notes.
  - _why:_ The division gives 102.2, but 0.2 of a banknote cannot be received as a whole note. Rounding down gives a maximum of 102 notes.
- 10 notes.
  - _why:_ This loses a factor of ten when handling the zeros in 20 440 000 divided by 200 000. The quotient is 102.2, allowing 102 whole notes.
