## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines two 3D shapes as mathematically similar?

**Back:** Identical shapeMatching lengths enlarged/reduced by the same multiplier

**Choices:**

- Identical shapeMatching lengths enlarged/reduced by the same multiplier ✔
  - _why:_ Similar 3D shapes have the same form, and every pair of corresponding lengths has one common ratio. This shared multiplier is the linear scale factor.
- Identical shapeMatching lengths enlarged/reduced by the same amount
  - _why:_ Adding the same amount to each length does not preserve proportions and can change the shape. Corresponding lengths must instead be multiplied by the same scale factor.
- Identical volumeMatching lengths enlarged/reduced by a different multiplier
  - _why:_ Equal volumes do not guarantee identical shape, and different length multipliers distort proportions. Similar shapes require identical shape with every corresponding length changed by the same multiplier.
- Identical surface areaMatching lengths enlarged/reduced by the same amount
  - _why:_ Equal surface areas can occur for shapes that are not similar, while equal additions do not preserve proportions. Similarity requires identical shape and one common length multiplier.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the linear scale factor is $k$, then the area scale factor is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If the linear scale factor is $k$, then the area scale factor is $\boxed{k^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$k^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Area is two-dimensional, so both corresponding length dimensions are multiplied by $k$. Their combined effect gives an area scale factor of $k \times k = k^2$.
- $$k$
  - _why:_ Using the linear scale factor unchanged ignores that area has two dimensions. Each dimension scales by $k$, so the correct area scale factor is $k^2$.
- $$2k$
  - _why:_ Doubling the scale factor confuses repeated multiplication with addition. Two scaled dimensions give $k \times k$, so the correct area scale factor is $k^2$.
- $$k^3$
  - _why:_ Cubing the linear scale factor applies to volume because volume is three-dimensional. Area is two-dimensional, so its scale factor is $k^2$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the linear scale factor is $k$, then the volume scale factor is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If the linear scale factor is $k$, then the volume scale factor is $\boxed{k^3}$.

**Choices:**

- $$k^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Volume involves three dimensions, each multiplied by $k$. Multiplying their effects gives $k \times k \times k = k^3$ as the volume scale factor.
- $$k^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the scale factor accounts for only two dimensions and therefore applies to area. Volume has three dimensions, so the correct scale factor is $k^3$.
- $$3k$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $k$ by 3 confuses repeated multiplication with addition. Three scaled dimensions produce $k \times k \times k$, giving a volume scale factor of $k^3$.
- $$k^4$
  - _why:_ Raising $k$ to the fourth power incorrectly introduces an extra dimension. A volume has three dimensions, so its scale factor is $k^3$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** If the radius of a shape increases by a factor of 3, what is the area scale factor?

**Back:** $$9$ (since $3^2 = 9$)

**Choices:**

- $$9$ (since $3^2 = 9$) ✔
  - _why:_ Area scales with the square of the linear scale factor. A radius multiplier of 3 therefore gives an area scale factor of $3^2 = 9$.
- $$3$ (since $3^1 = 3$)
  - _why:_ Using the radius multiplier directly treats area as one-dimensional. Area scales with the square of the linear factor, so the correct scale factor is $3^2 = 9$.
- $$6$ (since $3 \times 2 = 6$)
  - _why:_ Multiplying the linear factor by the number of dimensions is not the area rule. The factor must be squared, giving $3^2 = 9$.
- $$27$ (since $3^3 = 27$)
  - _why:_ Cubing the linear scale factor gives the volume scale factor, not the area scale factor. Area uses the square, so the correct value is $3^2 = 9$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** If the height of a shape increases by a factor of 2, what is the volume scale factor?

**Back:** $$8$ (since $2^3 = 8$)

**Choices:**

- $$8$ (since $2^3 = 8$) ✔
  - _why:_ Volume is three-dimensional, so a linear scale factor of 2 is applied three times. The volume scale factor is therefore $2^3 = 8$.
- $$2$ (since $2^1 = 2$)
  - _why:_ Using the height multiplier directly treats volume as one-dimensional. For similar shapes, all three dimensions scale by 2, so the volume scale factor is $2^3 = 8$.
- $$4$ (since $2^2 = 4$)
  - _why:_ Squaring the linear scale factor gives the area scale factor, not the volume scale factor. Volume is three-dimensional, so the correct calculation is $2^3 = 8$.
- $$6$ (since $2 \times 3 = 6$)
  - _why:_ Multiplying the scale factor by three confuses repeated multiplication with addition. Volume requires $2 \times 2 \times 2$, producing the correct scale factor of 8.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the linear scale factor ($k$) calculated from the area scale factor?

**Back:** $$k = \sqrt{\text{area scale factor}}$

**Choices:**

- $$k = \sqrt{\text{area scale factor}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The area scale factor equals $k^2$, so reversing the relationship requires taking its positive square root. This gives the linear scale factor $k$.
- $$k = \left(\text{area scale factor}\right)^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring moves from a linear scale factor to an area scale factor rather than reversing the process. The linear scale factor is the square root of the area scale factor.
- $$k = \sqrt[3]{\text{area scale factor}}$
  - _why:_ A cube root reverses a volume scale factor because volume scales with $k^3$. Area scales with $k^2$, so its square root gives $k$.
- $$k = \dfrac{\text{area scale factor}}{2}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 2 treats the exponent as an ordinary multiplier. Since area scale factor equals $k^2$, the inverse operation is a square root, not division.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the linear scale factor ($k$) calculated from the volume scale factor?

**Back:** $$k = \sqrt[3]{\text{volume scale factor}}$

**Choices:**

- $$k = \sqrt[3]{\text{volume scale factor}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The volume scale factor equals $k^3$, so taking its cube root reverses the cubing and recovers the linear scale factor $k$.
- $$k = \sqrt{\text{volume scale factor}}$
  - _why:_ A square root reverses an area scale factor, which equals $k^2$. Volume scales with $k^3$, so the linear scale factor requires a cube root.
- $$k = \left(\text{volume scale factor}\right)^3$
  - _why:_ Cubing would increase the volume scale factor rather than recover the original length ratio. Since volume scale factor equals $k^3$, its cube root gives $k$.
- $$k = \dfrac{\text{volume scale factor}}{3}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 3 incorrectly treats the power as a multiplier. To reverse $k^3$, take the cube root of the volume scale factor.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** Surface area changes by the square of the scale factor because it is a [ ___ ] measurement, whereas volume uses the cube because it is 3D.

**Back:** Surface area changes by the square of the scale factor because it is a 2D measurement, whereas volume uses the cube because it is 3D.

**Choices:**

- 2D ✔
  - _why:_ Surface area measures a two-dimensional covering, so two scaled length dimensions are multiplied together. This is why its scale factor is the square of the linear factor.
- 1D
  - _why:_ A one-dimensional measurement describes length rather than surface area. Surface area extends in two dimensions, so it scales with the square of the linear scale factor.
- 3D
  - _why:_ Three-dimensional scaling applies to volume because volume involves length, width and height. Surface area is a two-dimensional measurement, so it uses the square of the scale factor.
- 4D
  - _why:_ Four-dimensional measurement is not involved in ordinary similar solids. Surface area has two dimensions, so its scale factor is $k^2$, while volume uses $k^3$.

**Back:** Surface area changes by the square of the scale factor because it is a 2D measurement, whereas volume uses the cube because it is 3D.

**Choices:**

- 1D

  - _why:_ Ordinary geometric solids have three spatial dimensions, not four. Volume is 3D, so corresponding volumes change according to the cube of the linear scale factor.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** When moving from a larger similar shape to a smaller similar shape, how do you use the scale factor?

**Back:** Divide by the scale factor (e.g. divide by $k$ for length, $k^2$ for area).

**Choices:**

- Divide by the scale factor (e.g. divide by $k$ for length, $k^2$ for area). ✔
  - _why:_ Moving from a larger shape to a smaller similar shape reverses the enlargement, so divide by the relevant length, area or volume scale factor.
- Multiply by the scale factor (e.g. multiply by $k$ for length, $k^2$ for area).
  - _why:_ Multiplication applies when moving from the smaller shape to the larger one. Moving from larger to smaller requires division by $k$, $k^2$ or $k^3$ as appropriate.
- Subtract the scale factor (e.g. subtract $k$ for length, $k^2$ for area).
  - _why:_ Subtracting a scale factor confuses multiplicative scaling with an additive change. Similar dimensions are found by dividing by $k$, $k^2$ or $k^3$ when moving to the smaller shape.
- Divide by the reciprocal scale factor (e.g. divide by $\frac{1}{k}$ for length, $\frac{1}{k^2}$ for area).
  - _why:_ Taking a reciprocal and then dividing is equivalent to multiplying by the original factor, so the measurement grows. Instead, divide directly by the relevant scale factor.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a frustum?

**Back:** The solid remaining when the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to the base.

**Choices:**

- The solid remaining when the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to the base. ✔
  - _why:_ A cut parallel to the base removes a smaller similar cone or pyramid. The portion left between the parallel faces is called a frustum.
- The solid remaining when the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off perpendicular to the base.
  - _why:_ A perpendicular cut does not generally produce the standard smaller similar solid or parallel circular faces. A frustum is formed by cutting parallel to the base.
- The solid remaining when the base of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to the top.
  - _why:_ Removing the bottom is not the standard construction described for a frustum. It is the solid left when the top is cut off parallel to the base.
- The solid remaining when the top of a sphere or hemisphere is cut off parallel to the base.
  - _why:_ A sphere has no flat base to which this cut can be parallel. A frustum comes from cutting the top off a cone or pyramid parallel to its base.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the volume of a frustum calculated using similar shapes?

**Back:** $$\text{Large cone volume} - \text{Small cone volume}$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{Large cone volume} - \text{Small cone volume}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The large cone can be viewed as the frustum plus the smaller similar cone removed from its top, so subtracting their volumes leaves the frustum.
- $$\text{Large cone volume} + \text{Small cone volume}$
  - _why:_ Adding counts the removed small cone as part of the required solid. The frustum is what remains, so subtract the small cone volume from the large cone volume.
- $$\text{Small cone volume} - \text{Large cone volume}$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction gives a negative value because the small cone has less volume. The correct order is large cone volume minus small cone volume.
- $$\text{Large cone volume} \div \text{Small cone volume}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the two volumes gives a ratio rather than the volume of the remaining solid. The frustum’s volume is found by subtracting the smaller volume from the larger.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the volume of a cone?

**Back:** $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cone has one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base radius and perpendicular height, giving $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \pi r^2h$
  - _why:_ Omitting the factor of $\frac{1}{3}$ gives the volume of the corresponding cylinder. A cone occupies one-third of that cylinder’s volume.
- $$V = \frac{1}{3}2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ The base is a circle with area $\pi r^2$, not $2\pi r$. Multiply that area by the height and by $\frac{1}{3}$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi rh^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the height instead of the radius uses the wrong dimensions for the circular base. The radius must be squared, giving $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ The cone formula uses the radius, while diagrams may supply the full width through the centre. That measurement is the diameter and must be halved.

- height
  - _why:_ The perpendicular height is used directly in the cone volume formula and is unrelated to finding the radius. It is the diameter that must be halved.

  - _why:_ The slant height runs along the cone’s surface and is not halved to obtain the radius. The diameter is the full width that must be halved.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** Before using the cone volume formula, you must check if you are given the diameter; if so, you must [ ___ ] it to find the radius.

**Back:** Before using the cone volume formula, you must check if you are given the diameter; if so, you must halve it to find the radius.

**Choices:**

- halve ✔
  - _why:_ The diameter spans two radii, so dividing it by two gives the radius required in $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- double
  - _why:_ Doubling the diameter makes the value four times the true radius, leading to a severely inflated value after squaring. The diameter must instead be halved.
- square
  - _why:_ Squaring the diameter does not convert it to a radius and also creates squared units. Divide the diameter by two, or halve it, to obtain the radius.
- square root
  - _why:_ Square-rooting has no role in converting diameter to radius. Since a diameter consists of two radii, it must be halved before substitution.

- Area scale factor $= 4$$k = \sqrt{4} = 2$ ✔

  - _why:_ Using the area scale factor directly as the linear scale factor ignores that areas scale with $k^2$. Since $k^2=4$, the correct linear scale factor is $k=2$.
- Area scale factor $= 2$$k = \sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ Dividing $200$ by $50$ gives $4$, not $2$, so the area scale factor has been calculated incorrectly. Taking its square root gives the correct linear scale factor, $k=2$.
- Area scale factor $= 4$$k = 4^2 = 16$
  - _why:_ Squaring the area scale factor reverses the required operation. Because area scales as $k^2$, take the square root of $4$ to obtain the linear scale factor $k=2$.

**Front:** If the volume scale factor is 27, by what factor does a length on the shape change?

**Back:** $$3$ (since $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$)

**Choices:**

- $$3$ (since $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$) ✔
  - _why:_ For similar solids, the volume scale factor is the cube of the linear scale factor. Therefore, the length factor is $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$.

  - _why:_ Raising $27$ to the power $2/3$ gives the area scale factor, not the length factor. Lengths change by $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$.
- $$27$ (using the volume factor directly)
  - _why:_ Using $27$ directly confuses the volume scale factor with the linear scale factor. Since volume scales with the cube of length, the length factor is $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$.
- $$\sqrt{27}$ (using the square root)
  - _why:_ Taking a square root is appropriate when converting an area scale factor to a length factor. For volume, take the cube root, giving $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** If the ratio of lengths in two similar solids is $2:5$, what is the ratio of their surface areas?

**Back:** $$4:25$ (since $2^2:5^2$)

**Choices:**

- $$4:25$ (since $2^2:5^2$) ✔
  - _why:_ Surface areas of similar solids scale with the square of the length ratio. Squaring both parts of $2:5$ gives $2^2:5^2=4:25$.
- $$2:5$ (using the length ratio directly)
  - _why:_ Using $2:5$ directly overlooks that surface area is two-dimensional. Squaring each part of the length ratio gives the correct surface-area ratio, $4:25$.
- $$8:125$ (since $2^3:5^3$)
  - _why:_ Cubing the length ratio gives the volume ratio, not the surface-area ratio. Surface area requires squaring, so $2^2:5^2=4:25$.
- $$4:10$ (since $2^2:2\times5$)
  - _why:_ Only the first part of the ratio has been squared correctly. Both corresponding lengths must be squared, producing $2^2:5^2=4:25$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** When finding the volume of a frustum, why are the large cone and the removed top cone considered mathematically similar?

**Back:** Because the top is cut parallel to the base, preserving the same shape and angles.

**Choices:**

- Because the top is cut parallel to the base, preserving the same shape and angles. ✔
  - _why:_ A cut parallel to the cone’s base creates a smaller cone with equal corresponding angles and proportional lengths. Therefore, the removed cone and original large cone are mathematically similar.
- Because the top is cut perpendicular to the base, preserving the same shape and angles.
  - _why:_ A perpendicular cut would pass vertically through the cone and would not form a smaller similar cone. The cut must be parallel to the base to preserve corresponding angles and proportions.
- Because the top is cut through the cone’s midpoint, preserving the same shape and angles.
  - _why:_ Passing through a midpoint does not by itself guarantee similarity or preserve the circular cross-section appropriately. Similarity follows specifically because the cutting plane is parallel to the cone’s base.
- Because the top is cut at the same radius as the base, preserving the same shape and angles.
  - _why:_ A removed top cone normally has a smaller radius than the original cone, so equal radii are not required. Similarity is guaranteed by making the cut parallel to the base.
