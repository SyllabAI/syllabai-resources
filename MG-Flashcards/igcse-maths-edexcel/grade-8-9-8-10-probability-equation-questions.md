## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What calculation is used to find the probability of two sequential outcomes along a specific path?

**Back:** Multiply the probabilities of the individual outcomes together.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the probabilities of the individual outcomes together. ✔
  - _why:_ Following one specific path means both sequential outcomes must occur. The multiplication rule therefore combines their individual probabilities to find the probability of the complete path.
- Add the probabilities of the individual outcomes together.
  - _why:_ Adding probabilities is used for mutually exclusive alternative outcomes, not successive events on one path. For two sequential outcomes, multiply the individual probabilities together.
- Subtract the probabilities of the individual outcomes.
  - _why:_ Subtraction may be used when finding a complement, but it does not combine sequential events. Along a specific path, multiply the probabilities of the individual outcomes together.
- Divide the probabilities of the individual outcomes.
  - _why:_ Division can arise in conditional probability calculations, but it is not the path rule. The probability of successive outcomes along one path is found by multiplying.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In without replacement questions, the denominator for the second pick is usually [ ___ ] than the first denominator.

**Back:** In without replacement questions, the denominator for the second pick is usually one less than the first denominator.

**Choices:**

- one less ✔
  - _why:_ Without replacement, the first selected item is not returned, so the total number of available items decreases by one before the second pick.
- one greater
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats the first pick as adding an item to the bag. Without replacement, one item is removed, so the second denominator is one less.
- the same
  - _why:_ Keeping the denominator unchanged is appropriate with replacement. Without replacement, the first item remains removed, making the denominator for the second pick one less.
- two less
  - _why:_ Only one item has been selected before the second pick, so reducing the denominator by two is an off-by-one error. It should be one less.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** A bag contains $x$ blue counters and 5 red counters. What is the total number of counters?

**Back:** $$x + 5$

**Choices:**

- $$x + 5$ ✔
  - _why:_ The total is found by adding the numbers of each colour. There are $x$ blue counters and 5 red counters, giving $x + 5$ altogether.
- $$x - 5$
  - _why:_ Subtracting would find a difference between the numbers of counters, not the total. Adding the $x$ blue and 5 red counters gives $x + 5$.
- $$5x$
  - _why:_ Multiplication would mean there are five groups of $x$ counters, which is not stated. The two separate quantities must be added, giving $x + 5$.
- $$rac{x}{5}$
  - _why:_ Dividing compares the number of blue counters with the number of red counters rather than finding the total. The total number is $x + 5$.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** In with replacement questions, the probabilities for the second pick [ ___ ] as the first pick.

**Back:** In with replacement questions, the probabilities for the second pick stay the same as the first pick.

**Choices:**

- stay the same ✔
  - _why:_ With replacement, the selected item is returned before the second pick. The bag’s composition is restored, so all probabilities remain unchanged.
- decrease by one
  - _why:_ This confuses the number of items with the probability and ignores replacement. Returning the selected item restores the original composition, so the probabilities stay the same.
- always increase
  - _why:_ Replacement does not increase the number or proportion of any type of item. It restores the original contents, so the second-pick probabilities stay the same.
- always decrease
  - _why:_ Probabilities decrease only in some without-replacement situations, depending on the first outcome. With replacement, the original contents are restored and the probabilities stay the same.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you represent the number of red and blue counters if their ratio is 3:1?

**Back:** Red: $3k$Blue: $k$

**Choices:**

- Red: $3k$Blue: $k$ ✔
  - _why:_ A ratio of 3:1 means the number of red counters is three equal parts and the number of blue counters is one part, represented by $3k$ and $k$.
- Red: $k$Blue: $3k$
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the ratio and represents red to blue as 1:3. For 3:1, red must be $3k$ and blue must be $k$.
- Red: $3k$Blue: $2k$
  - _why:_ These expressions give a red-to-blue ratio of 3:2 rather than 3:1. The blue quantity must be one equal part, so it should be $k$.
- Red: $4k$Blue: $k$
  - _why:_ These expressions represent a ratio of 4:1, adding an extra part to the red quantity. A 3:1 ratio is represented by $3k$ and $k$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** A bag has $x$ blue counters. If one blue counter is removed and not replaced, what is the new number of blue counters?

**Back:** $$x - 1$

**Choices:**

- $$x - 1$ ✔
  - _why:_ Removing one of the $x$ blue counters reduces the number of blue counters by exactly one. Because it is not replaced, the new number is $x - 1$.
- $$x + 1$
  - _why:_ Adding one would represent a blue counter being put into the bag rather than removed. Removing one without replacement leaves $x - 1$ blue counters.
- $$x$
  - _why:_ The number would remain $x$ only if the removed blue counter were replaced. Since it is not returned, the new number is $x - 1$.
- $$x - 2$
  - _why:_ Subtracting two assumes that two blue counters were removed. Only one blue counter is removed and not replaced, so the new number is $x - 1$.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** A probability equation is formed by setting an [ ___ ] equal to a given probability value.

**Back:** A probability equation is formed by setting an algebraic probability equal to a given probability value.

**Choices:**

- algebraic probability ✔
  - _why:_ An algebraic probability expresses the probability using an unknown, often derived from numbers of outcomes. Equating it to the stated probability creates an equation that can be solved.
- experimental frequency
  - _why:_ An experimental frequency is a count from observed trials, not the unknown probability expression used to form the equation. The required quantity is an algebraic probability.
- sample space
  - _why:_ A sample space lists possible outcomes rather than providing an expression to equate to a probability value. A probability equation begins with an algebraic probability.
- outcome total
  - _why:_ The total number of outcomes may form part of a probability fraction, but it is not the complete expression being equated. The equation uses an algebraic probability.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** A probability equation is formed by setting an algebraic probability equal to a [ ___ ] value.

**Back:** A probability equation is formed by setting an algebraic probability equal to a given probability value.

**Choices:**

- given probability ✔

- expected frequency
  - _why:_ Expected frequency is found by multiplying probability by the number of trials, so it is not itself the required probability value. The expression is set equal to the given probability.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If the ratio of red to blue counters is 2:1, what is the algebraic expression for the total number of counters?

**Back:** $$3k$ (where red is $2k$ and blue is $k$).

**Choices:**

- $$3k$ (where red is $2k$ and blue is $k$). ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio 2:1 has 3 equal parts altogether, so the counters can be represented by $2k$ red and $k$ blue, giving a total of $3k$.
- $$2k$ (where red is $k$ and blue is $k$).
  - _why:_ This treats the two ratio parts as equal and therefore loses the 2:1 relationship. The total must contain three parts, so the correct expression is $3k$.
- $$k$ (where red is $2k$ and blue is $k$).
  - _why:_ This gives only the blue portion rather than adding both colours. Since red contributes $2k$ and blue contributes $k$, the total is $3k$.
- $$4k$ (where red is $2k$ and blue is $2k$).
  - _why:_ This changes the ratio to 1:1 by making both colours $2k$. The ratio 2:1 requires $2k$ red and $k$ blue, producing a total of $3k$.

**Back:** Probabilities cannot be negative.The number of counters must be positive.

**Choices:**

- Probabilities cannot be negative.The number of counters must be positive. ✔
  - _why:_ A probability cannot be negative, and a bag containing counters must have a positive number of counters. Therefore, only the positive square-root result is valid.
- Probabilities must be negative.The number of counters may be zero.
  - _why:_ Probabilities are never negative, and a bag described as containing counters cannot have zero counters. The valid result is selected because probabilities are non-negative and the counter count is positive.
- Probabilities must be greater than one.The number of counters must be even.
  - _why:_ Probabilities range from 0 to 1, not above 1, and there is no requirement that the number of counters be even. The positive root is chosen for the stated probability and count restrictions.
- Probabilities can be negative.The number of counters must be positive.
  - _why:_ Although a counter total must be positive, probabilities cannot be negative. The negative square-root result must therefore be rejected, leaving only the positive root.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** When a probability equation results in a quadratic, you must [ ___ ] any solutions that are negative or non-integers.

**Back:** When a probability equation results in a quadratic, you must reject any solutions that are negative or non-integers.

**Choices:**

- reject ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic may produce algebraic solutions that are negative or non-integers, but these cannot represent valid counter counts. Such solutions must be rejected.
- accept
  - _why:_ Accepting every quadratic solution could include negative or fractional counter counts, which are impossible in a probability model. These invalid solutions must be rejected.
- round
  - _why:_ Rounding an invalid solution can change the equation and create a value that was not actually obtained. Negative or non-integer solutions should be rejected rather than rounded.
- double
  - _why:_ Changing the size of a solution does not test whether it represents a valid number of counters. Negative or non-integer solutions must be rejected altogether.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** When a probability equation results in a quadratic, you must reject any solutions that are [ ___ ] or non-integers.

**Back:** When a probability equation results in a quadratic, you must reject any solutions that are negative or non-integers.

**Choices:**

- negative ✔
  - _why:_ A number of counters cannot be negative, so any negative quadratic solution is impossible in this context. Non-integer solutions must also be rejected because counters are counted in whole numbers.
- positive

- zero
  - _why:_ Zero is not generally the category that must be rejected; it can sometimes be a valid boundary value depending on the model. Negative values are impossible for numbers of counters.
- fractional
  - _why:_ Fractional values are already covered by non-integers, so this does not identify the separate impossible category. The missing category is negative solutions, which cannot represent counters.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** A bag has $n$ counters; 6 are red and the rest are blue. What is the expression for the number of blue counters?

**Back:** $$n - 6$

**Choices:**

- $$n - 6$ ✔
  - _why:_ The total number of counters is $n$, and 6 of them are red. Subtracting the red counters from the total leaves $n - 6$ blue counters.
- $$n + 6$
  - _why:_ Adding the red counters to the total double-counts them rather than removing them. The blue counters are what remains after subtracting 6 from $n$, giving $n - 6$.
- $$6 - n$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction usually gives a negative value when the total exceeds 6, which cannot represent blue counters. The correct remainder is found with $n - 6$.
- $$\frac{n}{6}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 6 does not separate the red counters from the total; it changes the scale instead. Removing the 6 red counters requires subtraction, so the expression is $n - 6$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** The ratio of red:blue is 2:1. Two counters are picked without replacement. Write the expression for $P(\text{red then red})$.

**Back:** $$$\frac{2k}{3k} \times \frac{2k - 1}{3k - 1}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{2k}{3k} \times \frac{2k - 1}{3k - 1}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Initially, the probability of red is $\frac{2k}{3k}$. After one red is removed, $2k-1$ red counters remain out of $3k-1$ total counters, giving the product shown.
- $$$\frac{2k}{3k} \times \frac{2k}{3k - 1}$$
  - _why:_ This forgets that the first red counter has been removed, so the second red count should decrease as well as the total. The correct second factor is $\frac{2k-1}{3k-1}$.
- $$$\frac{2k}{3k} \times \frac{2k - 1}{3k}$$
  - _why:_ Without replacement, the total number of counters decreases after the first pick, so the second denominator cannot remain $3k$. It must become $3k-1$, giving the stated product.
- $$$\frac{k}{3k} \times \frac{k - 1}{3k - 1}$$
  - _why:_ This uses the blue count $k$ instead of the red count $2k$, calculating the probability of blue then blue. Red then red requires $2k$ red counters initially.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Why might a solution like $n = 4$ be rejected if a bag is known to contain 6 red counters?

**Back:** The total number of counters ($n$) cannot be less than the number of red counters already in the bag.

**Choices:**

- The total number of counters ($n$) cannot be less than the number of red counters already in the bag. ✔
  - _why:_ The total includes all 6 red counters, so it must be at least 6. Since 4 is less than 6, it cannot be a possible value for $n$.
- The total number of counters ($n$) cannot be greater than the number of red counters already in the bag.
  - _why:_ A bag may contain blue counters as well as the 6 red counters, so its total can be greater than 6. The total cannot be less than the number of red counters.
- The total number of counters ($n$) must be equal to the number of red counters already in the bag.
  - _why:_ This would incorrectly assume that the bag contains no blue counters. The total may exceed 6, but it cannot be below 6 because all red counters are included in the total.
- The total number of counters ($n$) cannot be less than or equal to the number of red counters already in the bag.
  - _why:_ A total of exactly 6 is possible if every counter is red, so equality is not forbidden. Only totals below 6 are impossible because the bag already contains 6 red counters.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Back:** To solve $\frac{x(x - 1)}{(x + 3)(x + 2)} = \frac{5}{14}$, the first step is to cross-multiply the fractions.

**Choices:**

- cross-multiply ✔
  - _why:_ Cross-multiplication removes the two denominators by multiplying opposite numerator and denominator terms, producing an equation that can then be expanded and rearranged into a quadratic.
- factor
  - _why:_ Factoring is normally used after the equation has been rearranged into a suitable polynomial form. The first step here is to remove the fractions by cross-multiplying.
- simplify
  - _why:_ The fractions cannot be cancelled directly because their factors are not common across the equality. Cross-multiplication is the initial operation used to eliminate the denominators.
- add
  - _why:_ Adding the fractions would not correctly preserve the equation or remove the denominators. Multiplying opposite sides across the equality is the required first step.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** If the first pick has a probability of $\frac{x}{x+3}$, what is the probability of picking the same item again without replacement?

**Back:** $$$\frac{x - 1}{x + 2}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{x - 1}{x + 2}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ After the first item is selected, one matching item and one total item have been removed. The second probability is therefore $(x-1)/(x+2)$.
- $$$\frac{x}{x + 2}$$
  - _why:_ This reduces the total number of items but not the number of matching items. Without replacement, both quantities decrease by one, giving $(x-1)/(x+2)$.

- $$$\frac{x}{x + 3}$$
  - _why:_ This simply repeats the first-pick probability and treats the selection as though replacement occurred. Without replacement, the second probability is $(x-1)/(x+2)$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue counters. Find $P(\text{blue then blue})$ without replacement.

**Back:** $$$\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{6}{56}$$ (or $\frac{3}{28}$)

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{6}{56}$$ (or $\frac{3}{28}$) ✔
  - _why:_ The first blue probability is $3/8$. Without replacement, two blue counters remain among seven counters, so multiplication gives $6/56=3/28$.
- $$$\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{9}{64}$$ (or $\frac{9}{64}$)
  - _why:_ Using $3/8$ for both picks incorrectly assumes replacement. Without replacement, the second probability is $2/7$, so the required result is $6/56=3/28$.
- $$$\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{2}{8} = \frac{6}{64}$$ (or $\frac{3}{32}$)
  - _why:_ The blue numerator has decreased correctly, but the denominator must also decrease because only seven counters remain. The correct product is $3/8\times2/7=3/28$.
- $$$\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{3}{7} = \frac{9}{56}$$ (or $\frac{9}{56}$)
  - _why:_ The denominator reflects the removal, but the numerator incorrectly leaves three blue counters available. Only two remain, giving $3/8\times2/7=3/28$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify the algebraic fraction $\frac{2k}{3k}$ before solving a probability equation.

**Back:** $$$\frac{2}{3}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{2}{3}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The common non-zero factor $k$ cancels from the numerator and denominator: $2k/(3k)=2/3$. This simplifies the later probability equation.
- $$$\frac{2k}{3}$$
  - _why:_ This cancels $k$ from only the denominator rather than from both parts of the fraction. Cancelling the common factor completely gives $2/3$.
- $$$\frac{2}{3k}$$
  - _why:_ This removes $k$ from the numerator but incorrectly retains it in the denominator. The same factor must cancel from both, leaving $2/3$.
- $$$\frac{k}{3}$$
  - _why:_ Cancelling the coefficients instead of the shared factor $k$ changes the fraction’s value. Only $k$ cancels, so the simplified fraction is $2/3$.

## Card 23 — `basic`

**Front:** A bag has 4 red and $y$ blue counters. Two are picked with replacement. Write the expression for $P(\text{red then red})$.

**Back:** $$$\left(\frac{4}{y + 4}\right)^2$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\left(\frac{4}{y + 4}\right)^2$$ ✔
  - _why:_ There are four red counters among $y+4$ counters. Replacement leaves the probability unchanged for the second pick, so the probability is the square of $4/(y+4)$.
- $$$\frac{4}{y + 4} \times \frac{3}{y + 3}$$
  - _why:_ This uses the without-replacement probabilities by reducing both counts after the first pick. With replacement, both red-pick probabilities remain $4/(y+4)$.
- $$$\left(\frac{y}{y + 4}\right)^2$$
  - _why:_ This calculates the probability of two blue counters rather than two red counters. For red then red, the repeated probability is $4/(y+4)$.
- $$$\frac{16}{y + 4}$$
  - _why:_ Squaring a fraction requires squaring both its numerator and denominator. The denominator must be $(y+4)^2$, giving $(4/(y+4))^2$.
