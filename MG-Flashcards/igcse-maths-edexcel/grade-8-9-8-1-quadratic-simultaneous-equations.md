# Quadratic Simultaneous Equations Flashcards

## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** Graphically, what do the solutions to linear and quadratic simultaneous equations represent?

**Back:** The points of intersection between the line and the curve.

**Choices:**

- The points of intersection between the line and the curve. ✔
  - _why:_ A solution satisfies both equations simultaneously, so its coordinates lie on both graphs. Such coordinates are precisely the points where the line and quadratic curve intersect.
- The turning points of the line and the curve.
  - _why:_ A turning point describes where a curve changes direction, and a straight line has no turning point. The solutions are the points where the two graphs intersect.
- The points of intersection between the curve and the axes.
  - _why:_ Intercepts with the axes solve the quadratic together with $x=0$ or $y=0$, not with the given linear equation. The required solutions are where the line meets the curve.
- The midpoints between the line and the curve.
  - _why:_ A midpoint is calculated from two known coordinates and need not lie on either graph. Simultaneous solutions are the points that lie on both the line and the curve.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why do quadratic simultaneous equations typically result in two solutions?

**Back:** Because a straight line can intersect a quadratic curve (like a circle) at two distinct points.

**Choices:**

- Because a straight line can intersect a quadratic curve (like a circle) at two distinct points. ✔
  - _why:_ Each intersection satisfies both equations. A line can cross a quadratic curve in two places, producing two distinct coordinate pairs that solve the simultaneous equations.
- Because every quadratic equation must have two distinct roots.
  - _why:_ A quadratic can instead have one repeated real root or no real roots, depending on its discriminant. Two solutions typically arise when the line intersects the curve twice.
- Because each coordinate pair can be written in two different orders.
  - _why:_ Coordinates cannot be reversed freely because $(x,y)$ and $(y,x)$ usually represent different points. Two solutions arise from two distinct intersections of the line and curve.
- Because a straight line always crosses the coordinate axes at two distinct points.
  - _why:_ Axis intercepts are unrelated to whether the line meets the quadratic curve. The simultaneous equations have two solutions when their graphs intersect at two distinct points.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step in solving a quadratic simultaneous equation algebraically?

**Back:** Rearrange the linear equation to make one variable the subject.

**Choices:**

- Rearrange the linear equation to make one variable the subject. ✔
  - _why:_ Isolating one variable in the linear equation creates an expression that can be substituted into the quadratic equation, leaving a single equation in one unknown.
- Rearrange the quadratic equation to make both variables the subject.
  - _why:_ A quadratic equation usually cannot make both variables the subject in a useful single expression. Instead, rearrange the simpler linear equation to isolate one variable for substitution.
- Factorise the linear equation to find both variables immediately.
  - _why:_ A linear equation is not normally solved by factorisation and cannot determine both variables alone. Rearrange it to make one variable the subject before substituting.
- Add the two equations to eliminate every squared term.
  - _why:_ Adding the equations does not generally eliminate the squared term because only the quadratic equation contains it. First isolate a variable in the linear equation and substitute.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** Substitution turns two equations into one equation with [ ___ ].

**Back:** Substitution turns two equations into one equation with one unknown.

**Choices:**

- one unknown ✔
  - _why:_ Replacing one variable using the rearranged linear equation removes that variable from the quadratic equation, producing a single equation containing only one unknown.
- two unknowns
  - _why:_ Leaving two unknowns means the substitution has not fully replaced one variable. Correct substitution produces one equation in one unknown, which can then be solved.
- three unknowns
  - _why:_ Substitution does not introduce an additional variable; it replaces one variable with an equivalent expression. The resulting equation therefore contains one unknown, not three.
- no unknowns
  - _why:_ Substitution removes one of the two variables, but the remaining variable must still be found. It therefore creates an equation with one unknown rather than none.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How are the matching $y$-values found once the $x$-values are solved?

**Back:** Substitute each $x$-value into the rearranged linear equation.

**Choices:**

- Substitute each $x$-value into the rearranged linear equation. ✔
  - _why:_ The rearranged linear equation expresses $y$ directly in terms of $x$. Substituting each solved $x$-value therefore gives its corresponding $y$-value unambiguously.
- Substitute each $x$-value into the original quadratic equation.
  - _why:_ Using the quadratic equation may produce multiple possible $y$-values, including values that do not satisfy the line. The rearranged linear equation gives the matching value directly.
- Substitute each $x$-value into the quadratic formula.
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula is used to solve a quadratic for its unknown, not to recover the corresponding second coordinate. Use the rearranged linear equation to find each $y$-value.
- Substitute each $x$-value into the discriminant expression.
  - _why:_ The discriminant indicates the number and type of quadratic roots but does not calculate matching coordinates. Substitute each root into the rearranged linear equation instead.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** In what format should final solutions to simultaneous equations be given?

**Back:** As coordinate pairs $(x, y)$.

**Choices:**

- As coordinate pairs $(x, y)$. ✔
  - _why:_ Each solution consists of an $x$-value and its corresponding $y$-value, so writing $(x,y)$ keeps the values correctly matched as a point satisfying both equations.
- As separate lists $x, y$.
  - _why:_ Separate lists can lose the matching between each $x$-value and its corresponding $y$-value. Each complete solution should be written as an ordered coordinate pair $(x,y)$.
- As unordered pairs $(x, y)$.
  - _why:_ The order matters because the first coordinate is $x$ and the second is $y$. Solutions must therefore be given as ordered coordinate pairs, not unordered pairs.
- As single values $(x+y)$.
  - _why:_ Combining the coordinates into one sum loses the individual values and does not identify a point. Each solution must retain both values as a coordinate pair $(x,y)$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Explain the common mistake involving square roots and the equation $x^2 + y^2 = 10$.

**Back:** Incorrectly assuming $x + y = \sqrt{10}$; square roots do not distribute across addition.

**Choices:**

- Incorrectly assuming $x + y = \sqrt{10}$; square roots do not distribute across addition. ✔
  - _why:_ In general, $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is not equal to $x+y$. The entire sum is square-rooted, so the original equation cannot be simplified in this way.
- Incorrectly assuming $x^2 + y^2 = 100$; square roots must be squared across addition.
  - _why:_ Squaring the right-hand side without applying an equivalent operation to the left changes the equation. The common error is instead treating $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ as $x+y$.
- Incorrectly assuming $x + y = 10$; exponents do not distribute across addition.
  - _why:_ Simply removing the squares is invalid, but it is not the stated square-root misconception. The common error gives $x+y=\sqrt{10}$ by distributing the square root incorrectly.
- Incorrectly assuming $xy = \sqrt{10}$; square roots do not convert addition into multiplication.
  - _why:_ Taking a square root does not turn a sum into a product. The usual misconception is that it distributes across the sum, leading incorrectly to $x+y=\sqrt{10}$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the quadratic equation $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ represent graphically?

**Back:** A circle centred at the origin $(0,0)$.

**Choices:**

- A circle centred at the origin $(0,0)$. ✔
  - _why:_ All points satisfying $x^2+y^2=r^2$ are a distance $r$ from $(0,0)$ by Pythagoras, which is exactly the definition of a circle.
- An ellipse centred at the origin $(0,0)$.
  - _why:_ An ellipse generally has differently scaled $x^2$ and $y^2$ terms. Equal coefficients produce points at the same radius $r$ from the origin, forming a circle.
- A parabola with its vertex at the origin $(0,0)$.
  - _why:_ A parabola typically has only one variable squared, whereas both variables are squared and added here. This equation describes a circle centred at the origin.
- A circle centred at the point $(r,r)$.
  - _why:_ A circle centred at $(r,r)$ would involve $(x-r)^2+(y-r)^2$. With no horizontal or vertical shifts, the centre is the origin $(0,0)$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If a question requires answers to 3 significant figures, what does this imply about the quadratic?

**Back:** The quadratic will not factorise, and you must use the quadratic formula.

**Choices:**

- The quadratic will not factorise, and you must use the quadratic formula. ✔
  - _why:_ A requirement for 3 significant figures indicates that the roots are not expected to be exact factorisable values, so the quadratic formula is needed to calculate decimal solutions.
- The quadratic will factorise, and you must solve it by factorising.
  - _why:_ Factorising normally produces exact roots, so answers would not need to be requested to 3 significant figures. The quadratic will not factorise, requiring the quadratic formula instead.
- The quadratic has a repeated root, and you must use completing the square.
  - _why:_ A repeated root is a special case with a discriminant of zero, not something implied by decimal rounding. The expected implication is non-factorisation and use of the quadratic formula.
- The quadratic has integer roots, and you must use inspection.
  - _why:_ Integer roots would usually allow straightforward factorisation and exact answers. A request for 3 significant figures instead suggests non-factorisable roots calculated using the quadratic formula.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the number $-0.120$, the final zero is [ ___ ].

**Back:** In the number $-0.120$, the final zero is significant.

**Choices:**

- significant ✔
  - _why:_ A trailing zero to the right of a decimal follows non-zero digits and shows the stated precision. It therefore counts as a significant figure.
- not significant
  - _why:_ Trailing zeros after a decimal point are counted because they communicate precision. The final zero in this decimal is significant, giving the number three significant figures.
- a placeholder
  - _why:_ Placeholder zeros occur before the first non-zero digit, such as the zeros in 0.0012. Here, the final zero follows non-zero digits and is significant.
- ignored
  - _why:_ Only leading zeros before the first non-zero digit are ignored when counting significant figures. The final zero follows the digits 1 and 2, so it is significant.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** In the expansion of $(5 - 4x)^2$, what are the resulting three terms?

**Back:** $$25 - 40x + 16x^2$

**Choices:**

- $$25 - 40x + 16x^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$ gives $5^2-2(5)(4x)+(4x)^2$, which simplifies to $25-40x+16x^2$.
- $$25 - 20x + 16x^2$
  - _why:_ The middle term must include the factor of 2 from $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$. Therefore it is $-40x$, and the correct expansion is $25-40x+16x^2$.
- $$25 + 40x + 16x^2$
  - _why:_ The subtraction inside the bracket makes the cross-term negative: $2(5)(-4x)=-40x$. The correct expansion is therefore $25-40x+16x^2$.
- $$25 - 40x - 16x^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring $-4x$ gives positive $16x^2$, not a negative term. Including the negative cross-term, the correct expansion is $25-40x+16x^2$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the expression $2x^2 - (1 - 6x + 9x^2)$, the negative sign must be applied to [ ___ ] inside the bracket.

**Back:** In the expression $2x^2 - (1 - 6x + 9x^2)$, the negative sign must be applied to every term inside the bracket.

**Choices:**

- every term ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting a bracket is equivalent to multiplying the entire bracket by $-1$. Consequently, each term changes sign when the bracket is removed.
- only the first term
  - _why:_ The minus sign represents multiplication of the whole bracket by $-1$, not just its first entry. It must therefore be applied to every term inside the bracket.
- only the middle term
  - _why:_ Applying the outside minus to only the middle term leaves the constant and quadratic signs unchanged incorrectly. Every term in the bracket must have its sign reversed.
- only the final term
  - _why:_ The minus sign before a bracket distributes across the entire expression, rather than only the last entry. Every term inside the bracket must change sign.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic equation must be equal to [ ___ ] before using the quadratic formula or factorising.

**Back:** A quadratic equation must be equal to 0 before using the quadratic formula or factorising.

**Choices:**

- 0 ✔
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula and standard factorisation apply to an equation written as $ax^2+bx+c=0$. All terms must therefore be collected on one side first.
- 1
  - _why:_ Setting a quadratic equal to 1 does not place it in the required standard form. Rearrange all terms so that one side equals 0 before solving.
- x
  - _why:_ Leaving $x$ on the other side means the equation has not yet been arranged in standard quadratic form. Move it across so that the equation equals 0.
- -1
  - _why:_ The required standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$, not an equation equal to a chosen negative constant. Rearrange the constant so that one side equals 0.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the value under the square root in the quadratic formula is negative, the line and curve [ ___ ].

**Back:** If the value under the square root in the quadratic formula is negative, the line and curve do not intersect.

**Choices:**

- do not intersect ✔
  - _why:_ A negative discriminant gives no real solutions for the intersection equation. Graphically, this means there are no real coordinate points shared by the line and curve.
- intersect at two points
  - _why:_ Two intersections correspond to two distinct real roots and require a positive discriminant. A negative discriminant gives no real roots, so the line and curve do not intersect.
- touch at exactly one point
  - _why:_ Touching once represents a repeated real root, which occurs when the discriminant equals zero. A negative discriminant instead means the line and curve do not intersect.
- intersect at the origin
  - _why:_ Passing through the origin would require $(0,0)$ to satisfy both equations and is unrelated to a negative discriminant. A negative value means there are no intersections.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is indicated graphically if the quadratic equation results in a repeated root?

**Back:** The line is a tangent to the curve (they touch at exactly one point).

**Choices:**

- The line is a tangent to the curve (they touch at exactly one point). ✔
  - _why:_ A repeated root gives one shared $x$-coordinate counted twice. Graphically, the line meets the curve at exactly one point without crossing it, making the line tangent.
- The line is a secant to the curve (they cross at exactly two points).
  - _why:_ A secant produces two distinct intersection points and therefore two distinct real roots. A repeated root gives only one point of contact, so the line is tangent.
- The line is parallel to the curve (they never meet).
  - _why:_ No meeting points would mean there are no real roots, rather than a repeated root. A repeated root means one point of contact, making the line tangent.
- The line is a normal to the curve (they meet at a right angle).
  - _why:_ A normal concerns a line perpendicular to a curve’s tangent and is not implied by a repeated root. One repeated intersection means the given line is tangent.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** To solve [ ___ ] means to use methods like substitution and rearranging rather than using a graph. house.

**Back:** To solve algebraically means to use methods like substitution and rearranging rather than using a graph. house.

**Choices:**

- algebraically ✔
  - _why:_ Substitution, elimination and rearrangement manipulate equations using mathematical symbols and operations. These are algebraic methods, as opposed to reading intersections from a graph.
- graphically
  - _why:_ A graphical solution is obtained by plotting both relations and reading their intersection points. Substitution and rearrangement instead mean solving algebraically.
- numerically
  - _why:_ Numerical methods approximate solutions through repeated calculations or trials. Substitution and exact rearrangement are algebraic procedures, so the required term is algebraically.
- iteratively
  - _why:_ Iterative solving repeatedly applies a formula to approach an approximate solution. Direct substitution and rearrangement are instead methods of solving algebraically.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** The denominator of the quadratic formula is [ ___ ].

**Back:** The denominator of the quadratic formula is $2a$.

**Choices:**

- $$2a$ ✔
  - _why:_ In $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, the quadratic formula divides the entire numerator $-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}$ by $2a$.
- $$2b$
  - _why:_ This confuses the linear coefficient with the quadratic coefficient. The denominator is $2a$, where $a$ is the coefficient of $x^2$.
- $$a$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $2$ in the quadratic formula. The whole numerator must be divided by $2a$, not merely by $a$.
- $$4a$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly doubles the denominator’s numerical factor. The denominator of the quadratic formula is $2a$, while the discriminant contains the factor $4ac$.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** The part of the quadratic formula inside the square root is [ ___ ].

**Back:** The part of the quadratic formula inside the square root is $b^2 - 4ac$.

**Choices:**

- $$b^2 - 4ac$ ✔
  - _why:_ The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is the discriminant. It appears beneath the square root in the quadratic formula and determines the number of real roots.
- $$b^2 + 4ac$
  - _why:_ The sign between the terms has been changed incorrectly. The discriminant beneath the square root is $b^2 - 4ac$, with subtraction rather than addition.
- $$b - 4ac$
  - _why:_ This fails to square the coefficient $b$. The expression inside the square root is $b^2 - 4ac$, not $b - 4ac$.
- $$4ac - b^2$
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the subtraction and therefore changes the discriminant’s sign. The quadratic formula uses $b^2 - 4ac$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Rearrange $x - y = 3$ to make $y$ the subject.

**Back:** $$y = x - 3$

**Choices:**

- $$y = x - 3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Adding $y$ to both sides and subtracting $3$ from both sides isolates $y$, giving $y = x - 3$.
- $$y = x + 3$
  - _why:_ The constant’s sign has been changed incorrectly. From $x - y = 3$, isolating $y$ gives $y = x - 3$, not an addition of $3$.
- $$y = 3 - x$
  - _why:_ This results from moving terms without changing both signs consistently. Multiplying $-y = 3 - x$ by $-1$ gives $y = x - 3$.
- $$y = -x - 3$
  - _why:_ Both terms have been given incorrect signs. Rearranging produces $-y = 3 - x$, so multiplying throughout by $-1$ gives $y = x - 3$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Rearrange $2y = 1 - 3x$ to make $y$ the subject.

**Back:** $$y = \frac{1 - 3x}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$y = \frac{1 - 3x}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing every term in $2y = 1 - 3x$ by $2$ isolates $y$, giving $y = \frac{1 - 3x}{2}$.
- $$y = 1 - 3x$
  - _why:_ This forgets to divide the right-hand side by $2$. Since the left side is $2y$, both terms on the right must be halved.
- $$y = \frac{1}{2} - 3x$
  - _why:_ Only the constant has been divided by $2$, leaving the $x$ term unchanged. Every term must be divided, giving $y = \frac{1 - 3x}{2}$.
- $$y = \frac{1 + 3x}{2}$
  - _why:_ The sign of the $3x$ term has been changed unnecessarily. Division by $2$ does not alter its sign, so the numerator remains $1 - 3x$.
