## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the defining feature of a quadratic equation?

**Back:** The highest power of the variable is 2 (usually in the form $ax^2+bx+c=0$).

**Choices:**

- The highest power of the variable is 2 (usually in the form $ax^2+bx+c=0$). ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic equation has degree 2, meaning its greatest variable exponent is 2. Its standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$, where $a\ne0$.
- The highest power of the variable is 1 (usually in the form $ax+b=0$).
  - _why:_ A highest power of 1 defines a linear equation, not a quadratic equation. A quadratic’s highest variable power is 2, as in $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- The highest power of the variable is 3 (usually in the form $ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0$).
  - _why:_ A highest power of 3 defines a cubic equation. A quadratic instead has degree 2 and is usually written as $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- The equation contains exactly 2 terms involving the variable (usually in the form $ax^2+bx=0$).
  - _why:_ Having two variable terms does not determine an equation’s degree. A quadratic is identified by 2 being the highest variable power, with a non-zero $x^2$ coefficient.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the expression $(x+4)(x-7)=0$, then either $\text{[...]}$ or $x-7=0$ according to the zero product rule.

**Back:** If the expression $(x+4)(x-7)=0$, then either $\boxed{x+4=0}$ or $x-7=0$ according to the zero product rule.

**Choices:**

- $$x+4=0$ ✔
  - _why:_ The zero product rule states that if two factors multiply to zero, at least one factor equals zero. Therefore, one possible equation is $x+4=0$.
- $$x-4=0$
  - _why:_ Changing the sign inside the factor is an unjustified sign error. The factor given is $x+4$, so the zero product rule produces $x+4=0$.
- $$x+4=1$
  - _why:_ The zero product rule requires each original factor to be set equal to zero, not to one. The correct equation from the first factor is $x+4=0$.
- $$x=0$
  - _why:_ Setting $x$ itself to zero ignores the constant within the first factor. Since the factor is $x+4$, it must be set as $x+4=0$.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ When a product equals zero, the zero product rule allows either factor to equal zero. Setting the second factor equal to zero gives $x-7=0$.
- $$x+7=0$
  - _why:_ Reversing the minus sign is a common sign error. The second factor is $x-7$, so it must be set equal to zero as $x-7=0$.
- $$x-7=7$
  - _why:_ The zero product rule sets each factor equal to zero, not to seven. Applying it to the second factor gives the equation $x-7=0$.
- $$x=0$
  - _why:_ Setting $x$ directly to zero discards the subtraction within the factor. The complete second factor must equal zero, producing $x-7=0$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the roots of a quadratic equation?

**Back:** The values of the variable that make the equation true (where the graph crosses the x-axis).

**Choices:**

- The values of the variable that make the equation true (where the graph crosses the x-axis). ✔
  - _why:_ Roots are the variable values that satisfy the quadratic equation. Graphically, they are the x-coordinates where the quadratic graph meets or crosses the x-axis.
- The values of the variable that make the equation false (where the graph does not cross the x-axis).
  - _why:_ Values making an equation false are not solutions or roots. Roots make the equation true and correspond to the graph’s intersections with the x-axis.
- The values of the variable that make the equation true (where the graph crosses the y-axis).
  - _why:_ The graph’s intersection with the y-axis gives its y-intercept, not a root. Roots are variable values corresponding to intersections with the x-axis.
- The values of the variable at the graph’s turning point (where the gradient equals zero).
  - _why:_ The turning point is the quadratic’s maximum or minimum and need not be a root. Roots occur where the graph meets the x-axis and the equation equals zero.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** To factorise $x^2+bx+c$, what two conditions must the numbers in the brackets meet?

**Back:** Multiply to give cAdd to give b

**Choices:**

- Multiply to give cAdd to give b ✔
  - _why:_ In $(x+m)(x+n)$, expansion gives $x^2+(m+n)x+mn$. Therefore, the bracket numbers must multiply to $c$ and add to $b$.
- Multiply to give bAdd to give c
  - _why:_ This reverses the sum and product conditions. Expanding the brackets shows that the numbers multiply to give the constant $c$ and add to give the coefficient $b$.
- Multiply to give cSubtract to give b
  - _why:_ Subtracting the bracket numbers does not generally produce the middle coefficient. Their product must be $c$, while their sum must be $b$.
- Multiply to give bAdd to give b
  - _why:_ The bracket numbers are linked to both coefficients, not just $b$. They must multiply to the constant term $c$ and add to the middle coefficient $b$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic graph crosses the x-axis at points called [ ___ ] or solutions.

**Back:** A quadratic graph crosses the x-axis at points called roots or solutions.

**Choices:**

- roots ✔
  - _why:_ Roots are the x-values for which the quadratic equals zero. They therefore correspond to the points where its graph meets or crosses the x-axis.
- turning points
  - _why:_ Turning points are where a quadratic reaches a maximum or minimum, rather than necessarily where it crosses the x-axis. The x-axis crossing values are called roots.
- vertices
  - _why:_ A parabola has one vertex, which is its turning point and may lie away from the x-axis. Points where the graph crosses the x-axis correspond to roots.
- asymptotes
  - _why:_ Asymptotes are lines approached by certain graphs and are not x-axis crossings; ordinary quadratic graphs have no asymptotes. Their x-axis crossing values are called roots.

**Back:** A quadratic graph crosses the x-axis at points called roots or solutions.

**Choices:**

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the difference between factorising and solving a quadratic?

**Back:** Factorising: Rewriting the expression as a product of brackets.Solving: Finding the numerical values of the variable.

**Choices:**

- Factorising: Rewriting the expression as a product of brackets.Solving: Finding the numerical values of the variable. ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising changes an expression’s form into multiplied factors without finding $x$. Solving goes further by determining the numerical variable values that satisfy the equation.
- Factorising: Finding the numerical values of the variable.Solving: Rewriting the expression as a product of brackets.
  - _why:_ These definitions have been reversed. Factorising rewrites the expression as a product of brackets, whereas solving finds the numerical values that satisfy the equation.
- Factorising: Expanding the expression to remove all brackets.Solving: Collecting like terms without finding the variable.
  - _why:_ Expanding brackets is the opposite of factorising, and simplifying alone does not necessarily solve an equation. Factorising forms a product; solving finds the variable’s values.
- Factorising: Drawing the expression as a quadratic graph.Solving: Finding the coordinates of the turning point.
  - _why:_ Drawing a graph may help locate solutions, but it does not define factorising. Factorising creates a product of brackets, while solving determines the variable’s numerical values.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If the constant $c$ in $x^2+bx+c$ is positive, what is known about the signs in the brackets?

**Back:** They must be the same (both plus or both minus).

**Choices:**

- They must be the same (both plus or both minus). ✔
  - _why:_ A positive constant comes from multiplying two numbers with the same sign. Therefore, the two signs are either both plus or both minus.
- They must be opposite (one plus, one minus).
  - _why:_ Opposite signs produce a negative product, so they would give a negative constant. A positive constant requires the signs to be the same.
- They must both be positive (two plus signs).
  - _why:_ Two plus signs are possible, but two minus signs can also produce a positive constant. Only the middle coefficient determines which matching pair is needed.
- They must both be negative (two minus signs).
  - _why:_ Two minus signs are possible, but two plus signs also produce a positive constant. The only certain conclusion is that the signs must be the same.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Back:** Both signs are negative.

**Choices:**

- Both signs are negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A positive constant requires matching signs, while a negative middle coefficient requires their sum to be negative. Therefore, both signs must be negative.
- Both signs are positive.
  - _why:_ Positive signs would give a positive sum and therefore a positive middle term. A positive constant with a negative middle coefficient requires both signs to be negative.
- The signs are opposite.
  - _why:_ Opposite signs would make the constant negative because their product would be negative. Since the constant is positive, the signs match, and the negative middle term makes both negative.
- Only one sign is negative.
  - _why:_ Using only one negative sign makes the factors’ constant terms have a negative product. A positive constant and negative middle term require two negative signs.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Choices:**

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Back:** To solve $x^2+2x-48=0$, you need numbers that multiply to $\boxed{-48}$ and add to $2$.

**Choices:**

- $$-48$ ✔
  - _why:_ When factorising a monic quadratic, the two numbers must multiply to the constant term and add to the coefficient of $x$. Here the constant is $-48$.

  - _why:_ Dropping the negative sign would produce a positive constant and two matching signs. The required product is $-48$, so the factor terms have opposite signs.
- $$-2$
  - _why:_ This confuses the required product with the negative of the middle coefficient. The numbers must multiply to the constant term, which is $-48$, and add to $2$.
- $$-24$
  - _why:_ Halving the constant is not part of factorising a monic quadratic. The two numbers must multiply to the full constant term, so their product is $-48$.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** When solving $(x-2)(x+5)=0$, you must not [ ___ ] by a bracket containing the variable because that bracket might be zero.

**Back:** When solving $(x-2)(x+5)=0$, you must not divide by a bracket containing the variable because that bracket might be zero.

**Choices:**

- divide ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing by a variable-containing bracket could divide by zero and discard the solution that makes that bracket zero. Instead, set each factor equal to zero separately.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplication does not risk cancelling a zero factor, although it may be unnecessary. Division is forbidden because a bracket could equal zero and its corresponding root would be lost.
- expand
  - _why:_ Expanding the brackets is valid, though it makes the equation less convenient to solve. The dangerous operation is dividing by a bracket that might equal zero.
- factorise
  - _why:_ Factorising is a valid and useful step because it enables the zero-product rule. You must not divide by a variable-containing factor, as doing so could remove a solution.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** When solving $(x-2)(x+5)=0$, you must not divide by a bracket containing the variable because that bracket might be [ ___ ].

**Back:** When solving $(x-2)(x+5)=0$, you must not divide by a bracket containing the variable because that bracket might be zero.

**Choices:**

- zero ✔
  - _why:_ A factorised product is zero when at least one bracket is zero. Dividing by that bracket could mean dividing by zero, which is undefined and could remove a valid solution.
- one
  - _why:_ A bracket may equal one, but dividing by one is valid and does not lose a solution. The real danger is that the variable-containing bracket might equal zero.
- negative
  - _why:_ A negative bracket is a valid non-zero divisor, so dividing by it is allowed. The restriction exists because the bracket might equal zero, which is undefined as a divisor.
- undefined
  - _why:_ The bracket itself is not necessarily undefined; it can take ordinary numerical values. Division is unsafe because the bracket might equal zero, not because the bracket is undefined.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you check if your factorisation $(x+a)(x+b)$ is correct?

**Back:** Expand the brackets to see if they return the original quadratic expression.

**Choices:**

- Expand the brackets to see if they return the original quadratic expression. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding the two factors checks both the middle term and the constant term. If the result matches the original quadratic expression exactly, the factorisation is correct.
- Add the brackets to see if they return the original quadratic expression.
  - _why:_ Brackets are checked by multiplying them out, not by adding them. Expanding verifies whether the product reproduces the original quadratic expression.
- Subtract the brackets to see if they return the original quadratic expression.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the factors does not undo a factorisation, because the quadratic comes from their product. Expanding means multiplying the brackets and comparing the result.
- Divide the brackets to see if they return the original quadratic expression.
  - _why:_ Division of the factors cannot recreate the original quadratic product and may involve division by zero. Expanding the brackets is the correct verification method.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Why do quadratics usually have two solutions?

**Back:** The equation is true if either of the two factorised brackets is equal to zero.

**Choices:**

- The equation is true if either of the two factorised brackets is equal to zero. ✔
  - _why:_ If two factors multiply to zero, at least one factor must be zero. Setting each bracket equal to zero therefore usually produces two solutions, although repeated roots can give the same value.
- The equation is true only when both of the two factorised brackets are equal to zero.
  - _why:_ A product is zero when at least one factor is zero, not necessarily both. Setting each bracket to zero separately usually gives the two solutions.
- The equation is true when the two factorised brackets are equal to each other.
  - _why:_ Equal brackets do not make their product zero unless both are themselves zero. The zero-product rule says that at least one factor must equal zero.
- The equation is true when neither of the two factorised brackets is equal to zero.
  - _why:_ Two non-zero factors have a non-zero product, so this cannot solve a quadratic set equal to zero. At least one factor must equal zero.

## Card 24 — `cloze`

**Front:** If you are stuck factorising, you should list [ ___ ] of the constant term.

**Back:** If you are stuck factorising, you should list factor pairs of the constant term.

**Choices:**

- factor pairs ✔
  - _why:_ Listing factor pairs of the constant term identifies combinations whose product matches the constant. You can then choose the pair whose sum matches the coefficient of the linear term.
- multiples
  - _why:_ Multiples continue by repeated addition and do not directly list combinations with a required product. The useful list is factor pairs, because their products can match the constant term.
- square roots
  - _why:_ Square roots only identify numbers that square to the constant and can miss the pair needed for the middle term. Factor pairs systematically provide possible products.
- prime factors
  - _why:_ Prime factors show the building blocks of the constant, but they are not yet arranged into the two numbers needed. Listing factor pairs makes the possible products explicit.
