## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the radius ($r$) calculated if you are given the diameter ($d$)?

**Back:** $$r = \frac{d}{2}$ (half the diameter)

**Choices:**

- $$r = \frac{d}{2}$ (half the diameter) ✔
  - _why:_ The diameter spans the full width of the circle through its centre, while the radius spans half that distance. Therefore, dividing the diameter by 2 gives the radius.
- $$r = 2d$ (double the diameter)
  - _why:_ This reverses and exaggerates the relationship between radius and diameter. The diameter is twice the radius, so the correct calculation is $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- $$r = d$ (equal to the diameter)
  - _why:_ This treats the radius and diameter as the same measurement. A radius extends only from the centre to the circumference, so it is half the diameter: $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- $$r = \frac{d}{4}$ (quarter the diameter)
  - _why:_ This divides by 2 twice, making the radius too small. The diameter contains exactly two radii, so the correct relationship is $r = \frac{d}{2}$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the area of the circular end of a cylinder?

**Back:** $$A = \pi r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cylinder’s circular end is a circle of radius $r$. The area of any circle is found by multiplying $\pi$ by the square of its radius.
- $$A = 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ This is the formula for a circle’s circumference, not its area. The area of the circular end is found by squaring the radius: $A = \pi r^2$.
- $$A = \pi r$
  - _why:_ This omits the required square on the radius and therefore does not produce square units. The area of a circle is $A = \pi r^2$.
- $$A = 2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly doubles the area, often by counting both circular ends. The question asks for one circular end, whose area is $A = \pi r^2$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the volume of a cylinder?

**Back:** $$V = \pi r^2h$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \pi r^2h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cylinder’s volume equals its circular cross-sectional area, $\pi r^2$, multiplied by its perpendicular height, $h$. This gives $V = \pi r^2h$.
- $$V = 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This calculates the curved surface area by multiplying the circumference by the height. Volume instead uses the base area, so the correct formula is $V = \pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \pi rh^2$
  - _why:_ This squares the height instead of the radius. The circular base has area $\pi r^2$, which must be multiplied by $h$, giving $V = \pi r^2h$.
- $$V = 2\pi r^2h$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly doubles the cylinder’s volume, often by counting both circular ends. Only one base area is multiplied by the height, giving $V = \pi r^2h$.

**Back:** The volume of a cylinder is found by multiplying the area of the circular base by the height.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Multiplying the circumference by the height gives the curved surface area, not the volume. Volume is found by multiplying the area of the circular base by the height.

  - _why:_ The diameter is a length, so multiplying it by height would produce square units rather than cubic units. Volume requires the area of the circular base multiplied by height.
- area of the curved surface
  - _why:_ The curved surface area describes the cylinder’s outside covering and is not its cross-section. Volume is calculated using the area of the circular base multiplied by height.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Which three shapes make up the net of a closed cylinder?

**Back:** Two circles (top and bottom)One rectangle (curved surface)

**Choices:**

- Two circles (top and bottom)One rectangle (curved surface) ✔
  - _why:_ The two circles form the cylinder’s parallel ends. When the curved surface is cut and unfolded flat, it forms one rectangle, completing the closed cylinder’s net.
- Two semicircles (top and bottom)One rectangle (curved surface)
  - _why:_ Each end of a closed cylinder is a complete circle, not a semicircle. Its net consists of two circles for the ends and one rectangle for the curved surface.
- Two circles (top and bottom)One square (curved surface)
  - _why:_ The curved surface unfolds into a rectangle whose dimensions are the cylinder’s height and circumference. It is only a square in a special case, so the general shape is a rectangle.
- Two circles (top and bottom)One triangle (curved surface)
  - _why:_ A triangle cannot wrap evenly around a cylinder to form its curved surface. Unrolling that surface produces a rectangle, alongside the two circular ends.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** When a cylinder is unfolded, what circle measurement is equal to the width of the curved surface rectangle?

**Back:** The circumference ($2\pi r$ or $\pi d$)

**Choices:**

- The circumference ($2\pi r$ or $\pi d$) ✔
  - _why:_ The rectangle wraps exactly once around the circular end of the cylinder. Its width therefore equals the circle’s circumference, calculated as $2\pi r$ or $\pi d$.
- The diameter ($2r$ or $\frac{C}{\pi}$)
  - _why:_ The diameter measures straight across the circle, not the distance around it. The rectangle must wrap around the complete circle, so its width equals the circumference.
- The radius ($r$ or $\frac{d}{2}$)
  - _why:_ The radius measures from the centre to the circle’s edge and is much shorter than the distance around it. The rectangle’s width equals the full circumference.
- The area ($\pi r^2$ or $\frac{\pi d^2}{4}$)
  - _why:_ Area is measured in square units and describes the circular end’s surface, whereas the rectangle’s width is a length. That length equals the circle’s circumference.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the curved surface area (CSA) of a cylinder?

**Back:** $$\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh$ ✔
  - _why:_ Unrolling the curved surface produces a rectangle with width equal to the circumference $2\pi r$ and height $h$, so its area is $2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{CSA} = \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This uses the area of one circular end, not the curved surface. The curved surface unrolls to a rectangle, giving $\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{CSA} = \pi rh$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $2$ from the circumference of the circular base. Since the circumference is $2\pi r$, the correct formula is $\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{CSA} = 2\pi h^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly squares the height rather than multiplying the base circumference by the height. The unrolled rectangle has area $2\pi r \times h = 2\pi rh$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the total surface area (TSA) of a closed cylinder?

**Back:** $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$ ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has two circular ends with combined area $2\pi r^2$ and a curved surface of area $2\pi rh$, so these terms are added.
- $$\text{TSA} = \pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This includes only one circular end, as for a cylinder open at one end. A closed cylinder has two ends, so the correct formula is $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This omits both circular ends and gives only the curved surface area. A closed cylinder also contributes $2\pi r^2$, making the total $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + 4\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This doubles the curved surface term even though the cylinder has only one curved outer surface. The correct total is $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$.

**Back:** If a cylindrical tank is open at the top, the surface area formula changes because it only includes one circular face instead of two.

- one semi-circular face
  - _why:_ A semi-circular face belongs to a cut semi-cylinder, not an open-topped full cylinder. The tank retains one complete circular base, so the correct fill is one circular face.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a cylindrical tank is open at the top, the surface area formula changes because it only includes one circular face instead of [ ___ ].

**Back:** If a cylindrical tank is open at the top, the surface area formula changes because it only includes one circular face instead of two.

**Choices:**

- two ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has two circular faces, but removing the top leaves only one. Therefore, the open tank includes one circular face instead of two.

  - _why:_ Using one fails to distinguish the open tank from the closed cylinder. The open tank has one circular face, whereas the corresponding closed cylinder has two.

  - _why:_ Four double-counts both circular ends of the cylinder. There is one top and one base on a closed cylinder, so the open tank has one instead of two.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the volume of a semi-cylinder with length $L$?

**Back:** $$V = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$ ✔
  - _why:_ A full cylinder has volume $\pi r^2L$. A semi-cylinder is half of that solid, so its volume is $V = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- $$V = \pi r^2L$
  - _why:_ This is the volume of a complete cylinder and does not account for the solid being cut in half. The semi-cylinder’s volume is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{4}\pi r^2L$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly halves the radius before squaring, making the volume one quarter of a full cylinder rather than one half. The correct formula is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{2}\pi rL$
  - _why:_ This uses $rL$ and omits the squared radius needed for the area of the semi-circular cross-section. The correct volume is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.

**Front:** A cylinder has height $8$ m and volume $288\pi$ m³. Outline the steps to find the radius.

**Back:** Divide $288\pi$ by $8\pi$ to get $r^2 = 36$Square root $36$ to get $r = 6$

**Choices:**

- Divide $288\pi$ by $8\pi$ to get $r^2 = 36$Square root $36$ to get $r = 6$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $V=\pi r^2h$ and dividing by $8\pi$ gives $r^2=36$. Taking the positive square root gives the physical radius $r=6$ m.
- Divide $288\pi$ by $8$ to get $r^2 = 36\pi$Square root $36\pi$ to get $r = 6\sqrt{\pi}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by the height alone fails to cancel the factor of $\pi$. The calculation requires division by $8\pi$, giving $r^2=36$ and then $r=6$.
- Divide $288\pi$ by $8\pi$ to get $r^2 = 36$Divide $36$ by $2$ to get $r = 18$
  - _why:_ The first step correctly finds $r^2$, but dividing by two does not undo squaring. Taking the square root of $36$ gives the correct radius, $r=6$.
- Divide $288\pi$ by $\pi$ to get $r^2 = 288$Square root $288$ to get $r = 12\sqrt{2}$
  - _why:_ The volume must be divided by $\pi h$, not merely by $\pi$. Including the height gives $r^2=36$, whose positive square root is $r=6$.

**Back:** $$1000$ cm³

  - _why:_ One litre is defined as one cubic decimetre. Since $1$ dm equals $10$ cm, cubing the conversion gives $10^3=1000$ cm³.

  - _why:_ This applies the linear conversion from decimetres to centimetres without cubing it. Volume scales in three dimensions, so $1$ litre equals $1000$ cm³.
- $$100$ cm³
  - _why:_ This squares the factor of $10$, as though converting area. A volume conversion requires cubing the factor, so $1$ litre equals $1000$ cm³.

  - _why:_ This is ten times too large and commonly results from adding an extra zero. Cubing $10$ gives $1000$, so one litre contains $1000$ cm³.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** In practical problems (e.g., buying paint pots for a calculated area), how should you round the number of items needed?

**Back:** Always round up to the next whole number.

**Choices:**

- Always round up to the next whole number. ✔
  - _why:_ A fractional result means that another whole item is required to meet the calculated need. Rounding up ensures enough paint pots or other indivisible items are purchased.
- Always round down to the previous whole number.
  - _why:_ Rounding down would leave the required quantity unmet whenever the calculation has a fractional part. Practical quantities such as paint pots must instead be rounded up.
- Always round normally to the nearest whole number.
  - _why:_ Rounding to the nearest integer can round a fractional requirement down, leaving too few items. The number needed must always be rounded up to the next whole number.
- Always leave the answer as a decimal number.
  - _why:_ Leaving a decimal answer is unsuitable when items can only be purchased whole. The result must be rounded up so the purchased amount meets the requirement.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** Define a cylinder in terms of its faces.

**Back:** A 3D solid with two equal parallel circular faces joined by one curved surface.

**Choices:**

- A 3D solid with two equal parallel circular faces joined by one curved surface. ✔
  - _why:_ A cylinder has two congruent circular bases lying in parallel planes. Its remaining face is the single curved surface connecting the circumferences of those circles.
- A 3D solid with one circular face and one vertex joined by one curved surface.
  - _why:_ A solid with only one circular face and a curved surface tapering to a point is a cone. A cylinder has two equal parallel circular faces.
- A 3D solid with two unequal parallel circular faces joined by one curved surface.
  - _why:_ Unequal circular faces describe a tapered frustum rather than a cylinder. A cylinder’s two circular faces must be equal and parallel, joined by one curved surface.
- A 3D solid with two equal perpendicular circular faces joined by one curved surface.
  - _why:_ Circular faces cannot be perpendicular and still form the two bases of a cylinder. The two equal circular faces must lie in parallel planes.
