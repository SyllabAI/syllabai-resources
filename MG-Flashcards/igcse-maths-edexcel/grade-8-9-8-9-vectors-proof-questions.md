## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the definition of a position vector?

**Back:** A vector giving the position of a point relative to the origin ($O$).

**Choices:**

- A vector giving the position of a point relative to the origin ($O$). ✔
  - _why:_ A position vector starts at the origin and ends at the point, so it describes that point’s position relative to $O$.
- A vector giving the direction of a line relative to the origin ($O$).
  - _why:_ This confuses a position vector with a direction vector. A position vector locates a point relative to the origin $O$, rather than describing a line’s direction.
- A vector giving the distance of a point from another point ($P$).
  - _why:_ This describes a displacement between two points, not a position vector. The correct definition gives the position of one point relative to the origin $O$.
- A vector giving the midpoint of a line relative to the origin ($O$).
  - _why:_ A position vector can locate any point, not specifically a midpoint. It is the vector from the origin $O$ to the point being described.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the vector $\vec{AB}$, use the rule $\text{[...]}$, often remembered as end minus start.

**Back:** To find the vector $\vec{AB}$, use the rule $\boxed{\vec{OB} - \vec{OA}}$, often remembered as end minus start.

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{OB} - \vec{OA}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A vector from A to B is found by subtracting the position vector of the starting point from that of the ending point. This is the end-minus-start rule.
- $$\vec{OA} - \vec{OB}$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction gives the vector from B to A, not from A to B. For a vector from A to B, subtract the start position vector from the end position vector.
- $$\vec{OA} + \vec{OB}$
  - _why:_ Adding the two position vectors does not represent the displacement between the points. The required vector uses subtraction: take the ending point’s position vector minus the starting point’s position vector.
- $$\vec{AB} - \vec{OB}$
  - _why:_ This mixes a displacement vector with a position vector, so it does not give the vector joining A to B. Use the end point’s position vector minus the start point’s position vector.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the vector $\vec{AB}$, use the rule $\vec{OB} - \vec{OA}$, often remembered as [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find the vector $\vec{AB}$, use the rule $\vec{OB} - \vec{OA}$, often remembered as end minus start.

**Choices:**

- end minus start ✔
  - _why:_ For $vec{AB}$, $B$ is the endpoint and $A$ is the starting point, so the rule $vec{OB}-vec{OA}$ is remembered as end minus start.
- start minus end
  - _why:_ Start minus end reverses the direction and gives $vec{BA}$ instead. The vector $vec{AB}$ uses end minus start, or $vec{OB}-vec{OA}$.
- end plus start
  - _why:_ Adding the endpoint and starting-point position vectors does not find the vector between them. The required operation is end minus start: $vec{OB}-vec{OA}$.
- start divided by end
  - _why:_ Position vectors are not divided to find a displacement between two points. Subtract the start position vector from the end position vector, remembered as end minus start.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** If point $P$ divides the line $AB$ in the ratio $m:n$, what fraction of $\vec{AB}$ is the vector $\vec{AP}$?

**Back:** $$\frac{m}{m+n}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{m}{m+n}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The whole ratio is $m+n$, and $AP$ represents the first part $m$. Therefore, $vec{AP}$ is the fraction $frac{m}{m+n}$ of $vec{AB}$.
- $$frac{n}{m+n}$
  - _why:_ This uses the second ratio part as the distance from $A$. Since $AP$ corresponds to $m$, the correct fraction of $vec{AB}$ is $frac{m}{m+n}$.
- $$frac{m}{n}$
  - _why:_ This compares the two sections but does not give the fraction of the entire line $AB$. The whole line is $m+n$, so $AP$ is $frac{m}{m+n}$ of $vec{AB}$.
- $$frac{m+n}{m}$
  - _why:_ This is the reciprocal of the required fraction and can be greater than $1$, so it cannot represent the section from $A$ to an internal point $P$. The correct fraction is $frac{m}{m+n}$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** If $AP:PB = 2:3$, then point $P$ is $\text{[...]}$ of the way from $A$ to $B$.

**Back:** If $AP:PB = 2:3$, then point $P$ is $\boxed{\frac{2}{5}}$ of the way from $A$ to $B$.

**Choices:**

- $$frac{2}{5}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The total ratio is $2+3=5$, and $AP$ is the first part, $2$. Thus $P$ is $frac{2}{5}$ of the way from $A$ to $B$.
- $$frac{3}{5}$
  - _why:_ This uses $PB$, the second section, rather than $AP$, the distance from $A$. Since $AP$ is $2$ out of a total $5$, the correct fraction is $frac{2}{5}$.
- $$frac{2}{3}$
  - _why:_ The ratio $2:3$ compares the two sections but is not the fraction of the whole line. Add the parts to get $5$, giving $AP:AB=2:5$ and the answer $frac{2}{5}$.
- $$frac{3}{2}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the ratio and is greater than $1$, so it cannot be the fraction of the way to an internal point. The correct calculation is $2/(2+3)=frac{2}{5}$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the position vector $\vec{OP}$ for a point $P$ on the line $AB$?

**Back:** $$\vec{OP} = \vec{OA} + \text{fraction} \times \vec{AB}$

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{OP} = \vec{OA} + \text{fraction} \times \vec{AB}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Start at $A$ using $vec{OA}$, then move the required fraction of the way along $vec{AB}$. Their sum gives the position vector $vec{OP}$.
- $$vec{OP} = vec{OA} - text{fraction} times vec{AB}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting moves from $A$ in the direction opposite to $B$, giving a point on the extension away from $B$. To reach $P$ on $AB$, add the fraction of $vec{AB}$.
- $$vec{OP} = vec{OB} + text{fraction} times vec{AB}$
  - _why:_ The construction should begin at $A$, not $B$. Starting with $vec{OA}$ and adding the required fraction of $vec{AB}$ gives $vec{OP}$.
- $$vec{OP} = text{fraction} times vec{OA} + vec{AB}$
  - _why:_ This scales the position of $A$ and then adds the entire displacement, which does not locate the required fraction along $AB$. Use $vec{OA}+text{fraction}timesvec{AB}$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What two conditions prove that points $A, B$ and $C$ are collinear?

**Back:** The vectors (e.g., $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$) are scalar multiples.The vectors share a common point.

**Choices:**

- The vectors (e.g., $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$) are scalar multiples.The vectors share a common point. ✔
  - _why:_ Scalar-multiple vectors are parallel, and $vec{AB}$ and $vec{AC}$ share the starting point $A$. Therefore, both vectors lie on the same straight line and $A$, $B$, and $C$ are collinear.
- The vectors (e.g., $vec{AB}$ and $vec{AC}$) are perpendicular.The vectors share a common point.
  - _why:_ Perpendicular vectors sharing a point form a right angle, not one straight line. Collinearity requires the vectors to be scalar multiples and to share a common point.
- The vectors (e.g., $vec{AB}$ and $vec{AC}$) have equal magnitudes.The vectors share a common point.
  - _why:_ Equal lengths do not guarantee the same direction; equal-length vectors can form an angle. Collinearity requires scalar multiples, together with a shared common point.
- The vectors (e.g., $vec{AB}$ and $vec{AC}$) are unit vectors.The vectors share a common point.
  - _why:_ Being unit vectors only describes their lengths and says nothing about whether they point along the same line. Collinearity requires scalar multiples and a common point.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** If $\vec{AD} = \lambda\vec{AE}$, the vectors are [ ___ ] and, because they share point $A$, the points are collinear.

**Back:** If $\vec{AD} = \lambda\vec{AE}$, the vectors are parallel and, because they share point $A$, the points are collinear.

**Choices:**

- parallel ✔
  - _why:_ If $vec{AD}=lambdavec{AE}$, one vector is a scalar multiple of the other, so they are parallel. Sharing $A$ means their lines coincide, making the points collinear.
- perpendicular
  - _why:_ A scalar-multiple relationship indicates parallel vectors, not perpendicular vectors. Since both vectors start at $A$, their shared point then proves the points lie on one line.
- equal
  - _why:_ The vectors need not have the same magnitude because $lambda$ can have a value other than $1$. The scalar-multiple relationship proves they are parallel, which leads to collinearity.
- opposite
  - _why:_ The vectors are opposite only when $lambda$ is negative, so that is not guaranteed. Any scalar multiple makes them parallel; sharing $A$ then establishes collinearity.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** If $\vec{AD} = \lambda\vec{AE}$, the vectors are parallel and, because they share point $A$, the points are [ ___ ].

**Back:** If $\vec{AD} = \lambda\vec{AE}$, the vectors are parallel and, because they share point $A$, the points are collinear.

**Choices:**

- collinear ✔
  - _why:_ A scalar-multiple relationship shows that the two vectors are parallel. Since both vectors begin at $A$, their endpoints $D$ and $E$ lie on the same straight line through $A$.
- coplanar
  - _why:_ Coplanarity only means that the points lie in one plane, which is weaker than lying on one line. The shared starting point and parallel vectors prove the points are collinear.
- coincident
  - _why:_ Coincident points occupy exactly the same position, which would require stronger information such as equal vectors. A scalar-multiple relationship instead shows that $A$, $D$ and $E$ are collinear.
- equidistant
  - _why:_ A scalar multiple does not show that any distances are equal; $\lambda$ may change the vector’s magnitude. It shows parallel directions through $A$, so the points are collinear.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is showing $\vec{OD} = \lambda\vec{OE}$ insufficient to prove points $A, D, E$ are collinear?

**Back:** It only proves $O, D, E$ are on a line; it does not link them to point $A$.

**Choices:**

- It only proves $O, D, E$ are on a line; it does not link them to point $A$. ✔
  - _why:_ Because both position vectors start at $O$, the scalar-multiple relationship establishes a line through $O$, $D$ and $E$. Further vector work involving $A$ is required.
- It only proves $A, D, E$ are on a line; it does not link them to point $O$.
  - _why:_ This reverses the conclusion: the vectors begin at $O$, so they prove that $O$, $D$ and $E$ are collinear. They provide no link to $A$.
- It only proves $\vec{OD} = \vec{OE}$; it does not determine the value of $\lambda$.
  - _why:_ A scalar-multiple equation does not generally make the vectors equal; that occurs only when $\lambda=1$. Its limitation is that it links $O$, $D$ and $E$, not $A$.
- It only proves $D$ and $E$ are parallel; it does not show that point $O$ lies between them.
  - _why:_ Points themselves are not parallel, and collinearity does not require $O$ to lie between $D$ and $E$. The real issue is that the equation does not involve $A$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** In a parallelogram $OABC$ where $\vec{OA}=a$ and $\vec{OC}=c$, what is the position vector of the diagonal vertex $B$?

**Back:** $$\vec{OB} = a + c$

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{OB} = a + c$ ✔
  - _why:_ Moving from $O$ to $B$ can be achieved by following the two adjacent side vectors represented by $a$ and $c$. Vector addition therefore gives $\vec{OB}=a+c$.
- $$\vec{OB} = a - c$
  - _why:_ Subtracting $c$ reverses that side’s contribution and gives a displacement towards a different point. The diagonal position vector combines both adjacent side vectors, so it is $a+c$.
- $$\vec{OB} = c - a$
  - _why:_ The vector $c-a$ represents the displacement from $A$ to $C$, not the position vector of $B$. The diagonal from $O$ is the sum $a+c$.
- $$\vec{OB} = 2a + c$
  - _why:_ This mistakenly counts the side vector $a$ twice. A parallelogram diagonal is obtained by adding each adjacent side vector once, giving $\vec{OB}=a+c$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a regular hexagon with centre $O$, if $\vec{OP}=p$ and $\vec{OQ}=q$, the next vertex $R$ has position vector $\vec{OR} = $ $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** In a regular hexagon with centre $O$, if $\vec{OP}=p$ and $\vec{OQ}=q$, the next vertex $R$ has position vector $\vec{OR} = $ $\boxed{q - p}$.

**Choices:**

- $$q - p$ ✔
  - _why:_ For three consecutive vertices of a regular hexagon, the side displacement from $P$ to $Q$ is equal and parallel to the radius from $O$ to $R$. Hence $\vec{OR}=q-p$.
- $$p + q$
  - _why:_ Adding the two position vectors produces a vector in the wrong direction and with the wrong magnitude. The next radius equals $\vec{PQ}=q-p$, so $\vec{OR}=q-p$.
- $$p - q$
  - _why:_ This reverses the displacement between $P$ and $Q$, giving $\vec{QP}$ rather than $\vec{PQ}$. The next vertex has position vector $q-p$.
- $$-p$
  - _why:_ The vector $-p$ points to the vertex opposite $P$, not to the next vertex after $Q$. Using the consecutive-radius relationship gives $\vec{OR}=q-p$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** In a regular hexagon with centre $O$, if $\vec{OQ}=q$, what is the position vector of the opposite vertex $T$?

**Back:** $$-q$

**Choices:**

- $$-q$ ✔
  - _why:_ Opposite vertices lie on the same diameter, at equal distances from the centre but in opposite directions. Therefore the opposite position vector is the negative of $q$.
- $$q$
  - _why:_ Using $q$ gives the position vector of $Q$ itself, not the opposite vertex. Reversing the direction while preserving the magnitude gives the required vector $-q$.
- $$2q$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $2$ keeps the direction towards $Q$ and incorrectly doubles the distance from the centre. The opposite vertex has equal magnitude and opposite direction, so its vector is $-q$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}q$
  - _why:_ Halving $q$ gives a point between $O$ and $Q$, not a vertex opposite $Q$. The opposite radius has the same length but reversed direction, giving $-q$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** If $Y$ lies on $PQ$ extended beyond $Q$ such that $PQ:QY = 3:2$, express $\vec{QY}$ in terms of $\vec{PQ}$.

**Back:** $$\vec{QY} = \frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{QY} = \frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio $PQ:QY=3:2$ means $QY$ is $\frac{2}{3}$ of $PQ$. Since $Y$ lies beyond $Q$, both vectors have the same direction.
- $$\vec{QY} = -\frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$
  - _why:_ The negative sign would place $Y$ back towards $P$. Because $Y$ is beyond $Q$, $\vec{QY}$ has the same direction as $\vec{PQ}$ and equals $\frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$.
- $$\vec{QY} = \frac{3}{2}\vec{PQ}$
  - _why:_ This inverts the ratio, treating $QY:PQ$ as $3:2$. The given ratio makes $QY$ two-thirds of $PQ$, so $\vec{QY}=\frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$.
- $$\vec{QY} = \frac{5}{3}\vec{PQ}$
  - _why:_ This gives the displacement from $P$ to $Y$ by adding $PQ$ and $QY$. The question asks only for $\vec{QY}$, which is $\frac{2}{3}\vec{PQ}$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find an unknown vector on an extension (e.g., $E$ on $OB$), introduce an unknown scalar $x$ such that $\vec{BE} = $ $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To find an unknown vector on an extension (e.g., $E$ on $OB$), introduce an unknown scalar $x$ such that $\vec{BE} = $ $\boxed{xb}$.

**Choices:**

- $$xb$ ✔
  - _why:_ Because $E$ lies on the line through $O$ and $B$, $\vec{BE}$ must be a scalar multiple of the direction vector $b$. The unknown multiplier is represented by $x$.
- $$(1-x)b$
  - _why:_ This commonly arises from confusing the section parameter for a position vector with the displacement from $B$. The unknown vector $\vec{BE}$ should be introduced directly as $xb$.
- $$-xb$
  - _why:_ The negative sign fixes the vector in the direction opposite to $b$ before the extension’s direction has been established. Using a signed unknown scalar already allows either direction, so write $xb$.
- $$x^2b$
  - _why:_ Squaring the unknown unnecessarily restricts the multiplier to non-negative values and may exclude the required direction. A general scalar multiple of $b$ should be written as $xb$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** When given $\vec{CD} = \lambda\vec{CE}$ with vectors in terms of $a$ and $b$, how do you find the values of $\lambda$ or other unknowns?

**Back:** Compare the coefficients of $a$ and $b$ on both sides of the equation.

**Choices:**

- Compare the coefficients of $a$ and $b$ on both sides of the equation. ✔
  - _why:_ If $a$ and $b$ are independent vectors, equal vector expressions must have matching coefficients. Equating the $a$ coefficients and the $b$ coefficients gives scalar equations for the unknowns.
- Compare the magnitudes of $a$ and $b$ on both sides of the equation.
  - _why:_ Comparing magnitudes loses the separate contributions and directions of $a$ and $b$. Instead, equate their respective coefficients on both sides to obtain equations for the unknowns.
- Add the coefficients of $a$ and $b$ on each side of the equation.
  - _why:_ Adding coefficients combines two independent vector components and can make unequal vectors appear equivalent. The coefficient of $a$ and the coefficient of $b$ must each be compared separately.
- Compare the directions of $a$ and $b$ on both sides of the equation.
  - _why:_ The directions of the base vectors are already fixed and do not determine the unknown scalar values. Matching the separate coefficients of $a$ and $b$ produces the required equations.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** If $OC:CA = 3:1$, then $\vec{OC}$ is $\text{[...]}$ of $\vec{OA}$.

**Back:** If $OC:CA = 3:1$, then $\vec{OC}$ is $\boxed{\frac{3}{4}}$ of $\vec{OA}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{3}{4}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio $OC:CA=3:1$ divides $OA$ into four equal parts. Since $OC$ covers three of those parts, $\vec{OC}=\frac{3}{4}\vec{OA}$.
- $$\frac{1}{4}$
  - _why:_ This uses the single part represented by $CA$ rather than the three parts represented by $OC$. The full segment has four parts, so $\vec{OC}=\frac{3}{4}\vec{OA}$.
- $$3$
  - _why:_ This treats the ratio entry 3 as a multiplier of the whole vector. Since $OA$ contains $3+1=4$ parts, $\vec{OC}=\frac{3}{4}\vec{OA}$.
- $$\frac{4}{3}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required fraction and would make $OC$ longer than $OA$. Because $OC$ is three of the four total parts, the correct fraction is $\frac{3}{4}$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** If $\vec{AE} = \frac{3}{2}\vec{AD}$, what geometric relationship exists between points $A, D$ and $E$?

**Back:** They are collinear (they lie on a straight line).

**Choices:**

- They are collinear (they lie on a straight line). ✔
  - _why:_ Because $\vec{AE}$ is a scalar multiple of $\vec{AD}$, the two vectors are parallel and share the starting point $A$. Therefore, $A$, $D$ and $E$ are collinear.
- They are perpendicular (they meet at a right angle).
  - _why:_ A scalar multiple indicates parallel vectors, not perpendicular ones. Since $\vec{AE}=\frac{3}{2}\vec{AD}$ and both begin at $A$, the points $A$, $D$ and $E$ are collinear.
- They are equidistant (they are equally spaced apart).
  - _why:_ The scale factor $\frac{3}{2}$ means $AE$ and $AD$ have different lengths, so equal spacing is not implied. Their shared direction instead proves that the three points are collinear.
- They are non-collinear (they form a triangle).
  - _why:_ Multiplying $\vec{AD}$ by the positive scalar $\frac{3}{2}$ changes its length but not its direction. Thus $D$ and $E$ lie on the same straight line through $A$.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** The position vector of a midpoint $N$ on line $AB$ is found using $\vec{ON} = $ $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The position vector of a midpoint $N$ on line $AB$ is found using $\vec{ON} = $ $\boxed{\vec{OA} + \frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\vec{OA} + \frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Starting at $O$, travel to $A$ using $\vec{OA}$, then travel halfway from $A$ to $B$ using $\frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}$. This reaches the midpoint $N$.
- $$\vec{OA} + \frac{1}{2}\vec{OB}$
  - _why:_ This adds half of the position vector of $B$ after already travelling to $A$, so it does not generally reach the midpoint. The added vector must be $\frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}$.
- $$\vec{OA} + 2\vec{AB}$
  - _why:_ This travels twice the entire displacement from $A$ to $B$, placing the point beyond $B$. A midpoint requires travelling only half of $\vec{AB}$ after reaching $A$.
- $$\vec{OB} + \frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}$
  - _why:_ This starts at $B$ and then continues in the direction from $A$ to $B$, placing the point beyond $B$. From $A$, the midpoint is $\vec{OA}+\frac{1}{2}\vec{AB}$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** If $AP:PB = 3:7$, how many equal parts is the line segment $AB$ divided into?

**Back:** 10

**Choices:**

- 10 ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio $AP:PB=3:7$ assigns 3 equal parts to $AP$ and 7 equal parts to $PB$. Therefore, the whole segment $AB$ contains $3+7=10$ parts.
- 4
  - _why:_ This comes from subtracting the ratio entries, but the parts on adjacent sections must be added. The whole segment $AB$ is divided into $3+7=10$ equal parts.
- 7
  - _why:_ This counts only the parts belonging to $PB$ and ignores the 3 parts belonging to $AP$. Together, the two sections divide $AB$ into 10 equal parts.
- 21
  - _why:_ This multiplies the ratio entries instead of combining the parts along the line. Since $AP$ has 3 parts and $PB$ has 7, $AB$ has 10 parts.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean for one vector to be a scalar multiple of another?

**Back:** The vectors are parallel and one is a scaled version of the other.

**Choices:**

- The vectors are parallel and one is a scaled version of the other. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying a vector by a scalar changes its magnitude and may reverse its direction, but it keeps the vectors on parallel lines. Thus one vector is a scaled version of the other.
- The vectors are perpendicular and meet each other at a right angle.
  - _why:_ Perpendicular vectors differ in direction by $90^\circ$ and are not scalar multiples unless one is the zero vector. Scalar multiples lie in the same or opposite direction and are parallel.
- The vectors are equal and have exactly the same magnitude and direction.
  - _why:_ Equal vectors are the special case where the scalar is 1. In general, scalar multiples may have different magnitudes or opposite directions, but they remain parallel.
- The vectors are unrelated and have different magnitudes and directions.
  - _why:_ A scalar multiple creates a precise relationship between the vectors rather than leaving them unrelated. Their magnitudes differ by the scalar’s size, and their directions are parallel or opposite.

## Card 22 — `cloze`

**Front:** Three or more points that lie on the same straight line are called [ ___ ].

**Back:** Three or more points that lie on the same straight line are called collinear.

**Choices:**

- collinear ✔
  - _why:_ Points are collinear when they all lie on one straight line. In vector proofs, showing that displacement vectors are scalar multiples is a standard way to establish collinearity.
- concurrent
  - _why:_ Concurrent describes three or more lines that pass through the same point, not several points on one line. Points lying on the same straight line are called collinear.
- coplanar
  - _why:_ Coplanar points lie in the same plane, but they do not necessarily lie on one straight line. The more specific term for points sharing a straight line is collinear.
- equidistant
  - _why:_ Equidistant means being the same distance from a specified point or object; it does not describe alignment. Three or more points on the same straight line are collinear.
