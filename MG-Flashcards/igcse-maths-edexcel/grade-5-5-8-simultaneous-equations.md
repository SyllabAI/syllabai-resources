## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** Graphically, what represents the solution to two linear simultaneous equations?

**Back:** The point where the two straight lines cross (intersect).

**Choices:**

- The point where the two straight lines cross (intersect). ✔
  - _why:_ At the intersection, the coordinates lie on both straight lines. They therefore satisfy both linear equations simultaneously, making that point the graphical solution.
- The point where either straight line crosses the $y$-axis.
  - _why:_ A $y$-intercept satisfies only the equation represented by its own line. The simultaneous solution is where the two straight lines cross each other.
- The gradient shared by the two straight lines when they are parallel.
  - _why:_ Parallel lines may share a gradient, but distinct parallel lines never intersect and therefore have no simultaneous solution. A graphical solution is the lines’ intersection point.
- The region lying between the two straight lines on the graph.
  - _why:_ A region between two lines does not give a coordinate satisfying both equations. The solution is the single point where the two straight lines intersect.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a solution pair $(x, y)$ represent in simultaneous equations?

**Back:** The single pair of values that makes both equations true at the same time.

**Choices:**

- The single pair of values that makes both equations true at the same time. ✔
  - _why:_ A simultaneous solution must satisfy every equation in the system. Substituting its $x$-value and $y$-value makes both equations true at once.
- The separate pair of values that makes either equation true at a particular time.
  - _why:_ Satisfying either equation alone is insufficient because many points lie on each individual line. The solution pair must make both equations true simultaneously.
- The single value of $x$ that makes one equation true without using $y$.
  - _why:_ A solution pair contains both an $x$-value and a $y$-value, not just one unknown. Together, the values must satisfy both equations.
- The pair of intercepts where each equation crosses an axis on the graph.
  - _why:_ Axis intercepts describe where individual lines meet the axes, not necessarily each other. The solution pair gives the coordinates satisfying both equations simultaneously.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you verify if a coordinate like $(4, 1)$ is the solution to a system of equations?

**Back:** Substitute the values into both equations to see if they hold true.

**Choices:**

- Substitute the values into both equations to see if they hold true. ✔
  - _why:_ Replacing $x$ with $4$ and $y$ with $1$ must produce a true statement in each equation. Only then is $(4, 1)$ the simultaneous solution.
- Substitute the values into one equation to see if it holds true.
  - _why:_ A coordinate can satisfy one equation without satisfying the whole system. Substitute $x=4$ and $y=1$ into both equations and check both statements.
- Add the two values in both equations to see if their total is 5.
  - _why:_ The fact that $4+1=5$ does not verify a system unless an equation specifically requires it. Substitute each value for its corresponding variable in both equations.
- Plot the values on one line to see if the coordinate lies on it.
  - _why:_ Lying on one line proves that only one equation is satisfied. The coordinate must lie on both lines, checked by substitution into both equations.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** To eliminate a variable with [ ___ ] signs (e.g. $+3y$ and $-3y$), you add the equations together.

**Back:** To eliminate a variable with opposite signs (e.g. $+3y$ and $-3y$), you add the equations together.

**Choices:**

- opposite ✔
  - _why:_ Equal coefficients with opposite signs cancel when added: $+3y+(-3y)=0$. This eliminates $y$ while preserving the equality of the combined equation.
- the same
  - _why:_ Adding coefficients with the same sign would combine rather than eliminate them, such as $3y+3y=6y$. Opposite signs are required when adding equations.
- positive
  - _why:_ Two positive coefficients add together instead of cancelling. To eliminate by addition, the coefficients must have equal magnitude and opposite signs, such as $+3y$ and $-3y$.
- negative
  - _why:_ Two negative coefficients also combine rather than cancel, such as $-3y+(-3y)=-6y$. Addition eliminates a variable when its coefficients have opposite signs.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** To eliminate a variable with [ ___ ] sign (e.g. $2x$ and $2x$), you subtract one equation from the other.

**Back:** To eliminate a variable with the same sign (e.g. $2x$ and $2x$), you subtract one equation from the other.

**Choices:**

- the same ✔
  - _why:_ Equal coefficients with the same sign cancel under subtraction: $2x-2x=0$. This removes the variable and leaves an equation in the other unknown.
- opposite
  - _why:_ Subtracting a negative coefficient changes it to positive, so opposite signs combine rather than cancel. Opposite coefficients should be added; same-sign coefficients should be subtracted.
- a different
  - _why:_ Different coefficients do not cancel directly when one equation is subtracted from the other. First make their magnitudes equal, then use subtraction if their signs are the same.
- a negative
  - _why:_ Being negative alone does not determine the operation; two equal negative coefficients have the same sign and can cancel by subtraction. The key condition is the same sign.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a coefficient in an algebraic term like $4x$?

**Back:** The number multiplying the variable (the 4).

**Choices:**

- The number multiplying the variable (the 4). ✔
  - _why:_ In the term $4x$, the numerical factor attached to $x$ is $4$. This number is called the coefficient of the variable.
- The variable being multiplied by the number (the x).
  - _why:_ The $x$ is the variable because its value can change. The coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying it, which is $4$ in $4x$.
- The power applied to the variable (the 1).
  - _why:_ The implicit power of $x$ in $4x$ is $1$, but that is its exponent. The coefficient is the multiplying number, which is $4$.
- The term formed by multiplying the number and variable (the 4x).
  - _why:_ The whole expression $4x$ is an algebraic term, not its coefficient. The coefficient is only the numerical factor multiplying $x$, namely $4$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** When subtracting equations, what is the result of $5y - (-y)$?

**Back:** $$6y$

**Choices:**

- $$6y$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting a negative changes the operation to addition, so $5y-(-y)=5y+y=6y$. The two like terms then combine normally.
- $$4y$
  - _why:_ This results from treating $-(-y)$ as $-y$ and calculating $5y-y$. The two negatives make a positive, so the correct result is $6y$.
- $$5y$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly assumes that the two negative signs cancel the entire second term. They change subtraction to addition, giving $5y+y=6y$.
- $$-6y$
  - _why:_ This combines the coefficients as $5+1$ but incorrectly gives the result a negative sign. Since $-(-y)=+y$, the correct result is positive $6y$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** If neither variable has a matching coefficient, how can you prepare the equations for elimination?

**Back:** Multiply one (or both) whole equations by a constant to make one coefficient match.

**Choices:**

- Multiply one (or both) whole equations by a constant to make one coefficient match. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term on both sides preserves an equation’s solutions. Suitable multipliers can create equal or opposite coefficients, allowing one variable to be eliminated.
- Multiply only one variable term by a constant to make one coefficient match.
  - _why:_ Changing only one term alters the equation and therefore its solutions. Every term on both sides of one or both equations must be multiplied by the constant.
- Change one coefficient directly without altering any other terms in the equation.
  - _why:_ Changing a coefficient alone creates a different equation with different solutions. Instead, multiply the entire equation by a constant so that equality is preserved.
- Add a constant to one variable term until its coefficient matches the other.
  - _why:_ Adding to a variable term does not scale its coefficient correctly and changes the equation. Multiply one or both whole equations by a suitable constant instead.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the common mistake when multiplying an equation to match coefficients?

**Back:** Forgetting to multiply every term on both sides of the equals sign.

**Choices:**

- Forgetting to multiply every term on both sides of the equals sign. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying an equation by a factor means applying it to every term on both sides, preserving equality. Missing even one term changes the equation and produces an incorrect solution.
- Multiplying only the terms containing variables on both sides of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ This leaves constants unchanged even though the factor applies to the entire equation. The correct method is multiplying every term on both sides of the equals sign.
- Changing the equals sign to a plus sign after multiplying both sides.
  - _why:_ Multiplication does not change the operation joining the two sides; the equals sign must remain. The common mistake is failing to multiply every term on both sides.
- Dividing the equation by the coefficient instead of multiplying every term.
  - _why:_ Dividing may be useful in another rearrangement, but it does not match coefficients when multiplication is required. The correct precaution is multiplying every term on both sides.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Define standard form for linear simultaneous equations.

**Back:** $$ax + by = c$, where $x$ and $y$ terms are on the left and the constant is on the right.

**Choices:**

- $$ax + by = c$, where $x$ and $y$ terms are on the left and the constant is on the right. ✔
  - _why:_ This arrangement places both variable terms on the left and the constant term on the right, giving the standard linear form $ax + by = c$.
- $$ax - by = c$, where $x$ and $y$ terms are on the right and the constant is on the left.
  - _why:_ The variables and constant have been placed on opposite sides from the required arrangement, and the minus sign is not part of the general definition. The correct form is $ax + by = c$ with variables left and constant right.
- $$a + bx + cy = 0$, where all terms are on the left and there is no constant on the right.
  - _why:_ Although equations can sometimes be rearranged this way, it is not the stated standard form for two-variable linear equations. The correct form is $ax + by = c$, with variables left and constant right.
- $$x + y = ab + c$, where the variables are on the left and all coefficients are on the right.
  - _why:_ Coefficients belong with their variable terms rather than being moved to the constant side. The required standard form is $ax + by = c$, with both variable terms left and the constant right.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The standard form of $3x = 10 + y$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The standard form of $3x = 10 + y$ is $\boxed{3x - y = 10}$.

**Choices:**

- $$3x - y = 10$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $y$ from both sides of $3x = 10 + y$ places the $y$ term on the left, leaving the constant $10$ on the right.
- $$3x + y = 10$
  - _why:_ The $y$ term changes sign when it moves from the right to the left, so it must become negative. The correct standard form is $3x - y = 10$.
- $$3x - y = -10$
  - _why:_ Moving $y$ across changes its sign, but the positive constant $10$ stays positive on the right. The correct rearrangement is $3x - y = 10$.
- $$3x + y = -10$
  - _why:_ This changes the signs of both terms incorrectly. Only $y$ changes sign when subtracted from both sides, giving the correct form $3x - y = 10$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you simplify an equation containing decimals (e.g., 4.5) before solving?

**Back:** Multiply the whole equation by 10 to clear the decimal point.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the whole equation by 10 to clear the decimal point. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term in the equation by 10 changes tenths into whole numbers, such as $4.5$ into $45$, while preserving equality and making the equations easier to solve.
- Multiply only the decimal term by 10 and leave the other terms unchanged.
  - _why:_ Applying the factor to only one term changes the equation unevenly. The correct method is to multiply the whole equation by 10 so both sides remain equal.
- Divide the whole equation by 10 to move the decimal point left.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10 would make the decimal smaller rather than clear it, changing $4.5$ to $0.45$. Multiplying the whole equation by 10 clears the decimal point.
- Round each decimal term to the nearest whole number before solving.
  - _why:_ Rounding changes the exact equation and can alter the solutions. Instead, multiply the whole equation by 10 to remove the decimals without losing accuracy.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** If an equation contains 4.5, multiplying the whole equation by 10 changes it to [ ___ ].

**Back:** If an equation contains 4.5, multiplying the whole equation by 10 changes it to 45.

**Choices:**

- 45 ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying $4.5$ by 10 moves the decimal point one place to the right, giving $45$. Applying the same multiplication to the whole equation preserves its equality.
- 0.45
  - _why:_ Moving the decimal point one place left corresponds to dividing by 10, not multiplying by 10. Multiplying $4.5$ by 10 gives the correct result, $45$.
- 450
  - _why:_ This moves the decimal point two places right, which would mean multiplying by 100. Multiplying $4.5$ by 10 moves it one place right to give $45$.
- 4.05
  - _why:_ This incorrectly inserts a zero rather than moving the decimal point one place right. The calculation $4.5 \times 10$ gives $45$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the very first step when solving a word problem using simultaneous equations?

**Back:** Define variables (assign letters) for the unknown values.

**Choices:**

- Define variables (assign letters) for the unknown values. ✔
  - _why:_ Assigning variables to the unknown quantities gives the problem a clear algebraic structure. These variables can then be used to form the two simultaneous equations.
- Solve one equation before identifying the unknown values.
  - _why:_ An equation cannot be formed or solved reliably until the unknown quantities have been identified and represented. The first step is to define variables by assigning letters.
- Substitute numerical values for the unknowns immediately.
  - _why:_ The unknown values are what the problem asks you to find, so inserting numbers before forming equations is unjustified. First define variables for those unknown values.
- Eliminate one variable before translating the information into equations.
  - _why:_ Elimination is a solving method used after the equations have been formed. The first step is to define variables, allowing the worded relationships to be expressed algebraically.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** If you solve a word problem using pence, but the question asks for prices, what must you remember?

**Back:** Convert the final answers back into pounds (&pound;).

**Choices:**

- Convert the final answers back into pounds (&pound;). ✔
  - _why:_ Since 100 pence make one pound, divide each final pence answer by 100 and report the prices in pounds, matching the units requested by the question.
- Convert the final answers into pence (p).
  - _why:_ The calculations were already performed in pence, so leaving the answers in pence does not match a question asking for prices in pounds. The final answers must be converted back into pounds (&pound;).
- Multiply the final answers by 100 and write them in pounds (&pound;).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 100 makes the numerical values larger, whereas converting pence to pounds requires dividing by 100. The final answers should be converted into pounds (&pound;).
- Round the final answers to the nearest whole pound (&pound;).
  - _why:_ Rounding can lose the exact price and does not perform the required unit conversion. Divide the pence values by 100 to convert the final answers into pounds (&pound;).

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** After using elimination to find the value of $x$, how do you find the value of $y$?

**Back:** Substitute the value of $x$ back into one of the original equations.

**Choices:**

- Substitute the value of $x$ back into one of the original equations. ✔
  - _why:_ Replacing $x$ with its known value in either original equation leaves an equation containing only $y$. Solving that equation gives the corresponding value of $y$.
- Substitute the value of $y$ back into one of the original equations.
  - _why:_ At this stage the known value is $x$, so substituting $y$ reverses the order of the method and does not provide the needed value. Substitute the value of $x$ to find $y$.
- Eliminate the value of $x$ from one of the original equations.
  - _why:_ Elimination is used with both equations to remove a variable, but after finding $x$, the efficient next step is substitution. Put the value of $x$ into an original equation to find $y$.
- Divide the value of $x$ by one of the original equations.
  - _why:_ An equation is not divided by a value in this step, and doing so does not isolate $y$. Substitute the known value of $x$ into an original equation, then solve for $y$.

**Back:** In the elimination method, you add or subtract equations to remove one variable.

**Choices:**

- add ✔
  - _why:_ Adding the two equations can make opposite coefficients cancel, removing one variable and leaving an equation that contains only the other variable.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplication may first be used to create matching coefficients, but the equations are then added or subtracted. The missing word is add.
- divide
  - _why:_ Dividing an equation can simplify its coefficients, but it does not describe the combining step in elimination. The missing word is add.
- factorise
  - _why:_ Factorising rewrites an expression as a product and is not the operation used to combine equations. In elimination, you add or subtract equations.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** In the equations $4x + 3y = 26$ and $x - 3y = -1$, what is the result of adding them?

**Back:** $$5x = 25$

**Choices:**

- $$5x = 25$ ✔
  - _why:_ Adding gives $4x+x=5x$, while $3y+(-3y)=0$ and $26+(-1)=25$. Therefore the resulting equation is $5x=25$.
- $$3x = 27$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly subtracts the $x$ terms and treats $-1$ as positive. Adding correctly gives $4x+x=5x$ and $26+(-1)=25$, so $5x=25$.
- $$5x = 27$
  - _why:_ The variable terms have been combined correctly, but the constants have not: $26+(-1)$ is $25$, not $27$. The correct result is $5x=25$.
- $$5x = 26$
  - _why:_ This cancels the $y$ terms but overlooks the $-1$ on the right-hand side. Since $26+(-1)=25$, the correct result is $5x=25$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean to eliminate a variable?

**Back:** To remove it from the system so you are left with an equation in only one variable.

**Choices:**

- To remove it from the system so you are left with an equation in only one variable. ✔
  - _why:_ Elimination combines the equations so one variable’s terms cancel. This produces a simpler equation involving only the remaining variable, which can then be solved.
- To isolate it in the system so you are left with that variable on one side.
  - _why:_ Isolating a variable describes rearrangement or substitution, not elimination. To eliminate a variable means to remove it, leaving an equation in only one variable.
- To replace it in the system with a different variable from the other equation.
  - _why:_ Variables are not replaced by different variables during elimination. Instead, the equations are combined so one variable is removed, leaving an equation in the other variable.
- To divide it from the system so both equations are written in their simplest form.
  - _why:_ Elimination does not mean dividing a variable out of the equations. It means combining the equations to remove one variable and obtain a one-variable equation.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** Rearranging $x + 4y = 12$ to match $3x - y = 10$ involves multiplying the first equation by [ ___ ] to make the $x$ coefficients match.

**Back:** Rearranging $x + 4y = 12$ to match $3x - y = 10$ involves multiplying the first equation by 3 to make the $x$ coefficients match.

**Choices:**

- 3 ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term in $x+4y=12$ by $3$ gives $3x+12y=36$, so its $x$ coefficient matches the $3x$ coefficient in the other equation.
- $$\frac{1}{3}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $\frac{1}{3}$ changes the first $x$ coefficient to $\frac{1}{3}$ rather than $3$. The required multiplier is $3$.
- $$-3$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $-3$ produces an $x$ coefficient of $-3$, which is opposite rather than matching. To make both $x$ coefficients $3$, multiply by $3$.
- 4
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $4$ makes the first $x$ coefficient $4$, so it does not match the second equation’s coefficient. The correct multiplier is $3$.
