## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** Which two types of lines enclose a circle sector?

**Back:** Two radiiOne arc

**Choices:**

- Two radiiOne arc ✔
  - _why:_ The two radii run from the centre to the circumference, while the arc joins their endpoints along the circle, together forming the sector’s boundary.
- Two chordsOne arc
  - _why:_ Chords join two points on the circumference and do not necessarily pass through the centre. A sector is enclosed by two radii and one arc.
- One radiusOne chord
  - _why:_ Confusing a sector with another region leads to including a chord. A sector’s straight boundaries are two radii, and its curved boundary is one arc.
- Two radiiOne chord
  - _why:_ The endpoints of the radii are joined around the circumference by an arc, not directly by a chord. The correct boundaries are two radii and one arc.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Formula for the circumference of a full circle.

**Back:** $$C = 2\pi r$

**Choices:**

- $$C = 2\pi r$ ✔
  - _why:_ Circumference is the distance around the circle. Since the diameter is $2r$ and circumference equals $\pi$ times the diameter, $C=2\pi r$.
- $$C = \pi r$
  - _why:_ This treats the radius as though it were the diameter, making the circumference half its correct value. Using the diameter $2r$ gives $C=2\pi r$.
- $$C = 2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the radius is associated with area, not circumference, and gives square units. Circumference is a length, so the correct formula is $C=2\pi r$.
- $$C = \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the formula for the area of a circle, not its circumference. The distance around a circle is calculated using $C=2\pi r$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Formula for the area of a full circle.

**Back:** $$A = \pi r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A circle’s area depends on the square of its radius, so the formula is $A=\pi r^2$. The squared radius also produces the required square units.
- $$A = 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ This calculates the circumference, which is the distance around the circle rather than its area. The area enclosed by the circle is $A=\pi r^2$.
- $$A = \pi r$
  - _why:_ Failing to square the radius gives linear rather than square units. Because area measures a two-dimensional region, the correct formula is $A=\pi r^2$.
- $$A = 2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ The unnecessary factor of $2$ doubles the circle’s area. The full area is found by multiplying the squared radius by $\pi$, giving $A=\pi r^2$.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** A sector with central angle $\theta$ represents the fraction $\text{[...]}$ of a whole circle.

**Back:** A sector with central angle $\theta$ represents the fraction $\boxed{\frac{\theta}{360}}$ of a whole circle.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{\theta}{360}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A full turn contains $360$ degrees, so a central angle of $\theta$ degrees forms $\theta$ out of $360$ equal angular parts of the circle.
- $$\frac{360}{\theta}$
  - _why:_ Reversing the fraction gives how many such angles fit into $360$ degrees, not the fraction occupied by one sector. The required fraction is $\frac{\theta}{360}$.
- $$\frac{\theta}{180}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by $180$ compares the angle with a semicircle rather than a whole circle. A whole turn is $360$ degrees, so the fraction is $\frac{\theta}{360}$.
- $$\frac{2\theta}{360}$
  - _why:_ Doubling the central angle makes the fraction twice as large as the sector actually occupies. The angle should be compared directly with $360$, giving $\frac{\theta}{360}$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Formula for the area of a sector.

**Back:** $$A = \frac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The sector occupies the fraction $\frac{\theta}{360}$ of the full circle. Multiplying this fraction by the circle’s area $\pi r^2$ gives the sector’s area.
- $$A = \frac{360}{\theta} \times \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ The angular fraction has been inverted, producing an area larger than the whole circle for most sectors. The correct fraction is $\frac{\theta}{360}$, so $A=\frac{\theta}{360}\times\pi r^2$.
- $$A = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ This multiplies the sector fraction by the circle’s circumference, so it calculates arc length rather than area. Sector area uses $\pi r^2$, giving $A=\frac{\theta}{360}\times\pi r^2$.
- $$A = \frac{\theta}{180} \times \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ Using $180$ compares the central angle with a semicircle and doubles the required area. A whole circle has $360$ degrees, so use $A=\frac{\theta}{360}\times\pi r^2$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What does arc length represent in a sector?

**Back:** The length of the curved edge.

**Choices:**

- The length of the curved edge. ✔
  - _why:_ The arc is the part of the circle’s circumference between the endpoints of the two radii, so arc length measures the sector’s curved boundary.
- The length of the two radii.
  - _why:_ The radii form the sector’s straight edges, not its arc. Arc length measures only the curved section of the circumference between the two radii.
- The area of the curved region.
  - _why:_ Arc length is a one-dimensional measurement, whereas the region enclosed by the radii and arc is the sector’s area. It measures the curved edge only.
- The length of the straight chord.
  - _why:_ A chord joins two points on the circumference by a straight line, while an arc follows the circumference. Arc length is the length of the curved edge.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Formula for the arc length ($L$) of a sector.

**Back:** $$L = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$

**Choices:**

- $$L = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$ ✔
  - _why:_ The arc occupies the fraction $\frac{\theta}{360}$ of the full circumference. Multiplying that fraction by $2\pi r$ therefore gives the required arc length.
- $$L = \frac{360}{\theta} \times 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ Inverting the angular fraction usually makes the result larger than the full circumference. The arc is $\frac{\theta}{360}$ of the circumference, so $L=\frac{\theta}{360}\times2\pi r$.
- $$L = \frac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $\pi r^2$ calculates sector area and produces square units. Arc length requires the full circumference $2\pi r$, giving $L=\frac{\theta}{360}\times2\pi r$.
- $$L = \frac{\theta}{180} \times 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ Using $180$ treats a semicircle as the full turn and doubles the arc length. The central angle must be divided by $360$, giving $L=\frac{\theta}{360}\times2\pi r$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a major sector?

**Back:** A sector where the central angle is greater than 180 degrees.

**Choices:**

- A sector where the central angle is greater than 180 degrees. ✔
  - _why:_ A central angle greater than $180$ degrees covers more than half of the circle, so the resulting region is classified as a major sector.
- A sector where the central angle is less than 180 degrees.
  - _why:_ An angle below $180$ degrees forms less than half a circle, which defines a minor sector. A major sector has a central angle greater than $180$ degrees.
- A sector where the central angle is equal to 180 degrees.
  - _why:_ An angle of exactly $180$ degrees forms a semicircle, not a major sector. A major sector must have a central angle strictly greater than $180$ degrees.
- A sector where the central angle is greater than 360 degrees.
  - _why:_ A standard sector’s central angle cannot exceed one complete turn of $360$ degrees. A major sector is defined by an angle greater than $180$ but below $360$ degrees.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Which three lengths must be added to find the perimeter of a sector?

**Back:** The arc lengthTwo radii

**Choices:**

- The arc lengthTwo radii ✔
  - _why:_ The curved arc and the two radii form the sector’s complete boundary, so adding these three lengths gives its perimeter.
- The arc lengthTwo diameters
  - _why:_ Using diameters doubles the two straight boundary lengths. A sector is bounded by its arc length and two radii, not two diameters.
- The chord lengthTwo radii
  - _why:_ The chord joins the arc’s endpoints but is not part of the sector’s boundary. The perimeter consists of the arc length and two radii.
- The arc lengthOne radius
  - _why:_ This omits one of the sector’s straight sides. Both radii belong to the boundary, so the arc length and two radii must be added.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Formula for the perimeter ($P$) of a sector using arc length $L$ and radius $r$.

**Back:** $$P = L + 2r$

**Choices:**

- $$P = L + 2r$ ✔
  - _why:_ A sector’s boundary contains the curved arc of length $L$ and two straight radii of length $r$, giving $P=L+r+r=L+2r$.
- $$P = L + r$
  - _why:_ This includes only one radius and therefore misses one straight side of the sector. The correct perimeter is $P=L+2r$.
- $$P = 2L + 2r$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly counts the curved arc twice. A sector has one arc and two radii, so its perimeter is $P=L+2r$.
- $$P = L + 2\pi r$
  - _why:_ The term $2\pi r$ is the circumference of a complete circle, not the sector’s two straight sides. The correct formula is $P=L+2r$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** A semicircle has an area equal to $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** A semicircle has an area equal to $\boxed{\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A semicircle is half of a complete circle. Since a circle has area $\pi r^2$, halving it gives $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$.
- $$\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the area of a complete circle and does not account for the shape being halved. A semicircle has area $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\pi r$
  - _why:_ Omitting the square on the radius produces units of length rather than area. The semicircle’s area is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$.
- $$\frac{1}{4}\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This finds the area of a quarter-circle rather than a semicircle. A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the general strategy for finding a shaded region involving two shapes.

**Back:** Calculate the area of the larger shapeCalculate the area of the smaller shapeSubtract the smaller area from the larger

**Choices:**

- Calculate the area of the larger shapeCalculate the area of the smaller shapeSubtract the smaller area from the larger ✔
  - _why:_ The shaded region is the part of the larger shape remaining after the smaller shape is removed, so its area is larger area minus smaller area.
- Calculate the area of the larger shapeCalculate the area of the smaller shapeAdd the smaller area to the larger
  - _why:_ Adding counts both complete shapes instead of removing the unshaded inner part. The shaded area is found by subtracting the smaller area from the larger.
- Calculate the area of the smaller shapeCalculate the area of the larger shapeSubtract the larger area from the smaller
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction usually produces a negative result, which cannot represent an area. Subtract the smaller area from the larger area instead.
- Calculate the perimeter of the larger shapeCalculate the perimeter of the smaller shapeSubtract the smaller perimeter from the larger
  - _why:_ Perimeters measure boundary lengths and cannot determine the size of a shaded region. Calculate both areas, then subtract the smaller area from the larger.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Shortcut formula for sector area if the arc length ($L$) and radius ($r$) are known.

**Back:** $$A = \frac{1}{2}rL$

**Choices:**

- $$A = \frac{1}{2}rL$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $L=r\theta$ with the sector formula $A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ gives $A=\frac{1}{2}rL$ when the arc length and radius are known.
- $$A = rL$
  - _why:_ This misses the factor of one half and therefore gives twice the sector’s actual area. The correct shortcut is $A=\frac{1}{2}rL$.
- $$A = \frac{1}{2}r^2L$
  - _why:_ The extra factor of $r$ gives cubic units, so the result cannot be an area. The dimensionally correct formula is $A=\frac{1}{2}rL$.
- $$A = \frac{L}{2r}$
  - _why:_ Dividing arc length by radius relates to the angle in radians and produces no area units. Sector area is given by $A=\frac{1}{2}rL$.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ The radius may remain as a variable only when it is unknown, but “in terms of $\pi$” specifically requires the $\pi$ symbol to remain.
- $$A$
  - _why:_ The symbol $A$ usually labels area and is not what the instruction refers to. An answer in terms of $\pi$ must leave $\pi$ uncalculated.
- $$\theta$
  - _why:_ The symbol $\theta$ represents an angle, not the circle constant named in the instruction. The final answer must retain the $\pi$ symbol.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a semicircle is described as having a diameter of 10 cm, the value of $r$ used in formulas is $\text{[...]}$ cm.

**Back:** If a semicircle is described as having a diameter of 10 cm, the value of $r$ used in formulas is $\boxed{5}$ cm.

**Choices:**

- $$5$ ✔
  - _why:_ The radius is half the diameter, so a diameter of $10$ cm gives $r=10\div2=5$ cm.
- $$10$
  - _why:_ This treats the given diameter as though it were the radius. Since the radius is half the diameter, the correct value is $5$ cm.
- $$20$
  - _why:_ This doubles the diameter instead of halving it. The radius is the distance from the centre to the circumference, so it is $5$ cm.
- $$100$
  - _why:_ This squares the diameter, confusing a length with a step used in area calculations. The formula requires the radius, which is $10\div2=5$ cm.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you keep the full calculator value for calculations and only round at the final answer?

**Back:** To prevent rounding errors that could make the final 3 significant figure answer inaccurate.

**Choices:**

- To prevent rounding errors that could make the final 3 significant figure answer inaccurate. ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping full calculator precision prevents small intermediate rounding differences from accumulating. Rounding only the final result gives the most accurate answer to 3 significant figures.
- To ensure every intermediate value is rounded to 3 significant figures before it is reused.
  - _why:_ Rounding each intermediate value can accumulate errors and alter the final result. Keep full calculator values throughout and round only the final answer to 3 significant figures.
- To avoid converting between degrees and radians until the final line of working.
  - _why:_ Angle-unit conversion is separate from the issue of premature rounding. Full calculator values are retained to prevent accumulated rounding errors before the final answer.
- To ensure the final answer is always rounded up rather than to the nearest value.
  - _why:_ Significant-figure rounding may require rounding either up or down, depending on the next digit. Full values are kept to make that final rounding accurate.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the percentage of a rectangle that is shaded?

**Back:** $$\frac{\text{shaded area}}{\text{rectangle area}} \times 100$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{\text{shaded area}}{\text{rectangle area}} \times 100$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the shaded area by the rectangle’s total area gives the shaded proportion. Multiplying this decimal by 100 converts the proportion into a percentage.
- $$\frac{\text{rectangle area}}{\text{shaded area}} \times 100$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required fraction and can produce a percentage greater than 100%. The shaded area must be divided by the total rectangle area, then multiplied by 100.
- $$\frac{\text{shaded area}}{\text{rectangle area}}$
  - _why:_ This calculates the shaded proportion as a decimal or fraction but does not convert it to a percentage. The result must also be multiplied by 100.
- $$\frac{\text{unshaded area}}{\text{rectangle area}} \times 100$
  - _why:_ This calculates the percentage that is unshaded rather than shaded. The numerator must be the shaded area, while the denominator remains the total rectangle area.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** A common mistake when calculating sector perimeter is to calculate only the arc length and forget to add the $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** A common mistake when calculating sector perimeter is to calculate only the arc length and forget to add the $\boxed{two radii}$.

**Choices:**

- $$two radii$ ✔
  - _why:_ A sector’s boundary consists of the curved arc and two straight radii. Its perimeter is therefore the arc length plus the lengths of both radii.
- $$radius$
  - _why:_ Adding only one radius misses one straight side of the sector. The complete perimeter includes the arc length and two radii, one on each side.
- $$diameter$
  - _why:_ A sector does not generally have a diameter as part of its boundary. Its two straight boundary edges are radii, so both radii must be added.
- $$chord$
  - _why:_ A chord joins the arc’s endpoints directly and belongs to a segment boundary, not a sector boundary. A sector’s perimeter includes the arc and two radii.

  - _why:_ An angle of $90^\circ$ represents one quarter of a circle, not one half. The correct comparison angle for half a circle is $180^\circ$.

  - _why:_ An angle of $360^\circ$ represents the whole circle, so $200^\circ$ is not larger than it. The angle representing half a circle is $180^\circ$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Find the area of a sector with radius 8 cm and arc length $5\pi$ cm.

**Back:** $$20\pi$ cm$^2$

**Choices:**

- $$20\pi$ cm$^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using sector area $A=\frac{1}{2}rs$ with radius $8$ cm and arc length $5\pi$ cm gives $A=\frac{1}{2}\times8\times5\pi=20\pi$ cm$^2$.
- $$40\pi$ cm$^2$
  - _why:_ This uses $A=rs$ and omits the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$. Sector area is $\frac{1}{2}rs$, giving $\frac{1}{2}\times8\times5\pi=20\pi$ cm$^2$.
- $$10\pi$ cm$^2$
  - _why:_ This introduces an extra division by 2, effectively using $A=\frac{1}{4}rs$. The correct formula is $A=\frac{1}{2}rs$, which gives $20\pi$ cm$^2$.
- $$64\pi$ cm$^2$
  - _why:_ This is the area $\pi r^2$ of the entire circle and ignores the given arc length. Using $A=\frac{1}{2}rs$ gives the sector area $20\pi$ cm$^2$.
