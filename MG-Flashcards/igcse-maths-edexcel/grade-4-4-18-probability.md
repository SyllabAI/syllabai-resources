## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What numerical range represents all valid probabilities?

**Back:** Between 0 and 1.

**Choices:**

- Between 0 and 1. ✔
  - _why:_ A probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 for an impossible event to 1 for a certain event, including both endpoints.
- Between -1 and 1.
  - _why:_ Confusing probability with quantities that may be negative gives this range. Probabilities cannot be negative, so every valid probability lies between 0 and 1.
- Between 1 and 100.
  - _why:_ This confuses decimal probabilities with percentages. A percentage probability ranges from 0% to 100%, but a numerical probability ranges from 0 to 1.
- Between 0 and 100.
  - _why:_ This is the range used when probabilities are expressed as percentages. In decimal or fractional form, all probabilities must lie between 0 and 1.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** A probability of [ ___ ] means the event is impossible, while a probability of 1 means it is certain.

**Back:** A probability of 0 means the event is impossible, while a probability of 1 means it is certain.

**Choices:**

- 0 ✔
  - _why:_ A probability of 0 represents an event with no chance of occurring, so it means that the event is impossible.
- 1
  - _why:_ This reverses the meanings of the endpoints. A probability of 1 means an event is certain, whereas a probability of 0 means it is impossible.
- 0.5
  - _why:_ A probability of 0.5 means the event has an equal chance of occurring or not occurring. An impossible event has probability 0.
- -1
  - _why:_ Negative probabilities are not valid in this probability model. The lowest possible probability is 0, and this represents an impossible event.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean for a die or coin to be biased?

**Back:** The outcomes are not equally likely.

**Choices:**

- The outcomes are not equally likely. ✔
  - _why:_ A biased coin or die favours some outcomes over others, so the possible outcomes do not all have the same probability.
- The outcomes are always equally likely.
  - _why:_ Equal likelihood describes a fair coin or die, not a biased one. Bias means that at least one outcome has a different probability from another.
- The outcomes are all equally certain.
  - _why:_ Individual results of a random trial are not all certain. A biased object instead has outcomes whose probabilities are not all equal.
- The outcomes are not mutually exclusive.
  - _why:_ Mutual exclusivity concerns whether outcomes can occur together, not whether an object is biased. Bias means the outcomes are not equally likely.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What must the sum of probabilities be for a complete set of exhaustive outcomes?

**Back:** 1

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ A complete set includes every possible outcome, so one of its outcomes must occur. Therefore, their probabilities add up to 1.
- 0
  - _why:_ A total of 0 would mean every listed outcome is impossible. For a complete set containing every possible result, the probabilities must sum to 1.
- 0.5
  - _why:_ A total of 0.5 accounts for only half of the full probability. Exhaustive outcomes cover all possibilities, so their probabilities must total 1.
- 100
  - _why:_ This confuses numerical probabilities with percentages. A complete set totals 100% when using percentages, which is equivalent to a probability of 1.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** Outcomes are [ ___ ] when the list includes every possible result.

**Back:** Outcomes are exhaustive when the list includes every possible result.

**Choices:**

- exhaustive ✔
  - _why:_ A list of outcomes is exhaustive when it covers every possible result of the experiment, leaving no possible outcome unlisted.
- mutually exclusive
  - _why:_ Mutually exclusive outcomes cannot occur at the same time, but this does not mean every possible result has been listed. Such a list is exhaustive.
- independent
  - _why:_ Independent outcomes do not affect each other’s probabilities. The term for a list that includes every possible result is exhaustive.
- equally likely
  - _why:_ Equally likely outcomes have the same probability, but they do not necessarily include every possible result. A complete list of possibilities is exhaustive.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If a table lists all possible outcomes, how do you find one missing probability?

**Back:** Subtract all known probabilities from 1.

**Choices:**

- Subtract all known probabilities from 1. ✔
  - _why:_ All probabilities in a complete set sum to 1. Subtracting the total of the known probabilities from 1 therefore gives the missing probability.
- Add all known probabilities to 1.
  - _why:_ Adding the known probabilities to 1 makes the total exceed the complete probability. Instead, subtract their sum from 1 to find the missing value.
- Subtract 1 from the known probabilities.
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction usually produces a negative value, which cannot be a probability. The known probabilities must be subtracted from 1.
- Divide all known probabilities by 1.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 1 leaves the known probabilities unchanged and does not find the missing amount. Subtract their total from 1 instead.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** In a complete set, the probability left over is $0.52$. If the two missing outcomes are equally likely, what is the probability of each?

**Back:** $$0.26$ ($0.52 \div 2$)

**Choices:**

- $$0.26$ ($0.52 \div 2$) ✔
  - _why:_ The two outcomes share the remaining probability equally, so dividing 0.52 by 2 gives a probability of 0.26 for each outcome.
- $$0.104$ ($0.52 \div 5$)
  - _why:_ This divides the remaining probability into five equal parts instead of two. Since there are two equally likely missing outcomes, each probability is $0.52 \div 2 = 0.26$.
- $$1.04$ ($0.52 \times 2$)
  - _why:_ Multiplying assigns twice the total remaining probability to each outcome and even produces an invalid probability above 1. The remainder must be divided by 2, giving 0.26.
- $$0.27$ ($0.52 \div 2$)
  - _why:_ This is an arithmetic or rounding slip: 52 hundredths divided equally gives 26 hundredths, not 27 hundredths. Each probability is exactly 0.26.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If outcomes are given as fractions with different denominators, what step is required before subtracting from 1?

**Back:** Find a common denominator.

**Choices:**

- Find a common denominator. ✔
  - _why:_ Fractions must be rewritten with the same denominator before subtracting from 1, so their numerators represent comparable parts of one whole.
- Find a common numerator.
  - _why:_ Matching numerators does not make fractions directly comparable. The denominators must be made equal first, allowing the fractional parts to be subtracted correctly.
- Add the denominators together.
  - _why:_ Denominators are not combined by addition when subtracting fractions. Rewrite the fractions using a common denominator so the numerators can be compared.
- Subtract the denominators directly.
  - _why:_ Subtracting denominators changes the fraction incorrectly. A common denominator is needed before subtraction, because it expresses both fractions in equal-sized parts.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Translate the relationship "Red is three times as likely as Blue" into a ratio.

**Back:** Red : Blue = 3 : 1

**Choices:**

- Red : Blue = 3 : 1 ✔
  - _why:_ Three times as likely means that for every one equal part assigned to Blue, Red receives three equal parts, giving the ratio Red : Blue = 3 : 1.
- Red : Blue = 1 : 3
  - _why:_ This reverses the relationship, making Blue three times as likely as Red. Red being three times as likely requires three parts for Red and one for Blue.
- Red : Blue = 3 : 2
  - _why:_ The ratio must compare Red with exactly three times Blue, not with two additional parts. One part for Blue therefore corresponds to three parts for Red.
- Red : Blue = 2 : 1
  - _why:_ A ratio of 2 : 1 means Red is twice as likely as Blue, not three times as likely. The multiplier must appear as three parts.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** If outcome A is twice as likely as outcome B, you should split the remaining probability into [ ___ ] equal parts.

**Back:** If outcome A is twice as likely as outcome B, you should split the remaining probability into 3 equal parts.

**Choices:**

- 3 ✔
  - _why:_ If B has one equal probability part, A has two parts because it is twice as likely. Together, the remaining probability is split into three parts.
- 2
  - _why:_ Two parts would represent equal probabilities or an incomplete 2:1 relationship. A twice-as-likely outcome needs two parts alongside one part for the other outcome.
- 4
  - _why:_ Four equal parts could represent a different ratio, such as 3:1, but not a 2:1 relationship. The required parts are two plus one.
- 5
  - _why:_ Five parts does not follow from the 2:1 ratio. Assigning two parts to A and one to B gives three equal parts altogether.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you turn a ratio (e.g., $4:3:5$) into probabilities?

**Back:** Divide each part by the total number of parts ($12$ in this case).

**Choices:**

- Divide each part by the total number of parts ($12$ in this case). ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio has 4 + 3 + 5 = 12 total parts. Dividing each individual part by 12 converts the ratio into probabilities whose total is 1.
- Divide each part by the largest part ($5$ in this case).
  - _why:_ The largest part is not the whole ratio. All parts must be added first, giving 12, because probabilities are each part divided by the total.
- Divide each part by the number of ratios ($3$ in this case).
  - _why:_ There are three entries, but their sizes are different, so counting entries does not give the total probability parts. Adding 4, 3 and 5 gives 12.
- Divide each part by the difference between the largest and smallest parts ($1$ in this case).
  - _why:_ The difference between parts does not represent the whole ratio. The correct denominator is their sum, 12, so all resulting probabilities add to 1.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for expected frequency.

**Back:** $$\text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Expected frequency estimates how often an event should occur, so multiply the event’s probability by the total number of trials.
- $$\text{probability} + \text{number of trials}$
  - _why:_ Adding these quantities mixes a proportion with a count and does not estimate occurrences. Expected frequency is found by multiplying probability by the number of trials.
- $$\frac{\text{probability}}{\text{number of trials}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by the number of trials makes the value smaller as more trials are performed, which is the opposite of expected frequency. Multiplication gives the expected count.
- $$\text{number of trials} - \text{probability}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting a probability from a count has no suitable interpretation for expected frequency. The probability must scale the number of trials through multiplication.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** A biased die has $P(6) = 0.31$. Estimate the number of sixes in $200$ rolls.

**Back:** 62 ($0.31 \times 200$)

**Choices:**

- 62 ($0.31 \times 200$) ✔
  - _why:_ Expected frequency equals probability multiplied by the number of trials: $0.31 \times 200 = 62$. Therefore, about 62 sixes are expected.
- 31 ($0.31 \times 100$)
  - _why:_ This uses 100 trials instead of the stated 200, so it halves the expected frequency. Using all rolls gives $0.31 \times 200 = 62$.
- 124 ($0.62 \times 200$)
  - _why:_ The probability has been doubled from 0.31 to 0.62, doubling the estimate as well. The given probability produces $0.31 \times 200 = 62$.
- 6.2 ($0.31 \times 20$)
  - _why:_ This uses 20 rolls rather than 200, likely from dropping a zero. The correct calculation uses all trials: $0.31 \times 200 = 62$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** An expected frequency is an [ ___ ], not a guaranteed result.

**Back:** An expected frequency is an estimate, not a guaranteed result.

**Choices:**

- estimate ✔
  - _why:_ An expected frequency is a calculated long-run prediction based on probability and the number of trials. Random variation means the actual result is not guaranteed.
- certainty
  - _why:_ Certainty means the result must happen, but expected frequency only predicts what is likely on average. Random outcomes can produce a different actual frequency.
- guarantee
  - _why:_ A guarantee implies an outcome is assured, whereas expected frequency is only a probability-based prediction. The actual number may be higher or lower.
- observation
  - _why:_ An observation is a result recorded after an experiment, not the calculated predicted frequency. Expected frequency is an estimate that may differ from observations.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the basic formula relating probability, the number in an event, and the total number?

**Back:** $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number in event}}{\text{total number}}$

**Choices:**

- $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number in event}}{\text{total number}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ For equally likely outcomes, probability is the number of outcomes belonging to the event divided by the total number of possible outcomes.
- $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{total number}}{\text{number in event}}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the fraction, often producing a value greater than 1, which cannot be a probability. Event outcomes must be divided by all outcomes.
- $$P(\text{event}) = \text{number in event} + \text{total number}$
  - _why:_ Adding the two counts does not produce a proportion and can exceed 1. Probability requires the event count divided by the total count.
- $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number in event}}{\text{total number} - 1}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting one from the total incorrectly removes an outcome from the sample space. The denominator must include every possible outcome.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** If $P(\text{Red}) = 0.2$ and there are $14$ red counters, what is the total number of counters?

**Back:** 70 ($14 \div 0.2$)

**Choices:**

- 70 ($14 \div 0.2$) ✔
  - _why:_ The 14 red counters represent 0.2 of the total, so dividing 14 by 0.2 gives the total number of counters: $14 \div 0.2 = 70$.
- 2.8 ($14 \times 0.2$)
  - _why:_ Multiplying finds 20% of 14, rather than the total from which 14 is 20%. The required calculation is $14 \div 0.2 = 70$.
- 7 ($14 \div 2$)
  - _why:_ Dropping the zero from 0.2 changes the divisor to 2 and ignores its place value. Dividing by the stated probability gives $14 \div 0.2 = 70$.
- 17.5 ($14 \div 0.8$)
  - _why:_ Using 0.8 mistakenly divides by the probability of not selecting red. The 14 red counters represent probability 0.2, so the total is $14 \div 0.2 = 70$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** If two outcomes cannot happen at the same time, how do you calculate $P(A \text{ or } B)$?

**Back:** Add their probabilities ($P(A) + P(B)$).

**Choices:**

- Add their probabilities ($P(A) + P(B)$). ✔
  - _why:_ Mutually exclusive outcomes have no overlap, so the probability that either occurs is found by adding their individual probabilities: $P(A \text{ or } B)=P(A)+P(B)$.
- Multiply their probabilities ($P(A) \times P(B)$).
  - _why:_ Multiplication is associated with finding the probability of combined independent events, not mutually exclusive alternatives. Since $A$ and $B$ cannot coincide, their probabilities must be added.
- Subtract their probabilities ($P(A) - P(B)$).
  - _why:_ Subtraction does not give the probability that either mutually exclusive outcome occurs. With no overlap to account for, the correct rule is $P(A \text{ or } B)=P(A)+P(B)$.
- Add their probabilities, then subtract from 1 ($1 - P(A) - P(B)$).
  - _why:_ Subtracting the sum from 1 finds the probability that neither event occurs, not that either occurs. For mutually exclusive events, the required probability is $P(A)+P(B)$.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the total number of items from a category count, [ ___ ] the count by the probability.

**Back:** To find the total number of items from a category count, divide the count by the probability.

**Choices:**

- divide ✔
  - _why:_ The category count equals its probability multiplied by the total. Reversing this relationship means dividing the category count by its probability to find the total.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplication finds an expected category count when the total is already known. To work backwards from a category count to the total, divide the count by the probability.
- subtract
  - _why:_ Subtracting a probability from a count mixes unlike quantities and does not reverse the probability relationship. The total is found by dividing the category count by its probability.
- add
  - _why:_ Adding the probability to the count does not scale the category up to the full set. Since the count is a fraction of the total, it must be divided by the probability.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Define an outcome versus an event.

**Back:** Outcome: one possible result.Event: the result(s) we are interested in.

**Choices:**

- Outcome: one possible result.Event: the result(s) we are interested in. ✔
  - _why:_ An outcome is an individual possible result of an experiment. An event is the specified outcome or collection of outcomes whose probability is being considered.
- Outcome: the result(s) we are interested in.Event: one possible result.
  - _why:_ These definitions reverse the two terms. An outcome is one possible result, while an event is the particular outcome or group of outcomes being considered.
- Outcome: all possible results.Event: one repetition of an experiment.
  - _why:_ All possible results form the sample space, while one repetition is a trial. An outcome is one possible result, and an event contains the result or results of interest.
- Outcome: one repetition of an experiment.Event: the probability of a result.
  - _why:_ One repetition is a trial, and a probability is a numerical measure rather than an event. An outcome is one result; an event is the result or results being studied.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** A seed has $P(\text{grow}) = 0.82$. Why is this not a guarantee?

**Back:** Because the probability is less than 1.

**Choices:**

- Because the probability is less than 1. ✔
  - _why:_ Only a probability of 1 represents certainty. Since 0.82 is below 1, there remains a probability of $1-0.82=0.18$ that the seed will not grow.
- Because the probability is greater than 0.5.
  - _why:_ A probability above 0.5 means growth is more likely than non-growth, but it does not prevent failure. The result is not guaranteed because 0.82 is less than 1.
- Because the probability is more than 0.
  - _why:_ A probability above 0 only means the event is possible; it does not explain uncertainty. The seed is not guaranteed to grow because 0.82 is less than 1.
- Because the probability is equal to 1.
  - _why:_ The stated probability is 0.82, not 1. A probability of 1 would guarantee growth, whereas 0.82 leaves a 0.18 probability that the seed will not grow.
