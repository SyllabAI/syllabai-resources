## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a percentage change, which amount goes on the bottom of the fraction?

**Back:** The original amount.

**Choices:**

- The original amount. ✔
  - _why:_ Percentage change compares the change with the starting value, so the original amount is the denominator before multiplying the fraction by 100.
- The new amount.
  - _why:_ Using the new amount as the denominator compares the change with the final value. Percentage change must use the original amount as the denominator.
- The actual change.
  - _why:_ The change belongs on the top of the fraction, not the bottom. The denominator must be the original amount because it is the starting reference value.
- The average amount.
  - _why:_ A percentage change is measured relative to the starting value, not the midpoint of the two values. The original amount therefore goes on the bottom.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find a percentage change, divide the [ ___ ] by the original amount, then multiply by [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find a percentage change, divide the change by the original amount, then multiply by 100.

**Choices:**

- To find a percentage change, divide the change by the original amount, then multiply by 100. ✔
  - _why:_ The change measures the difference between the amounts, and dividing it by the original amount gives the proportional change. Multiplying by 100 converts this proportion into a percentage.
- To find a percentage change, divide the new amount by the original amount, then multiply by 100.
  - _why:_ Using the new amount as the numerator calculates how large the final value is relative to the original. The numerator must be the change, followed by multiplication by 100.
- To find a percentage change, divide the change by the original amount, then multiply by 10.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 does not convert a decimal proportion into a percentage and makes the result ten times too small. The change must be divided by the original amount and multiplied by 100.
- To find a percentage change, divide the original amount by the original amount, then multiply by 100.
  - _why:_ Dividing the original amount by itself always gives 1, so this would always produce 100%. The change must instead be divided by the original amount.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** For an increase, how do you find the actual change?

**Back:** New amount - original amount.

**Choices:**

- New amount - original amount. ✔
  - _why:_ During an increase, the new amount is larger. Subtracting the smaller original amount from the larger new amount gives the positive size of the increase.
- Original amount - new amount.
  - _why:_ This subtraction order is used for a decrease. For an increase, subtract the original amount from the new amount to obtain a positive actual change.
- New amount + original amount.
  - _why:_ Adding the two amounts gives their total, not the difference between them. The actual increase is found using new amount minus original amount.
- New amount ÷ original amount.
  - _why:_ Dividing the amounts gives a multiplier rather than the actual numerical change. For an increase, the actual change is new amount minus original amount.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** For a decrease, how do you find the actual change?

**Back:** Original amount - new amount.

**Choices:**

- Original amount - new amount. ✔
  - _why:_ During a decrease, the original amount is larger. Subtracting the smaller new amount from it gives the positive size of the decrease.
- New amount - original amount.
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction produces a negative result because the new amount is smaller. The positive size of the decrease is original amount minus new amount.
- Original amount + new amount.
  - _why:_ Adding the amounts finds their combined total rather than the amount lost. The actual decrease is found by subtracting the new amount from the original amount.
- Original amount ÷ new amount.
  - _why:_ Dividing the amounts calculates a multiplier or proportion, not the actual change. The decrease itself is original amount minus new amount.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** A percentage increase is used when the new amount is [ ___ ] than the original; a percentage decrease when it is [ ___ ].

**Back:** A percentage increase is used when the new amount is bigger than the original; a percentage decrease when it is smaller.

**Choices:**

- A percentage increase is used when the new amount is bigger than the original; a percentage decrease when it is smaller. ✔
  - _why:_ An increase means the value has risen, so the new amount is bigger than the original. A decrease means it has fallen, making the new amount smaller.
- A percentage increase is used when the new amount is smaller than the original; a percentage decrease when it is bigger.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two directions. A bigger new amount represents an increase, while a smaller new amount represents a decrease.
- A percentage increase is used when the new amount is equal than the original; a percentage decrease when it is smaller.
  - _why:_ Equal amounts represent no change, not an increase. An increase requires the new amount to be bigger, while a decrease requires it to be smaller.
- A percentage increase is used when the new amount is bigger than the original; a percentage decrease when it is bigger.
  - _why:_ A new amount bigger than the original cannot represent a decrease. An increase uses bigger, whereas a decrease uses smaller.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If a question asks for percentage change, what direction should the final answer usually state?

**Back:** State increase or decrease.

**Choices:**

- State increase or decrease. ✔
  - _why:_ The percentage alone gives the size of the change but not its direction. Stating increase or decrease makes clear whether the value rose or fell.
- State positive or negative.
  - _why:_ Positive and negative signs may arise during calculation, but the final contextual answer should identify the direction explicitly by stating increase or decrease.
- State old or new.
  - _why:_ Old and new identify which values were compared, not the direction of change. The final answer should state whether the result is an increase or decrease.
- State maximum or minimum.
  - _why:_ Maximum and minimum describe extreme values rather than whether a quantity rose or fell. A percentage-change answer should state increase or decrease.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Council tax rises from £1840 to £1935. What is the percentage increase to 1 decimal place?

**Back:** 5.2%

**Choices:**

- 5.2% ✔
  - _why:_ The increase is £1935 - £1840 = £95. Dividing £95 by the original £1840 and multiplying by 100 gives about 5.163%, which rounds to 5.2%.
- 4.9%
  - _why:_ This results from dividing the £95 increase by the new amount of £1935. Percentage increase must use the original £1840 as the denominator, giving 5.2%.
- 51.6%
  - _why:_ This is ten times too large because a place-value error effectively uses £184 instead of £1840 as the denominator. The correct calculation gives 5.2%.
- 105.2%
  - _why:_ Dividing £1935 by £1840 gives the new amount as a percentage of the original, including the initial 100%. The increase alone is 5.2%.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** Profit happens when the selling price is [ ___ ] than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is [ ___ ].

**Back:** Profit happens when the selling price is more than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is less.

**Choices:**

- Profit happens when the selling price is more than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is less. ✔
  - _why:_ Selling for more than the item cost produces a profit, while selling for less than the item cost means money has been lost.
- Profit happens when the selling price is less than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is more.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two relationships. Profit occurs when the selling price exceeds the cost price, while loss occurs when the selling price is below it.
- Profit happens when the selling price is equal than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is less.
  - _why:_ An equal selling price and cost price gives neither profit nor loss. Profit requires the selling price to be more than the cost price.
- Profit happens when the selling price is more than the cost price; loss happens when the selling price is equal.
  - _why:_ Selling at the cost price is breaking even, not making a loss. A loss occurs only when the selling price is less than the cost price.

**Back:** The cost price.

**Choices:**

- The cost price. ✔
  - _why:_ The cost price is the amount paid originally, before the item is sold. Percentage profit or loss is therefore usually calculated as a percentage of the cost price.

  - _why:_ The selling price is the final amount received, not the original amount. Profit and loss percentages are usually based on the cost price paid initially.
- The profit amount.
  - _why:_ Profit is the difference between the selling price and cost price, rather than the starting amount. The original amount used is the cost price.
- The percentage change.
  - _why:_ The percentage change is the result being calculated, not the original monetary amount. In profit and loss calculations, the cost price is normally the original amount.

**Back:** 5.5%

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Before combining prices like 55p and £15, what should you do?

**Back:** Convert pence to pounds.

**Choices:**

- Convert pence to pounds. ✔
  - _why:_ Both prices must use the same unit before they are combined. Since the other price is in pounds, 55p should be written as £0.55.
- Treat pence as pounds.
  - _why:_ Reading 55p as £55 makes the value one hundred times too large. Pence must be divided by 100, so 55p becomes £0.55.
- Round pence to pounds.
  - _why:_ Rounding would discard some or all of the pence and change the exact value. Convert pence to pounds by dividing by 100 before combining prices.
- Convert prices to percentages.
  - _why:_ Percentages describe proportions and are not a common currency unit for adding prices. Convert the pence amount to pounds so both amounts have matching units.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** Rounding to 1 decimal place means [ ___ ]; rounding to 3 significant figures means [ ___ ].

**Back:** Rounding to 1 decimal place means one digit after the decimal point; rounding to 3 significant figures means the first three important digits.

**Choices:**

- Rounding to 1 decimal place means one digit after the decimal point; rounding to 3 significant figures means the first three important digits. ✔
  - _why:_ Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, whereas significant figures begin at the first non-zero digit and count the important digits from there.
- Rounding to 1 decimal place means one digit before the decimal point; rounding to 3 significant figures means the first three important digits.
  - _why:_ Decimal places are counted after, not before, the decimal point. Therefore, 1 decimal place means one digit after the decimal point, while 3 significant figures uses the first three important digits.
- Rounding to 1 decimal place means one digit after the decimal point; rounding to 3 significant figures means the first three digits after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ This confuses significant figures with decimal places. Three significant figures are the first three important digits, beginning with the first non-zero digit, not necessarily three digits after the decimal point.
- Rounding to 1 decimal place means one significant digit; rounding to 3 significant figures means the first three digits after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two rounding methods. One decimal place gives one digit after the decimal point, while three significant figures retain the first three important digits.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** In a multi-step selling question, what should you work out before profit or loss?

**Back:** The total selling price.

**Choices:**

- The total selling price. ✔
  - _why:_ Profit or loss compares the total money received from sales with the total cost. Therefore, all selling amounts must first be combined to find the total selling price.
- The total cost price.
  - _why:_ The total cost may already be given, but the separate sales still need combining. Work out the total selling price first, then compare it with the total cost.
- The selling price per item.
  - _why:_ Using one item’s selling price ignores how many items were sold at each price. Calculate the total selling price from all actual sales before finding profit or loss.
- The profit made per item.
  - _why:_ Per-item profit is unreliable when items sell for different prices or remain unsold. First calculate the total selling price, then subtract the total cost to find overall profit or loss.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** If some items are not sold, should they be included in the selling price?

**Back:** No - use only money actually made from selling.

**Choices:**

- No - use only money actually made from selling. ✔
  - _why:_ Selling price means the revenue actually received. Unsold items produce no sales revenue, so only money collected from items that were sold belongs in the total selling price.
- Yes - use money that could have been made from selling.
  - _why:_ This treats expected revenue as if it had been received. Unsold items contribute nothing to the selling price, so use only money actually made from selling.
- Yes - include all items at their original marked selling price.
  - _why:_ A marked price does not create revenue unless an item is sold. The total selling price includes only the money actually received from completed sales.
- Yes - add the unsold items’ cost to the selling price.
  - _why:_ This mixes cost with sales revenue. Unsold items remain part of the original cost but add nothing to the selling price, which includes only money actually made from selling.

  - _why:_ This calculates the £7.20 revenue as a percentage of the £4.80 cost without subtracting the cost. The profit is £2.40, which is 50% of £4.80.
- 20%
  - _why:_ This mistakes the 20p profit per bottle for a percentage. Each bottle costs 40p, so the percentage profit is 20p divided by 40p, giving 50%.
