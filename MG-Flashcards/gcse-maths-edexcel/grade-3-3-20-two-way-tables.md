# Two Way Tables Flashcards

## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] goes across a table; a [ ___ ] goes down.

**Back:** A row goes across a table; a column goes down.

**Choices:**

- A row goes across a table; a column goes down. ✔
  - _why:_ Rows run horizontally across a table, while columns run vertically down it. This orientation helps locate values using a row category and a column category.
- A column goes across a table; a row goes down.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two directions. A row goes horizontally across a table, while a column goes vertically down the table.
- A row goes across a table; a diagonal goes down.
  - _why:_ A diagonal travels at an angle rather than straight down. A row goes across a table, and a column goes down it.
- A column goes across a table; a diagonal goes down.
  - _why:_ This confuses both table directions. A row goes horizontally across a table, and a column goes vertically down the table.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] adds across a row; a [ ___ ] adds down a column.

**Back:** A row total adds across a row; a column total adds down a column.

**Choices:**

- A row total adds across a row; a column total adds down a column. ✔
  - _why:_ A row total is the sum of the entries across one row. A column total is the sum of the entries down one column.
- A column total adds across a row; a row total adds down a column.
  - _why:_ This swaps the names of the totals. A row total adds entries across a row, while a column total adds entries down a column.
- A grand total adds across a row; a column total adds down a column.
  - _why:_ A grand total combines all entries in the table, not just one row. The correct terms are row total across and column total down.
- A row total adds across a row; a grand total adds down a column.
  - _why:_ A grand total represents the entire table rather than one column. A row total adds across a row, and a column total adds down a column.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** In a two-way table, what does a blank box mean?

**Back:** The number is missing, not zero.

**Choices:**

- The number is missing, not zero. ✔
  - _why:_ A blank box represents a value that has not yet been provided or calculated. It must not automatically be treated as zero.
- The number is zero, not missing.
  - _why:_ Treating a blank as zero is a common mistake that changes the totals. The number is missing and may need to be calculated from other entries.
- The box is not applicable, not missing.
  - _why:_ A blank in this context does not mean the category combination is impossible. It represents a missing number that can often be found using totals.
- The number is irrelevant and can be ignored.
  - _why:_ Ignoring a blank can make the row, column and grand totals inconsistent. The blank represents a missing number, not an irrelevant entry.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** In the drink table, what is at the Girls row and Water column?

**Back:** 18 girls chose water.

**Choices:**

- 18 girls chose water. ✔
  - _why:_ The entry where the Girls row intersects the Water column is 18, so 18 girls chose water.
- 18 boys chose water.
  - _why:_ The value 18 has been read with the wrong row label. At the Girls row and Water column, it represents 18 girls choosing water.
- 12 girls chose water.
  - _why:_ This selects a different entry from the table rather than the Girls-Water intersection. The correct value at that intersection is 18.
- 25 girls chose water.
  - _why:_ This confuses the required cell with another value or total in the table. The Girls row and Water column intersect at 18.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you work backwards from totals to fill a missing table value?

**Back:** Subtract the known entries from the total.

**Choices:**

- Subtract the known entries from the total. ✔
  - _why:_ A total is the sum of all entries, so removing the known entries by subtraction leaves the missing table value.
- Add the known entries to the total.
  - _why:_ Adding the known entries again makes the result too large. Subtract the known entries from the total to isolate the missing value.
- Divide the known entries by the total.
  - _why:_ Division does not reverse the addition used to create a row or column total. Subtract the known entries from the total instead.
- Multiply the known entries by the total.
  - _why:_ Multiplication does not isolate a missing part of a total and produces an unnecessarily large value. Subtract the known entries from the total.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Travel table: grand total 100, boys total 54. What is the girls’ total?

**Back:** 46

**Choices:**

- 46 ✔
  - _why:_ The boys’ and girls’ totals must add to 100, so the girls’ total is $100-54=46$.
- 54
  - _why:_ This copies the boys’ total instead of finding the remaining students. Subtracting gives the girls’ total: $100-54=46$.
- 44
  - _why:_ This comes from an arithmetic slip when subtracting 54 from 100. The correct subtraction is $100-54=46$.
- 154
  - _why:_ This adds the boys’ total to the grand total instead of finding the remainder. The girls’ total is $100-54=46$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Travel table: Boys total 54, Boys Walk 18, Boys Other 11. What is Boys Bus?

**Back:** 25

**Choices:**

- 25 ✔
  - _why:_ The Boys total includes all travel methods, so subtract the 18 who walk and the 11 using other methods: $54-18-11=25$ boys travel by bus.

  - _why:_ This subtracts only the 18 boys who walk and overlooks the 11 using other methods. Both known groups must be removed: $54-18-11=25$.
- 43
  - _why:_ This subtracts only the 11 boys using other methods and forgets those who walk. Subtract both known categories from 54 to obtain $25$.
- 29

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** After completing a two-way table, how should you check it?

**Back:** Add rows across to row totals.Add columns down to column totals.

**Choices:**

- Add rows across to row totals.Add columns down to column totals. ✔
  - _why:_ Each horizontal row must sum to its stated row total, and each vertical column must sum to its stated column total. These checks reveal missing or incorrect entries.
- Add rows down to row totals.Add columns across to column totals.
  - _why:_ The directions have been reversed: rows run horizontally, while columns run vertically. Add rows across to row totals and columns down to column totals.
- Add rows across to column totals.Add columns down to row totals.
  - _why:_ This matches each sum to the wrong type of total. Horizontal rows must match row totals, while vertical columns must match column totals.
- Multiply rows across to row totals.Multiply columns down to column totals.
  - _why:_ Two-way tables contain frequencies that combine by addition, not multiplication. Add each row across and each column down, then compare the sums with their totals.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** Probability = [ ___ ] divided by [ ___ ].

**Back:** Probability = favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes.

**Choices:**

- Probability = favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes. ✔
  - _why:_ For equally likely outcomes, probability is the number of outcomes matching the event divided by the total number of possible outcomes in the sample space.
- Probability = total outcomes divided by favourable outcomes.
  - _why:_ This reverses the probability fraction and can produce a value greater than 1. Favourable outcomes form the numerator, and total outcomes form the denominator.
- Probability = favourable outcomes divided by unfavourable outcomes.
  - _why:_ The denominator must include every possible outcome, not only those where the event fails. Probability is favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes.
- Probability = unfavourable outcomes divided by total outcomes.
  - _why:_ Using unfavourable outcomes calculates the probability that the event does not happen. The required probability uses favourable outcomes over total outcomes.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** If one person is chosen from the whole table, which denominator is used?

**Back:** The grand total.

**Choices:**

- The grand total. ✔
  - _why:_ Choosing from the whole table means every person is a possible outcome. Therefore, the denominator is the grand total of all people in the table.
- The row total.
  - _why:_ A row total is used only when the choice is restricted to members of that row. Choosing from the whole table requires the grand total.
- The column total.
  - _why:_ A column total applies only when selection is restricted to that column. Since anyone in the table can be chosen, use the grand total.
- The favourable total.
  - _why:_ The favourable count belongs in the numerator, not the denominator. When one person is selected from the whole table, the grand total is the denominator.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** If “one of the boys” is chosen, which denominator is used?

**Back:** The Boys row total.

**Choices:**

- The Boys row total. ✔
  - _why:_ The phrase “one of the boys” restricts the sample space to boys only. Therefore, every boy is a possible outcome and the Boys row total is the denominator.
- The grand total.
  - _why:_ The grand total would be used if anyone in the table could be selected. Here the choice is restricted to boys, so use the Boys row total.
- The Girls row total.
  - _why:_ The Girls row is excluded by the condition that the chosen person is a boy. The denominator must therefore be the Boys row total.
- The relevant column total.
  - _why:_ No travel-method column defines the sample space here; the condition identifies a gender row. Use the Boys row total as the denominator.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Travel table: 30 students walk out of 100. What is the probability a student walks?

**Back:** $$\frac{3}{10}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{3}{10}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The probability is $\frac{30}{100}$. Dividing both numerator and denominator by 10 gives the simplified probability $\frac{3}{10}$.
- $$\frac{3}{100}$
  - _why:_ This divides only the numerator of $\frac{30}{100}$ by 10, changing the fraction’s value. Divide both parts by 10 to get $\frac{3}{10}$.
- $$\frac{7}{10}$
  - _why:_ This is the complementary probability that a student does not walk, since $100-30=70$. The probability of walking is $\frac{30}{100}=\frac{3}{10}$.
- $$\frac{10}{3}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required fraction, placing the total above the favourable count. Probability is favourable outcomes over total outcomes: $\frac{30}{100}=\frac{3}{10}$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** When making a table from words, put in known [ ___ ] first, then fill the [ ___ ].

**Back:** When making a table from words, put in known totals first, then fill the inside cells.

**Choices:**

- When making a table from words, put in known totals first, then fill the inside cells. ✔
  - _why:_ Entering the given totals first establishes the row and column constraints. The unknown inside cells can then be found by adding or subtracting within each row and column.
- When making a table from words, put in known inside cells first, then fill the totals.
  - _why:_ This reverses the intended method and makes missing entries harder to organise. Record the known totals first, then use them to calculate the inside cells.
- When making a table from words, put in known totals first, then fill the headings.
  - _why:_ Headings identify the categories and should already be present before numerical data are entered. After recording known totals, calculate and fill the inside cells.
- When making a table from words, put in known probabilities first, then fill the inside cells.
  - _why:_ A two-way frequency table is constructed from given counts and totals, not probabilities. Put in the known totals first, then calculate the missing inside cells.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Back:** 10

**Choices:**

- 10 ✔
  - _why:_ The girls’ row must total 84. Subtracting the 24 who walk and the 50 who take the bus gives $84-24-50=10$ who cycle.

  - _why:_ This comes from subtracting only the 50 bus users from 84 and forgetting the 24 walkers. Subtract both known groups: $84-50-24=10$.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** A percentage means [ ___ ]; with a known total, change percentages into [ ___ ] first.

**Back:** A percentage means out of 100; with a known total, change percentages into numbers first.

**Choices:**

- A percentage means out of 100; with a known total, change percentages into numbers first. ✔
  - _why:_ Percent means “out of 100”. When the overall total is known, each percentage should be converted into the corresponding number of people or items before completing the table.
- A percentage means out of 10; with a known total, change percentages into numbers first.
  - _why:_ This confuses percentages with tenths. A percentage is out of 100, and multiplying each percentage by the known total converts it into a number for the table.
- A percentage means out of 100; with a known total, change percentages into degrees first.
  - _why:_ Degrees are used when constructing pie charts, not when filling a two-way table with a known total. Here, percentages must be converted into numbers of people or items.
- A percentage means out of 360; with a known total, change percentages into angles first.
  - _why:_ This applies the 360-degree total of a circle to percentages. Percentages are out of 100, and a two-way table requires numbers rather than pie-chart angles.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What does “18% are boys who get the bus” usually mean?

**Back:** 18% of everyone, unless it says “of the boys”.

**Choices:**

- 18% of everyone, unless it says “of the boys”. ✔
  - _why:_ Without a restricted reference group, the percentage uses the whole population as its denominator. “Of the boys” would instead make only the boys the relevant total.
- 18% of the boys, unless it says “of everyone”.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly assumes that “boys” supplies the denominator. It identifies part of the group being counted; the denominator is everyone unless the question explicitly says “of the boys”.
- 18% of the bus users, unless it says “of everyone”.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats bus users as the reference group. The statement describes the joint group who are boys and use the bus, equal to 18% of everyone.
- 18 boys, unless a total is given.
  - _why:_ This drops the percentage sign and treats 18% as a frequency of 18. It means 18% of the whole population unless a smaller reference group is specified.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is [ ___ ] because percentages are out of [ ___ ].

**Back:** If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is 100 because percentages are out of 100.

**Choices:**

- If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is 100 because percentages are out of 100. ✔
  - _why:_ Using a total of 100 makes each percentage numerically equal to its frequency. For example, 35% can be represented by 35 people out of a hypothetical 100.
- If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is 1 because percentages are out of 100.
  - _why:_ Although proportions can be written as decimals between 0 and 1, percentages are defined out of 100. A hypothetical total of 100 makes the table entries straightforward whole-number percentages.
- If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is 100 because percentages are out of 1.
  - _why:_ This confuses percentages with decimal proportions. Percentages are out of 100, which is precisely why choosing a hypothetical total of 100 allows the percentages to act as frequencies.
- If no total is given in a percentage table, pretend the total is 360 because percentages are out of 360.
  - _why:_ This mistakes percentages for angles in a full circle. A circle contains 360 degrees, but percentages are out of 100, so the convenient hypothetical total is 100.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Conference table: 14% staff, 46% Centre A, 50% students from Centre B. What is $P(\text{staff from Centre A})$?

**Back:** $$\frac{1}{10}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{10}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using 100 attendees gives 14 staff and 86 students. Centre B has 54 people, including 50 students, so 4 staff are from B and 10 staff are from A.
- $$\frac{7}{50}$
  - _why:_ This is 14%, the probability that an attendee is staff from either centre. Removing the 4% who are staff from Centre B leaves 10%, or $\frac{1}{10}$.
- $$\frac{23}{50}$
  - _why:_ This is 46%, the probability that an attendee is from Centre A regardless of role. Only 10% are both staff and from Centre A, giving $\frac{1}{10}$.
- $$\frac{1}{25}$
  - _why:_ This is 4%, the probability of selecting a staff member from Centre B. The remaining 10% of the 14% staff are from Centre A, so the answer is $\frac{1}{10}$.
