## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a solution to a pair of simultaneous equations represent graphically?

**Back:** The intersection point(s) where the graphs meet.

**Choices:**

- The intersection point(s) where the graphs meet. ✔
  - _why:_ A solution satisfies both equations, so its coordinates lie on both graphs. Graphically, these coordinates are precisely the point or points where the graphs intersect.
- The turning point(s) where the graphs change direction.
  - _why:_ A turning point shows where an individual graph changes direction, not where two equations are simultaneously satisfied. Solutions are the intersection point or points where the graphs meet.
- The axis intercept(s) where the graphs cross an axis.
  - _why:_ An axis intercept only shows where one coordinate equals zero. A simultaneous solution must lie on both graphs, so it occurs where the two graphs intersect.
- The gradient point(s) where the graphs have equal slopes.
  - _why:_ Equal gradients do not mean that a point satisfies both equations, as the graphs could remain separate. Solutions are the intersection point or points where the graphs meet.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In the context of simultaneous equations, what is an ordered pair?

**Back:** A solution written as $(x, y)$, where the $x$-value is listed first.

**Choices:**

- A solution written as $(x, y)$, where the $x$-value is listed first. ✔
  - _why:_ An ordered pair records the two coordinate values in a fixed order. By convention, the $x$-coordinate comes first and the $y$-coordinate comes second.
- A solution written as $(y, x)$, where the $y$-value is listed first.
  - _why:_ This reverses the conventional coordinate order, a common mistake when reading points from a graph. An ordered pair is written $(x, y)$, with the $x$-value first.
- A solution written as $x = y$, where the two values are set equal.
  - _why:_ An equals sign states that two values are identical; it does not record a coordinate pair. A simultaneous solution is written $(x, y)$, with the $x$-value first.
- A solution written as $\{x, y\}$, where the values can appear in either order.
  - _why:_ Braces describe a set, whose elements have no fixed order. Coordinates require an ordered pair $(x, y)$ because exchanging the $x$- and $y$-values usually gives a different point.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** A linear equation contains variables only to the [ ___ ] power, while a quadratic equation contains a squared term.

**Back:** A linear equation contains variables only to the first power, while a quadratic equation contains a squared term.

**Choices:**

- first ✔
  - _why:_ In a linear equation, each variable has a highest power of one. A variable raised to the second power would instead make the equation quadratic.
- second
  - _why:_ Confusing linear and quadratic powers makes this tempting, but a second-power variable creates a quadratic term. Linear equations contain variables only to the first power.
- third
  - _why:_ A variable raised to the third power produces a cubic equation, not a linear one. The highest power of any variable in a linear equation is first.
- fourth
  - _why:_ A fourth-power term creates a quartic equation rather than a linear equation. A linear equation contains variables only to the first power.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** A linear equation contains variables only to the first power, while a quadratic equation contains a [ ___ ] term.

**Back:** A linear equation contains variables only to the first power, while a quadratic equation contains a squared term.

**Choices:**

- squared ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic equation has degree two, meaning its highest variable power is two. It therefore contains a squared term such as $x^2$.
- cubed
  - _why:_ A cubed term such as $x^3$ gives an equation degree three, making it cubic. A quadratic equation instead contains a squared term.
- linear
  - _why:_ A linear term has a variable to the first power and does not by itself make an equation quadratic. A quadratic equation must contain a squared term.
- square-root
  - _why:_ A square-root term involves a fractional power and does not define a quadratic equation. A quadratic equation contains a squared term with power two.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the primary algebraic method for solving a linear and quadratic pair?

**Back:** Substitution.

**Choices:**

- Substitution. ✔
  - _why:_ Substitution replaces one variable using the linear equation, producing a quadratic equation in one variable. Solving it gives values that can then be substituted back.
- Elimination.
  - _why:_ Standard elimination works most directly when matching linear terms can be cancelled. For a linear and quadratic pair, substitution is the primary method because it creates one quadratic equation.
- Factorisation.
  - _why:_ Factorisation may solve the quadratic obtained later, but it does not initially combine the two simultaneous equations. The primary method for the pair is substitution.
- Completing the square.
  - _why:_ Completing the square can solve or rearrange a quadratic after the equations have been combined. Substitution is the primary method used to combine a linear and quadratic pair.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** The variable isolated on one side of an equation is known as the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The variable isolated on one side of an equation is known as the subject.

**Choices:**

- subject ✔
  - _why:_ When a variable is isolated on one side of an equation, that variable is called the subject. For example, $y$ is the subject in $y = 2x + 3$.
- coefficient
  - _why:_ A coefficient is a number multiplying a variable, such as $2$ in $2x$. The isolated variable itself is called the subject of the equation.
- constant
  - _why:_ A constant is a term with no variable, such as $3$ in $y = 2x + 3$. The isolated variable is known as the subject.
- factor
  - _why:_ A factor is a quantity multiplied by another quantity, not the name for an isolated variable. A variable alone on one side is the subject.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is substitution used to solve simultaneous equations?

**Back:** It creates one quadratic equation in one variable.

**Choices:**

- It creates one quadratic equation in one variable. ✔
  - _why:_ Replacing one variable with an equivalent expression removes that variable from the other equation. The result is one quadratic equation involving only one unknown.
- It creates two linear equations in two variables.
  - _why:_ Substitution is used to reduce the number of variables, not preserve both of them, and the quadratic term remains. It creates one quadratic equation in one variable.
- It creates one linear equation in one variable.
  - _why:_ Substitution does reduce the problem to one variable, but the squared term means the resulting equation is quadratic, not linear. It creates one quadratic equation in one variable.
- It creates one quadratic equation in two variables.
  - _why:_ This keeps an unnecessary second variable, missing the purpose of substitution. Replacing one variable completely produces one quadratic equation in one variable.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard form of a quadratic equation in one variable?

**Back:** $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $a \neq 0$.

**Choices:**

- $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $a \neq 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ Standard form places every term on one side and zero on the other. Requiring $a \neq 0$ ensures that the $x^2$ term is present, making the equation quadratic.
- $$ax^3 + bx + c = 0$, where $a \neq 0$.
  - _why:_ Using $x^3$ makes the highest power three, so this is a cubic equation. A quadratic has highest power two: $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
- $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $a = 0$.
  - _why:_ Setting $a=0$ removes the $x^2$ term, leaving a linear equation at most. For a quadratic in standard form, $a$ must not equal zero.
- $$ax^2 + bx + c = 1$, where $a \neq 0$.
  - _why:_ This has a quadratic term but is not written in standard form because the right-hand side is not zero. Standard form is $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** When substituting an expression like $y = 5 - 3x$ into a quadratic, you must use [ ___ ] to ensure the whole expression is squared.

**Back:** When substituting an expression like $y = 5 - 3x$ into a quadratic, you must use brackets to ensure the whole expression is squared.

**Choices:**

- brackets ✔
  - _why:_ Brackets group $5 - 3x$ as a single expression, so writing $(5 - 3x)^2$ squares every term in the substituted expression correctly.
- indices
  - _why:_ Indices describe powers but do not group terms together. The correct answer is brackets, which ensure that the entire expression $(5 - 3x)$ is squared.
- coefficients
  - _why:_ Coefficients are numbers multiplying variables, so they cannot show the scope of a square. Brackets are required to group the whole substituted expression before squaring.
- square roots
  - _why:_ Square roots reverse squaring rather than group an expression. Brackets are needed so the power applies to the whole expression instead of only its final term.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** State the quadratic formula for $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

**Back:** $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

**Choices:**

- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ ✔
  - _why:_ This formula gives both roots of any quadratic in the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, with the discriminant appearing beneath the square root.
- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{a}$
  - _why:_ The denominator has incorrectly omitted the factor of $2$. The quadratic formula divides the complete numerator by $2a$, not merely by $a$.
- $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 + 4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ The sign inside the square root has been changed to addition. The discriminant is $b^2 - 4ac$, so subtraction must be used in the quadratic formula.
- $$x = \frac{b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ The leading term in the numerator must be $-b$, not $b$. Losing this negative sign changes both calculated roots unless $b$ happens to be zero.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is called the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is called the discriminant.

**Choices:**

- discriminant ✔
  - _why:_ The discriminant is the expression beneath the square root in the quadratic formula. Its sign determines whether a quadratic has two, one or no real roots.
- coefficient
  - _why:_ A coefficient is a number multiplying a variable, such as $a$ or $b$ in a quadratic. The full expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is called the discriminant.
- determinant
  - _why:_ A determinant is associated with matrices and can be confused with this similarly named quantity. For a quadratic, $b^2 - 4ac$ is the discriminant.
- factorisation
  - _why:_ Factorisation rewrites an expression as a product of factors; it is not the name of this quantity. The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is the discriminant.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How does the discriminant ($b^2 - 4ac$) relate to the number of solution pairs?

**Back:** Positive: Two real solutions.Zero: One repeated solution.Negative: No real solutions.

**Choices:**

- Positive: Two real solutions.Zero: One repeated solution.Negative: No real solutions. ✔
  - _why:_ The discriminant controls how many real roots the resulting quadratic has. These roots correspond to the solution pairs, or intersection points, of the simultaneous equations.
- Positive: No real solutions.Zero: One repeated solution.Negative: Two real solutions.
  - _why:_ This reverses the positive and negative cases. A positive discriminant gives two real solutions, zero gives one repeated solution, and a negative discriminant gives none.
- Positive: One repeated solution.Zero: Two real solutions.Negative: No real solutions.
  - _why:_ This confuses the positive and zero cases. A positive discriminant produces two distinct real solutions, while zero produces one repeated solution and negative produces none.
- Positive: Two real solutions.Zero: No real solutions.Negative: One repeated solution.
  - _why:_ This swaps the zero and negative cases. Zero makes the two roots equal, giving one repeated solution, whereas a negative discriminant gives no real solutions.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a negative discriminant indicate about the relationship between a line and a curve?

**Back:** The line misses the curve (they do not intersect).

**Choices:**

- The line misses the curve (they do not intersect). ✔
  - _why:_ A negative discriminant means the substituted quadratic has no real roots. Therefore, there are no real coordinate pairs where the line and curve intersect.
- The line crosses the curve (they intersect at two points).
  - _why:_ Two intersection points correspond to two distinct real roots and therefore a positive discriminant. A negative discriminant means the line misses the curve completely.
- The line touches the curve (they intersect at one point).
  - _why:_ A single touching point gives one repeated root, which occurs when the discriminant is zero. A negative discriminant means there are no real intersections.
- The line overlaps the curve (they share every point).
  - _why:_ A line and a genuinely quadratic curve cannot share every point. A negative discriminant specifically shows that the line misses the curve, giving no real intersections.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** How should solution pairs be presented to avoid marks for "unpaired answers"?

**Back:** As distinct coordinate pairs, e.g., $(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$.

**Choices:**

- As distinct coordinate pairs, e.g., $(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$. ✔
  - _why:_ Each $x$-value must be matched with its corresponding $y$-value. Writing distinct ordered coordinate pairs makes both complete simultaneous solutions clear to the examiner.
- As separate x-values and y-values, e.g., $x = 3, -4$ and $y = 4, -3$.
  - _why:_ Separate lists do not show which $x$-value belongs with which $y$-value. The answers must be written as distinct coordinate pairs such as $(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$.
- As a single list of values, e.g., $3, 4, -4, -3$.
  - _why:_ A single list hides both the coordinate order and the correct pairings. Each solution should be shown as an ordered coordinate pair, such as $(3, 4)$.
- As two sets of coordinates, e.g., $\{3, -4\}$ and $\{4, -3\}$.
  - _why:_ Sets are unordered and separate the $x$-values from their corresponding $y$-values. Distinct ordered coordinate pairs are needed to show each complete solution correctly.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** When a question requires answers to 3 significant figures, when should you round?

**Back:** Only at the final step; keep full calculator values for intermediate steps.

**Choices:**

- Only at the final step; keep full calculator values for intermediate steps. ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping full calculator values prevents rounding errors from accumulating during substitution or later calculations. Round each completed final answer to 3 significant figures only at the end.
- At every step; use 3 significant figures throughout the calculation.
  - _why:_ Rounding repeatedly can accumulate errors and produce an inaccurate final answer. Keep full calculator values during working and round to 3 significant figures only at the final step.
- After the first step; use the rounded value for all later calculations.
  - _why:_ Rounding after the first step introduces avoidable error into every later calculation. Intermediate values should remain unrounded, with 3 significant figures applied only to the final answer.
- Before the final step; substitute values already rounded to 3 significant figures.
  - _why:_ Substituting prematurely rounded values can shift the final result outside the accepted range. Use full calculator values in the final calculation, then round the completed answer.

**Back:** Solving algebraically means using equation steps like rearranging and factorising rather than reading from a graph.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Solving algebraically uses symbolic operations such as substitution, expansion, rearrangement, factorisation or the quadratic formula, rather than estimating intersection points from a graph.
- graphically
  - _why:_ Solving graphically means drawing or viewing graphs and reading their intersection points. Using rearrangement and factorisation is described as solving algebraically.
- numerically
  - _why:_ Numerical methods use repeated approximations or calculations to estimate solutions. Exact equation steps such as rearranging and factorising mean the equations are being solved algebraically.

  - _why:_ Geometrical methods rely on shapes, measurements or spatial properties. Manipulating equations through rearrangement and factorisation is instead classified as solving algebraically.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step when the linear equation does not have an isolated variable?

**Back:** Rearrange the linear equation to make $x$ or $y$ the subject.

**Choices:**

- Rearrange the linear equation to make $x$ or $y$ the subject. ✔
  - _why:_ Substitution requires one variable to be written explicitly in terms of the other. Rearranging the linear equation provides the expression that can be inserted into the quadratic equation.
- Set the linear equation equal to zero and solve it on its own.
  - _why:_ A linear equation containing two variables cannot normally determine both values on its own. Instead, rearrange it to make $x$ or $y$ the subject before substituting.
- Factorise the linear equation to obtain separate values for $x$ and $y$.
  - _why:_ Factorisation is generally used to find roots of quadratic expressions, not to separate two variables in a linear equation. Rearrange the linear equation to isolate $x$ or $y$.
- Square the linear equation to turn it into a second quadratic equation.
  - _why:_ Squaring unnecessarily complicates the system and can introduce extra solutions. The correct first step is to rearrange the linear equation so that one variable is the subject.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Which variable should you choose to make the subject of the linear equation?

**Back:** The one that is easiest to substitute (e.g., has a coefficient of 1).

**Choices:**

- The one that is easiest to substitute (e.g., has a coefficient of 1). ✔
  - _why:_ Choosing a variable with coefficient 1 usually avoids fractions, making substitution and later simplification less error-prone while still producing the same valid solutions.
- The one with the largest coefficient in the linear equation.
  - _why:_ A large coefficient often creates awkward fractions when the equation is rearranged. The best choice is the variable that is easiest to isolate, usually one with coefficient 1.
- The one that is squared in the quadratic, regardless of its coefficient.
  - _why:_ Being squared in the quadratic does not determine which variable is easiest to isolate. Choose the variable that makes rearrangement and substitution simplest, often one with coefficient 1.
- The one with the most terms attached in the linear equation.
  - _why:_ A variable involved in more terms is usually harder, not easier, to isolate. Choose whichever variable can be made the subject most simply, ideally without creating fractions.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the matching $y$-value for a calculated $x$-root, substitute the $x$-value back into the [ ___ ] equation.

**Back:** To find the matching $y$-value for a calculated $x$-root, substitute the $x$-value back into the linear equation.

**Choices:**

- linear ✔
  - _why:_ The linear equation gives a direct relationship between $x$ and $y$, so substituting each calculated $x$-root into it produces the corresponding $y$-value.
- quadratic
  - _why:_ Substituting into the quadratic may not identify the unique corresponding $y$-value and can repeat the harder calculation. Use the linear equation because it directly links the two variables.
- cubic
  - _why:_ The simultaneous system contains a linear and a quadratic equation, not a cubic equation. Substitute each calculated $x$-root into the linear equation to find its matching $y$-value.
- exponential
  - _why:_ An exponential equation is not part of this linear-quadratic system. The matching coordinate is found by substituting the known $x$-root back into the linear equation.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the result of expanding $(y - 1)^2$?

**Back:** $$y^2 - 2y + 1$

**Choices:**

- $$y^2 - 2y + 1$ ✔
  - _why:_ Using $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$ with $a=y$ and $b=1$ gives $y^2-2y+1$, including both cross-products in the middle term.
- $$y^2 - 2y - 1$
  - _why:_ The constant comes from $(-1)^2$, which is positive rather than negative. Correctly multiplying $(y-1)(y-1)$ gives $y^2-2y+1$.
- $$y^2 - y + 1$
  - _why:_ The middle term must include two cross-products, $-y$ and $-y$, rather than only one. Their sum is $-2y$, giving $y^2-2y+1$.
- $$y^2 + 2y + 1$
  - _why:_ The negative signs in both brackets make each cross-product negative, not positive. The middle terms total $-2y$, so the expansion is $y^2-2y+1$.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** The graph of a linear equation is a [ ___ ], whereas the graph of a quadratic is curved.

**Back:** The graph of a linear equation is a straight line, whereas the graph of a quadratic is curved.

**Choices:**

- straight line ✔
  - _why:_ A first-degree equation in two variables has a constant gradient, so all coordinate pairs satisfying it lie on a single straight line when plotted.
- parabolic curve
  - _why:_ A parabola is associated with a quadratic equation because one variable is squared. A linear equation has constant gradient, so its graph is a straight line.
- circular arc
  - _why:_ Circular graphs arise from equations involving squared coordinates in a circle relationship, not from linear equations. A linear equation instead produces a straight line.
- exponential curve
  - _why:_ An exponential graph changes gradient as the variable appears in an exponent. A linear equation has constant gradient, so its graph is a straight line.
