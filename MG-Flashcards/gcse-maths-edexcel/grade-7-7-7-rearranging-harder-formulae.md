## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the subject of a formula?

**Back:** The single letter isolated on one side of the equals sign.

**Choices:**

- The single letter isolated on one side of the equals sign. ✔
  - _why:_ The subject is the variable that has been made the focus of the formula, appearing alone on one side of the equals sign.
- The letter repeated most often on both sides of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ A letter appearing several times is not necessarily the subject. The subject is the single letter isolated on one side of the equals sign.
- The first letter written on the left side of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ The first letter written is not automatically the subject. The subject is identified by being isolated on one side of the equals sign.
- The number multiplying a letter on one side of the equals sign.
  - _why:_ A numerical factor multiplying a letter is its coefficient, not the subject. The subject is the single letter isolated on one side of the equals sign.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the balance rule for rearranging equations?

**Back:** Whatever is done to one side of the equation must be done to the other side.

**Choices:**

- Whatever is done to one side of the equation must be done to the other side. ✔
  - _why:_ Applying the same operation to both sides preserves equality, so the two expressions remain balanced while the required variable is isolated.
- Whatever is done to one side of the equation must be reversed on the other side.
  - _why:_ Using the inverse operation on the other side generally destroys equality. The same operation must be applied to both sides of the equation.
- Whatever is done to one side of the equation must not affect the other side.
  - _why:_ Changing only one side makes the two sides unequal. Whatever is done to one side must also be done to the other side.
- Whatever is added to one side of the equation must be multiplied on the other side.
  - _why:_ Swapping operations is not the balance rule and will not generally preserve equality. The same operation must be performed on both sides.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** In algebraic rearranging, [ ___ ] is undone by subtracting and squaring is undone by square rooting.

**Back:** In algebraic rearranging, adding is undone by subtracting and squaring is undone by square rooting.

**Choices:**

- adding ✔
  - _why:_ Subtraction is the inverse operation of addition, so subtracting the same quantity reverses an addition while preserving the equation’s balance.
- multiplying
  - _why:_ Division reverses multiplication, not subtraction. The operation undone by subtracting is adding because addition and subtraction are inverse operations.
- squaring
  - _why:_ Square rooting reverses squaring, whereas subtraction does not. The operation undone by subtracting is adding, its inverse operation.
- subtracting
  - _why:_ Adding is used to undo subtraction, not the other way around. Subtracting undoes adding because these operations form an inverse pair.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** In algebraic rearranging, adding is undone by subtracting and [ ___ ] is undone by square rooting.

**Back:** In algebraic rearranging, adding is undone by subtracting and squaring is undone by square rooting.

**Choices:**

- squaring ✔
  - _why:_ Taking a square root reverses the operation of squaring, subject to considering both positive and negative roots in pure algebra.
- multiplying
  - _why:_ Multiplication is reversed by division, not by taking a square root. Square rooting specifically undoes squaring.
- cubing
  - _why:_ Cubing is reversed by taking a cube root rather than a square root. The operation undone by square rooting is squaring.
- square rooting
  - _why:_ Square rooting is reversed by squaring, so the stated direction is backwards. The operation that square rooting undoes is squaring.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** If the subject is affected by multiple operations, in what order should they be undone?

**Back:** From the outside in.

**Choices:**

- From the outside in. ✔
  - _why:_ The outermost operation was applied last, so it must be reversed first. Continuing towards the subject undoes the operations in reverse order.
- From the inside out.
  - _why:_ Starting with the operation closest to the subject reverses the construction order incorrectly. Operations should be undone from the outside in.
- From the products to sums.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is not always the first operation to reverse; the structure of the formula determines the order. Work from the outside in.
- From the left to right.
  - _why:_ The written left-to-right order does not reliably show which operation was applied last. Reverse operations structurally, working from the outside in.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** When making $p$ the subject of $q^2 = p^2 + 5rs$, you must first isolate $p^2$ to get $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** When making $p$ the subject of $q^2 = p^2 + 5rs$, you must first isolate $p^2$ to get $\boxed{q^2 - 5rs = p^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$q^2 - 5rs = p^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $5rs$ from both sides removes the added term beside $p^2$, leaving $p^2$ isolated while maintaining equality.
- $$q^2 + 5rs = p^2$
  - _why:_ Adding another $5rs$ does not cancel the existing positive term. Subtracting $5rs$ from both sides gives $q^2 - 5rs = p^2$.
- $$q^2 - rs = p^2$
  - _why:_ This forgets the coefficient $5$ when moving the whole term $5rs$. Subtracting the complete term gives $q^2 - 5rs = p^2$.
- $$5rs - q^2 = p^2$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction changes the sign of the isolated expression. Subtracting $5rs$ from $q^2$ correctly gives $q^2 - 5rs = p^2$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** When is it necessary to use $\pm$ when square rooting a formula to find the subject?

**Back:** In pure algebra (where the value could be negative).

**Choices:**

- In pure algebra (where the value could be negative). ✔
  - _why:_ In pure algebra, both positive and negative values may square to the same expression, so square rooting requires the two possibilities indicated by $b1$.
- In physical contexts (where the value must be positive).
  - _why:_ A physical context often restricts quantities such as length to non-negative values, so only the positive root may be valid. Pure algebra generally requires $b1$.
- In fractional formulae (where the value could be non-integer).
  - _why:_ The need for $b1$ comes from a squared variable having positive and negative roots, not from the presence of fractions. It is required in pure algebra.
- In linear algebra (where the value has power one).
  - _why:_ A linear subject has not been squared, so square rooting and $b1$ do not arise. In pure algebra, $b1$ is needed when reversing a square.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Identify the coefficient of $k$ in the term $\frac{1}{2}kt^2$.

**Back:** $$\frac{1}{2}t^2$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2}t^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficient of $k$ is everything multiplying it. In $rac{1}{2}kt^2$, removing $k$ leaves the factor $rac{1}{2}t^2$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}$
  - _why:_ This includes only the numerical factor and overlooks the factor $t^2$. Everything multiplying $k$ forms its coefficient, namely $\frac{1}{2}t^2$.
- $$t^2$
  - _why:_ This omits the numerical factor $\frac{1}{2}$. The complete factor multiplying $k$, and therefore its coefficient, is $\frac{1}{2}t^2$.
- $$2t^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly replaces the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ with its reciprocal. The expression multiplying $k$ is $\frac{1}{2}t^2$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step to rearrange $D = nt + \frac{1}{2}kt^2$ to make $k$ the subject?

**Back:** Subtract $nt$ from both sides to isolate the term with $k$.

**Choices:**

- Subtract $nt$ from both sides to isolate the term with $k$. ✔
  - _why:_ The $nt$ term does not contain $k$, so subtracting it from both sides leaves the term involving $k$ ready for further rearrangement.
- Divide both sides by $t^2$ to isolate the term with $k$.
  - _why:_ Dividing immediately by $t^2$ also affects the $nt$ term and does not isolate $k$. First subtract $nt$ from both sides to separate the term containing $k$.
- Subtract $\frac{1}{2}kt^2$ from both sides to isolate the term with $k$.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the term containing $k$ moves it away rather than beginning to isolate it. The correct first step is to subtract $nt$ from both sides.
- Divide both sides by $n$ to isolate the term with $k$.
  - _why:_ Dividing by $n$ does not isolate the $k$ term because the two terms on the right have different coefficients. First subtract $nt$ from both sides.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To factorise an expression like $4y + cy - dy$, you take out the common factor to get $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To factorise an expression like $4y + cy - dy$, you take out the common factor to get $\boxed{y(4 + c - d)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$y(4 + c - d)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Every term contains the common factor $y$. Dividing $4y$, $cy$ and $-dy$ by $y$ gives $4$, $c$ and $-d$ inside the brackets.
- $$y(4 + c + d)$
  - _why:_ The negative sign before $dy$ has been lost during factorisation. Taking out the common factor $y$ correctly gives $y(4 + c - d)$.
- $$y(4c - d)$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly combines the separate terms $4y$ and $cy$ into a product involving $4c$. Factoring out $y$ gives $y(4 + c - d)$.
- $$y(4 + c) - d$
  - _why:_ The common factor has not been taken from the final term, because $-dy$ becomes $-d$ only inside the brackets. The correct result is $y(4 + c - d)$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the necessary step if the subject appears more than once in a formula?

**Back:** Collect all subject terms on one sideFactorise to isolate the subject

**Choices:**

- Collect all subject terms on one sideFactorise to isolate the subject ✔
  - _why:_ When the subject occurs in multiple terms, those terms must first be brought together. Factoring out the subject then allows division by the remaining expression.
- Cancel the repeated subject termsDivide by the subject immediately
  - _why:_ Repeated subject terms cannot simply be cancelled, and dividing by the subject may lose valid solutions. Collect the subject terms on one side, then factorise.
- Substitute a value for the subjectSimplify only the numerical terms
  - _why:_ Substitution does not rearrange a general formula, and simplifying constants cannot isolate repeated subject terms. They must be collected on one side and then factorised.
- Move all constants to one sideExpand without collecting subject terms
  - _why:_ Moving constants may help, but expanding alone leaves the subject in several separate terms. Collect all subject terms on one side and factorise them.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What does "clearing the fraction" mean in algebraic rearranging?

**Back:** Multiplying both sides by the denominator.

**Choices:**

- Multiplying both sides by the denominator. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplication by the denominator cancels the division represented by the fraction. Applying it to both sides preserves equality while removing that denominator.
- Dividing both sides by the denominator.
  - _why:_ Dividing by the denominator introduces an additional denominator rather than removing the fraction. Clearing it requires multiplying both sides by the denominator.
- Multiplying both sides by the numerator.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by the numerator does not cancel the denominator of a fraction. The fraction is cleared by multiplying both sides by its denominator.
- Subtracting the denominator from both sides.
  - _why:_ A denominator represents division, so subtraction cannot cancel it. Multiplying both sides by the denominator uses the inverse operation and clears the fraction.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** The reciprocal of $q$ is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The reciprocal of $q$ is $\boxed{\frac{1}{q}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{q}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The reciprocal of a non-zero quantity is found by dividing $1$ by that quantity. Therefore, multiplying $q$ by its reciprocal gives $1$.
- $$q$
  - _why:_ A quantity is not generally its own reciprocal; that happens only for special values such as $1$ and $-1$. The reciprocal of $q$ is $\frac{1}{q}$.
- $$-\frac{1}{q}$
  - _why:_ Taking a reciprocal does not change the sign of a quantity. The reciprocal must multiply by $q$ to give $1$, so it is $\frac{1}{q}$.

  - _why:_ Squaring the denominator confuses a reciprocal with a negative power of greater magnitude. The reciprocal is $q^{-1}$, which equals $\frac{1}{q}$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you efficiently clear all fractions in a reciprocal formula like $\frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{q} + \frac{1}{r}$?

**Back:** Multiply every term by $pqr$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply every term by $pqr$. ✔
  - _why:_ The product $pqr$ is a common multiple of all three denominators. Multiplying every term by it cancels each reciprocal denominator and produces $qr = pr + pq$.
- Multiply every term by $pq$.
  - _why:_ The product $pq$ cancels the denominators $p$ and $q$ but leaves the term containing denominator $r$ as a fraction. Use $pqr$ to clear all three.
- Multiply every term by $q + r$.
  - _why:_ Adding denominators does not provide factors that cancel the reciprocal terms. Multiplying every term by the product $pqr$ clears the denominators $p$, $q$ and $r$.
- Multiply every term by $p + q + r$.
  - _why:_ The sum of the denominators cannot cancel any individual denominator by multiplication. Their product $pqr$ contains every required factor and clears all fractions.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Why must brackets be expanded early when rearranging for a specific letter?

**Back:** To release the subject if it is trapped inside the brackets.

**Choices:**

- To release the subject if it is trapped inside the brackets. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding distributes the outside factor across the bracket, turning the hidden subject term into a separate term that can be collected and isolated.
- To cancel the subject if it appears inside the brackets.
  - _why:_ A term inside a sum cannot be cancelled merely because brackets surround it. Expanding releases the subject into separate terms so it can be collected and isolated.
- To remove the subject from the formula completely.
  - _why:_ Rearrangement aims to isolate the subject, not eliminate it from the formula. Expanding brackets exposes its terms so the necessary inverse operations can be applied.
- To divide the subject by every term outside the brackets.
  - _why:_ Expansion requires multiplying each bracketed term by the outside factor, not dividing the subject. This releases the subject term so it can later be isolated.

**Back:** In the formula $w = r + kt$, making $r$ the subject results in $\boxed{r = w - kt}$.

**Choices:**

- $$r = w - kt$ ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $kt$ from both sides reverses the addition of $kt$, leaving $r$ alone and giving $r = w - kt$.
- $$r = w + kt$
  - _why:_ Keeping $kt$ positive fails to undo its addition on the right-hand side. Subtracting $kt$ from both sides gives the correct result, $r = w - kt$.
- $$r = kt - w$
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the subtraction, producing the negative of the required expression. Subtracting $kt$ from $w$ gives $r = w - kt$.
- $$r = w - k - t$
  - _why:_ The term $kt$ is a product, so it must be subtracted as a whole rather than split into separate terms. The correct rearrangement is $r = w - kt$.

**Choices:**

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a formula involves a square root, the inverse operation to remove it is [ ___ ] both sides.

**Back:** If a formula involves a square root, the inverse operation to remove it is squaring both sides.

**Choices:**

- squaring ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring reverses taking a square root because $(\sqrt{x})^2=x$. Both sides must be squared to preserve the equality while removing the root.
- square rooting
  - _why:_ Taking another square root does not cancel the original root and instead introduces a fourth root. Squaring both sides is the inverse operation that removes a square root.
- doubling
  - _why:_ Doubling changes a value by multiplying it by $2$, which does not undo a square root. The root is removed by squaring both sides.
- halving
  - _why:_ Halving reverses multiplication by $2$, not taking a square root. To eliminate a square root while maintaining equality, square both sides of the formula.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $x(a + b) = xa + b$ an incorrect expansion?

**Back:** The factor $x$ must multiply every term inside the bracket ($xa + xb$).

**Choices:**

- The factor $x$ must multiply every term inside the bracket ($xa + xb$). ✔
  - _why:_ The distributive law applies the outside factor to each term inside the bracket. Therefore, $x(a+b)$ expands to $xa+xb$, not $xa+b$.
- The factor $x$ must multiply only the first term inside the bracket ($xa + b$).
  - _why:_ This is the misconception shown in the proposed expansion: the second term has been missed. Distribution requires $x$ to multiply both terms, giving $xa+xb$.
- The factor $x$ must be added to every term inside the bracket ($(x + a) + (x + b)$).
  - _why:_ The factor outside the bracket indicates multiplication, not addition. Applying the distributive law correctly means multiplying both $a$ and $b$ by $x$, producing $xa+xb$.
- The factor $x$ must be squared with every term inside the bracket ($x^2a + x^2b$).
  - _why:_ Expanding a bracket does not create an extra factor of $x$. The single outside factor multiplies each term once, so the correct expansion is $xa+xb$.
