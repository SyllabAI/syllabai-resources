## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the relationship between radius $r$ and diameter $d$?

**Back:** $$r = \frac{d}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $$r = \frac{d}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The radius runs from the centre to the circumference, while the diameter crosses the whole circle through its centre. Therefore, the radius is half the diameter.
- $$r = 2d$
  - _why:_ This reverses and enlarges the relationship. The diameter is twice the radius, so rearranging $d = 2r$ correctly gives $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- $$r = \frac{d}{4}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly divides the diameter by four. A diameter consists of exactly two radii placed end to end, so the correct relationship is $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- $$r = d$
  - _why:_ This treats the radius and diameter as equal lengths. The diameter spans two radii through the centre, so the correct relationship is $r = \frac{d}{2}$.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** To avoid losing accuracy, rounding should only be performed [ ___ ] of the calculation.

**Back:** To avoid losing accuracy, rounding should only be performed at the end of the calculation.

**Choices:**

- at the end ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping full calculator values throughout prevents small rounding errors from accumulating. Rounding only the final result gives the greatest possible accuracy for the required answer.
- at the start
  - _why:_ Rounding at the start introduces an error that affects every later step. Full-precision values should be retained, with rounding performed only at the end of the calculation.
- at each stage
  - _why:_ Repeated rounding allows errors to accumulate across several steps, potentially changing the final answer. Intermediate values should remain unrounded until the calculation is complete.
- halfway through
  - _why:_ Rounding an intermediate result reduces the accuracy of all calculations that follow it. Rounding should be delayed until the end, after the full-precision result has been found.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Which measurement of a cone is used to calculate its volume?

**Back:** The vertical height ($h$).

**Choices:**

- The vertical height ($h$). ✔
  - _why:_ Cone volume uses the perpendicular distance from the base to the vertex. This vertical height appears in $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- The slant height ($l$).
  - _why:_ The slant height measures along the cone’s curved surface and is used for curved surface area. Volume instead requires the vertical height $h$.
- The base diameter ($d$).
  - _why:_ The standard cone volume formula uses the base radius $r$, not the diameter as its height measurement. The required height is the vertical height $h$.
- The base circumference ($C$).
  - _why:_ Base circumference is not the height used in the cone volume formula. Volume requires the perpendicular vertical height $h$, together with the base radius.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Which measurement of a cone is used to calculate its curved surface area?

**Back:** The slant height ($l$).

**Choices:**

- The slant height ($l$). ✔
  - _why:_ The curved surface follows the slope from the base rim to the vertex, so its area uses the slant height in the formula $\pi rl$.
- The vertical height ($h$).
  - _why:_ Vertical height is used for cone volume because it is perpendicular to the base. Curved surface area follows the slope, so it requires the slant height $l$.
- The base diameter ($d$).
  - _why:_ The base diameter can be converted into a radius, but it is not the cone’s required height measurement. Curved surface area uses the slant height $l$.
- The base circumference ($C$).
  - _why:_ The base circumference does not describe the length of the cone’s curved slope. The required measurement for curved surface area is the slant height $l$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** The volume of a cone is calculated using the formula $V = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The volume of a cone is calculated using the formula $V = \boxed{\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cone has one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same circular base and vertical height. Therefore, its volume is $\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$\pi r^2 h$
  - _why:_ This is the volume formula for a cylinder, not a cone. A cone occupies one-third of the corresponding cylinder, giving $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 h$
  - _why:_ Using one-half confuses the cone’s volume factor with formulas involving triangular cross-sections. A cone has one-third of the matching cylinder’s volume, so the factor is $\frac{1}{3}$.
- $$\frac{1}{3}\pi r h$
  - _why:_ This fails to square the radius when finding the circular base area. Since the base area is $\pi r^2$, the volume is $\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** The volume of a sphere is calculated using the formula $V = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The volume of a sphere is calculated using the formula $V = \boxed{\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ The volume of a sphere depends on the cube of its radius because volume is three-dimensional. Its exact formula is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$4\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the surface area of a sphere and uses square units, not cubic units. Sphere volume is calculated using $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This gives half the required volume and is the formula for a hemisphere. A complete sphere has volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$\frac{3}{4}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This reverses the fraction in the sphere volume formula. The correct coefficient is $\frac{4}{3}$, so the volume is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the volume of a hemisphere in terms of $r$?

**Back:** $$\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ A hemisphere is exactly half a sphere. Halving the sphere volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ gives $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This is the volume of a complete sphere. A hemisphere is half of that volume, so its volume is $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$\frac{1}{3}\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly quarters the full sphere formula rather than halving it. A hemisphere is half a sphere, giving $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
- $$2\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ This multiplies the coefficient incorrectly when halving the sphere’s volume. Half of $\frac{4}{3}$ is $\frac{2}{3}$, so the result is $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the formula $\pi rl$ represent for a cone?

**Back:** The curved surface area.

**Choices:**

- The curved surface area. ✔
  - _why:_ For a cone, multiplying $\pi$, the base radius $r$, and the slant height $l$ gives the area of the curved surface only.
- The total surface area.
  - _why:_ Total surface area must also include the circular base area $\pi r^2$. Therefore, $\pi rl$ alone represents only the curved surface area.
- The circular base area.
  - _why:_ The circular base has area $\pi r^2$ and does not involve the slant height. The formula $\pi rl$ represents the cone’s curved surface area.
- The volume of the cone.
  - _why:_ Volume uses the vertical height and has formula $\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$. The expression $\pi rl$ instead calculates the cone’s curved surface area.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the total surface area of a solid cone calculated?

**Back:** Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($\pi r^2$)

**Choices:**

- Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($\pi r^2$) ✔
  - _why:_ A solid cone has a curved surface and a circular base. Adding $\pi rl$ and $\pi r^2$ gives its total surface area.
- Curved surface area ($\pi r^2$)Base area ($\pi rl$)
  - _why:_ This swaps the two formulae. The curved surface area is $\pi rl$, while the circular base has area $\pi r^2$; these must be added.
- Curved surface area ($2\pi rl$)Base area ($\pi r^2$)
  - _why:_ The curved surface has been incorrectly doubled. A cone's curved surface area is $\pi rl$, and adding the base area $\pi r^2$ gives the total.
- Curved surface area ($\pi rl$)Base area ($2\pi r^2$)
  - _why:_ The circular base has been counted twice. There is one base with area $\pi r^2$, which is added to the curved surface area $\pi rl$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the surface area of a sphere?

**Back:** $$4\pi r^2$

**Choices:**

- $$4\pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The surface area of a sphere is four times the area of a circle with the same radius, so the formula is $4\pi r^2$.
- $$\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the area of a single circle, not the surface area of a sphere. A sphere's complete surface area is $4\pi r^2$.
- $$2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the curved surface area of a hemisphere. A complete sphere has twice that curved area, giving $4\pi r^2$.
- $$4\pi r^3$
  - _why:_ Using $r^3$ produces cubic units, which measure volume rather than area. Surface area requires square units, and the correct formula is $4\pi r^2$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The total surface area of a solid hemisphere is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The total surface area of a solid hemisphere is $\boxed{3\pi r^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$3\pi r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ A solid hemisphere includes curved area $2\pi r^2$ and a circular base of area $\pi r^2$. Their sum is $3\pi r^2$.
- $$2\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This includes only the hemisphere's curved surface and omits its flat circular base. Adding the base area $\pi r^2$ gives $3\pi r^2$.
- $$4\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ This is the surface area of a complete sphere. A solid hemisphere has half that curved area plus one circular base, giving $3\pi r^2$.
- $$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^2$
  - _why:_ The coefficient $\frac{4}{3}$ is associated with the sphere volume formula, not hemisphere surface area. Adding curved and base areas gives $3\pi r^2$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Why does a solid hemisphere have a total surface area of $3\pi r^2$?

**Back:** It combines the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) and the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$).

**Choices:**

- It combines the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) and the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ Half a sphere has curved area $2\pi r^2$, and its exposed circular base has area $\pi r^2$. Together these total $3\pi r^2$.
- It combines the curved surface ($\pi r^2$) and the flat circular base ($2\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ The component areas have been reversed. The curved hemisphere contributes $2\pi r^2$, while its single flat circular base contributes $\pi r^2$.
- It combines the curved surface ($4\pi r^2$) and the flat circular base ($\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ The curved area shown is for a complete sphere, not a hemisphere. Halving it gives $2\pi r^2$, then the base adds $\pi r^2$.
- It combines the curved surface ($2\pi r^2$) and the flat circular base ($2\pi r^2$).
  - _why:_ The flat base has been doubled. It is one circle with area $\pi r^2$, so adding it to $2\pi r^2$ gives $3\pi r^2$.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a cone, the relationship between slant height ($l$), vertical height ($h$), and radius ($r$) is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** In a cone, the relationship between slant height ($l$), vertical height ($h$), and radius ($r$) is $\boxed{l^2 = h^2 + r^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$l^2 = h^2 + r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The vertical height and radius are perpendicular sides of a right-angled triangle, while the slant height is its hypotenuse. Pythagoras therefore gives $l^2=h^2+r^2$.
- $$h^2 = l^2 + r^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats the vertical height as the hypotenuse. The slant height is opposite the right angle, so the correct relationship is $l^2=h^2+r^2$.
- $$r^2 = h^2 + l^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats the radius as the hypotenuse. The slant height is the longest side of the right-angled triangle, giving $l^2=h^2+r^2$.
- $$l = h + r$
  - _why:_ Pythagoras requires the squares of the perpendicular sides to be added, not their lengths directly. The correct relationship is $l^2=h^2+r^2$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What theorem is used to find the slant height of a cone if only the vertical height and radius are known?

**Back:** Pythagoras' theorem.

**Choices:**

- Pythagoras' theorem. ✔
  - _why:_ A cone's vertical height and radius meet at a right angle, with the slant height forming the hypotenuse. Pythagoras' theorem therefore finds the slant height.
- Cosine rule.
  - _why:_ The cosine rule is mainly needed for non-right-angled triangles or when an included angle is known. Here, the right angle makes Pythagoras' theorem the direct method.
- Sine rule.
  - _why:_ The sine rule requires known angles and opposite sides, which are not provided here. The height and radius are perpendicular, so Pythagoras' theorem applies.
- Circle theorem.
  - _why:_ Circle theorems determine angles in circles and do not relate the three side lengths here. The cone's cross-section forms a right-angled triangle, so use Pythagoras' theorem.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the total volume of a compound solid?

**Back:** Add the volumes of the individual shapes together.

**Choices:**

- Add the volumes of the individual shapes together. ✔
  - _why:_ A compound solid is formed from multiple component solids. Calculate each component's volume using its appropriate formula, then add those volumes to obtain the total.
- Subtract the volumes of the individual shapes together.
  - _why:_ Subtraction is used only when a section is removed or hollowed out. For joined component shapes, add their individual volumes to find the total volume.
- Multiply the volumes of the individual shapes together.
  - _why:_ Multiplying volumes produces incorrect units and does not measure the combined space occupied. The total volume is found by adding the component volumes.
- Average the volumes of the individual shapes together.
  - _why:_ An average gives a typical component volume, not the space occupied by the complete solid. Add all the individual volumes to find the total.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Using the calculator value produces a rounded numerical approximation. To answer in terms of $\pi$, leave $\pi$ unchanged in the exact final expression.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** When a solid is fully submerged, the rise in water level = $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** When a solid is fully submerged, the rise in water level = $\boxed{\frac{\text{volume of solid}}{\text{area of container base}}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{\text{volume of solid}}{\text{area of container base}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The displaced volume equals the solid’s volume. Since a prism-shaped container has volume equal to base area multiplied by height, the rise is volume divided by base area.
- $$\frac{\text{area of container base}}{\text{volume of solid}}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required division and does not produce a measurement of length. The rise is the volume of the solid divided by the area of the container base.
- $$\frac{\text{surface area of solid}}{\text{area of container base}}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the solid’s surface area rather than its displaced volume. The water-level rise equals the volume of the solid divided by the container’s base area.
- $$\frac{\text{volume of solid}}{\text{perimeter of container base}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by the base perimeter is a common confusion between perimeter and area. The rise is found by dividing the solid’s volume by the container’s base area.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** How does the volume of a submerged solid relate to the water it displaces?

**Back:** The volume of the solid equals the volume of the water displaced.

**Choices:**

- The volume of the solid equals the volume of the water displaced. ✔
  - _why:_ A fully submerged solid takes up space that the water previously occupied, so it displaces a volume of water exactly equal to its own volume.
- The volume of the solid is twice the volume of the water displaced.
  - _why:_ Submersion does not introduce a factor of two. A fully submerged solid displaces a volume of water exactly equal to the volume of the solid.
- The volume of the solid is half the volume of the water displaced.
  - _why:_ Halving the displaced volume confuses full submersion with partial submersion. When the solid is fully submerged, its volume equals the volume of water displaced.
- The volume of the solid exceeds the volume of the water displaced.
  - _why:_ A fully submerged object cannot occupy more volume than the water it displaces. Its entire volume replaces an equal volume of water, regardless of its density.

**Front:** What operation is used to find the radius of a sphere if the volume is known?

**Back:** A cube root.

**Choices:**

- A cube root. ✔
  - _why:_ The sphere formula contains $r^3$, so after rearranging to isolate $r^3$, taking the cube root gives the radius.
- A square root.
  - _why:_ A square root would undo $r^2$, which appears in area formulas. Sphere volume contains $r^3$, so the radius is found using a cube root.
- A division by three.
  - _why:_ Dividing by three does not undo a power of three. After rearranging the sphere’s volume formula, a cube root is needed to recover the radius.
- A multiplication by three.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by three confuses an exponent with a coefficient. Since the volume formula contains the radius cubed, the final operation is a cube root.
