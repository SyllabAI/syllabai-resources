## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines a quadratic expression?

**Back:** An expression where the highest power of the variable is 2 (e.g., $ax^2 + bx + c$).

**Choices:**

- An expression where the highest power of the variable is 2 (e.g., $ax^2 + bx + c$). ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic expression has degree 2, meaning its greatest variable exponent is 2. Its general form is $ax^2 + bx + c$, where $a \ne 0$.
- An expression where the highest power of the variable is 1 (e.g., $ax + b$).
  - _why:_ A highest power of 1 defines a linear expression, not a quadratic expression. A quadratic has highest power 2, as in $ax^2 + bx + c$.
- An expression where the highest power of the variable is 3 (e.g., $ax^3 + bx + c$).
  - _why:_ A highest power of 3 defines a cubic expression. A quadratic expression has degree 2, so its highest-powered term is of the form $ax^2$.
- An expression containing exactly two variable terms (e.g., $ax^2 + bx$).
  - _why:_ The word quadratic refers to the highest power, not the number of variable terms. The highest power must be 2, although some coefficients may be zero.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$ (where $a=1$), find two numbers that multiply to make $\text{[...]}$ and add to make $b$.

**Back:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$ (where $a=1$), find two numbers that multiply to make $\boxed{c}$ and add to make $b$.

**Choices:**

- $$c$ ✔
  - _why:_ For a monic quadratic $x^2 + bx + c$, the constants in the two brackets must multiply to $c$ and add together to give $b$.
- $$b$
  - _why:_ This swaps the product and sum conditions. The two numbers add to $b$, but they must multiply to $c$ when factorising $x^2 + bx + c$.
- $$b+c$
  - _why:_ Adding the coefficients does not produce the required product. The bracket constants must multiply to the constant term $c$ and separately add to the coefficient $b$.
- $$c^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the constant would make the expanded constant term too large. The two selected numbers must multiply directly to $c$, not to $c^2$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$ (where $a=1$), find two numbers that multiply to make $c$ and add to make $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$ (where $a=1$), find two numbers that multiply to make $c$ and add to make $\boxed{b}$.

**Choices:**

- $$b$ ✔
  - _why:_ The two bracket constants produce the middle coefficient when added. Therefore, for $x^2 + bx + c$, they must add to $b$ while multiplying to $c$.
- $$c$
  - _why:_ This confuses the sum condition with the product condition. The two numbers multiply to $c$, but their sum must equal the middle-term coefficient $b$.
- $$-b$
  - _why:_ Using the opposite sign would produce a middle term of $-bx$ when the brackets are expanded. The required sum is $b$, including its given sign.
- $$b+c$
  - _why:_ The constant term is not included in the required sum. The two numbers must add to the coefficient of $x$, which is $b$, and multiply to $c$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step in the split the middle term method for $ax^2 + bx + c$?

**Back:** Multiply the coefficient $a$ by the constant $c$.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the coefficient $a$ by the constant $c$. ✔
  - _why:_ The split numbers must have product $ac$ and sum $b$. Calculating $a \times c$ first therefore identifies the product needed when searching for those numbers.
- Add the coefficient $a$ to the constant $c$.
  - _why:_ Adding the outer coefficients does not give the target product for splitting the middle term. The first step is to multiply $a$ by $c$ to obtain $ac$.
- Multiply the coefficient $b$ by the constant $c$.
  - _why:_ This uses the middle coefficient in the product by mistake. The required split numbers multiply to $ac$, so the first step is multiplying $a$ by $c$.
- Divide the coefficient $b$ by the coefficient $a$.
  - _why:_ Dividing $b$ by $a$ is not part of the split the middle term method. First calculate $ac$, then find factors of it whose sum is $b$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the expression $ax^2 + bx + c$, the numbers used to split the middle term must multiply to $\text{[...]}$ and add to $b$.

**Back:** In the expression $ax^2 + bx + c$, the numbers used to split the middle term must multiply to $\boxed{ac}$ and add to $b$.

**Choices:**

- $$ac$ ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding two brackets shows that the split terms must combine to $bx$ while their coefficients multiply to the product of the leading and constant coefficients, $ac$.
- $$a+c$
  - _why:_ Adding the leading and constant coefficients is a common confusion, but the method requires their product. The split numbers must multiply to $ac$ and add to $b$.
- $$ab$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly multiplies the leading and middle coefficients. The required product uses the outer coefficients $a$ and $c$, so the split numbers must multiply to $ac$.
- $$bc$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the middle and constant coefficients for the product. The split numbers must multiply to $ac$, while their sum is determined by $b$.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the expression $ax^2 + bx + c$, the numbers used to split the middle term must multiply to $ac$ and add to $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** In the expression $ax^2 + bx + c$, the numbers used to split the middle term must multiply to $ac$ and add to $\boxed{b}$.

**Choices:**

- $$b$ ✔
  - _why:_ The original middle term is $bx$, so the coefficients of the two split terms must total $b$. Their product must also equal $ac$.
- $$c$
  - _why:_ The constant $c$ contributes to the product condition through $ac$, not the sum condition. The split numbers must add to the middle coefficient $b$.
- $$ac$
  - _why:_ This repeats the product condition instead of giving the required sum. The split numbers multiply to $ac$, but they must add together to make $b$.
- $$-b$
  - _why:_ Changing the sign would create a middle term with the opposite coefficient when the groups are recombined. The split numbers must add to $b$, including its original sign.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** In $ax^2 + bx + c$, what signs must the split numbers have if $c$ is negative?

**Back:** One positive and one negative.

**Choices:**

- One positive and one negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A negative product can only be formed by multiplying numbers with opposite signs. Therefore, the two split numbers must consist of one positive number and one negative number.
- Both positive.
  - _why:_ Two positive numbers have a positive product, so they cannot produce the required negative product. One split number must be positive and the other must be negative.
- Both negative.
  - _why:_ Multiplying two negative numbers gives a positive result, not a negative one. A negative product requires one positive split number and one negative split number.
- Both have the same sign.
  - _why:_ Numbers with the same sign always have a positive product. To obtain a negative product, the split numbers must have opposite signs: one positive and one negative.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** In $ax^2 + bx + c$, what signs must the split numbers have if $c$ is positive but $b$ is negative?

**Back:** Both numbers must be negative.

**Choices:**

- Both numbers must be negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A positive product requires matching signs, while a negative sum rules out two positive numbers. Therefore, both split numbers must be negative.
- Both numbers must be positive.
  - _why:_ Two positive numbers would give the required positive product, but their sum would also be positive. To produce negative $b$, both numbers must instead be negative.
- One positive and one negative.
  - _why:_ Numbers with opposite signs have a negative product, which conflicts with the positive product required here. A negative sum and positive product require two negative numbers.
- Their signs cannot be determined.
  - _why:_ The product and sum determine the signs. A positive product means matching signs, and a negative sum means those matching signs must both be negative.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** List the steps to factorise $ax^2 + bx + c$ by grouping after splitting the middle term.

**Back:** Factorise the first two termsFactorise the last two termsTake out the common bracket

**Choices:**

- Factorise the first two termsFactorise the last two termsTake out the common bracket ✔
  - _why:_ After splitting the middle term, factorising each pair produces the same binomial factor. Taking out that common bracket gives the quadratic as a product of two brackets.
- Expand the first two termsExpand the last two termsCollect the common terms
  - _why:_ Expanding the pairs reverses rather than continues the factorisation process. Factorise the first two terms and last two terms separately, then take out their common bracket.
- Factorise the outer two termsFactorise the inner two termsTake out the common factor
  - _why:_ Combining the outer and inner terms does not create two groups with a shared bracket. Group consecutive pairs, factorise each pair, then take out the common bracket.
- Take out the common bracketFactorise the first two termsFactorise the last two terms
  - _why:_ Taking a bracket out before factorising the two groups assumes the required shared binomial already exists. First factorise both pairs, then identify and remove their common bracket.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you mentally check if your factorisation is correct?

**Back:** Expand the brackets; the result should match the original expression.

**Choices:**

- Expand the brackets; the result should match the original expression. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term in one bracket by every term in the other reconstructs the quadratic. Matching the original expression confirms the factors, signs and coefficients.
- Add the brackets; the result should match the original expression.
  - _why:_ Adding the bracket contents does not reverse factorisation, so it cannot verify the product. Expand the brackets by multiplying their terms and compare the result with the original expression.
- Substitute zero; the result should match the original expression.
  - _why:_ Substituting only zero may occasionally give matching constants despite an incorrect factorisation. The reliable check is to expand the brackets and compare every resulting term with the original expression.
- Differentiate the brackets; the result should match the original expression.
  - _why:_ Differentiating changes the expression and therefore cannot directly confirm an algebraic factorisation. Expand the proposed brackets instead and check that the resulting quadratic matches the original expression.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the Zero Product Property?

**Back:** If $AB = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$.

**Choices:**

- If $AB = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ A product can equal zero only if at least one of its factors is zero. This allows each factor of a factorised equation to be set equal to zero.
- If $AB = 0$, then $A = 0$ and $B = 0$.
  - _why:_ Requiring both factors to be zero is too restrictive; only one factor needs to be zero for their product to vanish. The correct connector is “or”, not “and”.
- If $AB = 0$, then $A = -B$ or $B = -A$.
  - _why:_ Opposite factors can have a non-zero product, such as $2(-2)=-4$, so this conclusion does not follow. A zero product means $A=0$ or $B=0$.
- If $AB = 0$, then $A = B$ or $B = A$.
  - _why:_ A zero product does not imply that the factors are equal; for example, $0\times5=0$. It implies that $A=0$ or $B=0$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the next step to solve $(3x - 2)(x + 4) = 0$ after factorising?

**Back:** Set each bracket to zero: $3x - 2 = 0$ and $x + 4 = 0$.

**Choices:**

- Set each bracket to zero: $3x - 2 = 0$ and $x + 4 = 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ The Zero Product Property says that a product equal to zero must have at least one zero factor. Therefore, each linear bracket is set equal to zero.
- Set each bracket to one: $3x - 2 = 1$ and $x + 4 = 1$.
  - _why:_ Setting both brackets equal to one misuses the Zero Product Property. Since their product is zero, each bracket must instead be set equal to zero.
- Set the brackets equal: $3x - 2 = x + 4$ and $x + 4 = 3x - 2$.
  - _why:_ Equating the two brackets does not use the fact that their product is zero. Apply the Zero Product Property by setting $3x-2=0$ and $x+4=0$.
- Set each constant to zero: $-2 = 0$ and $4 = 0$.
  - _why:_ Setting only the constants to zero ignores the variable terms and does not solve either factor. Each complete bracket must be set equal to zero.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** If an exam question asks you to factorise fully, the first thing you should check for is a [ ___ ].

**Back:** If an exam question asks you to factorise fully, the first thing you should check for is a common factor.

**Choices:**

- common factor ✔
  - _why:_ A factor shared by every term must be taken outside the brackets first. Otherwise, the resulting expression may be factorised but not factorised fully.
- common multiple
  - _why:_ A common multiple is divisible by the terms rather than being a divisor of them, so it cannot generally be taken outside a bracket. Check for a common factor first.
- constant term
  - _why:_ A constant term is simply the term without a variable and need not divide every term. Full factorisation begins by checking for a common factor.
- middle term
  - _why:_ A middle term is relevant when splitting a quadratic, but it is not the first general check for full factorisation. First identify any factor common to every term.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** State the identity for the difference of two squares.

**Back:** $$A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$

**Choices:**

- $$A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The cross terms $AB$ and $-AB$ cancel when the conjugate brackets are expanded, leaving exactly $A^2-B^2$. This is the difference of two squares identity.
- $$A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A - B)$
  - _why:_ Using two subtraction brackets creates a perfect-square expression with a middle term: $(A-B)^2=A^2-2AB+B^2$. The difference of squares requires conjugate brackets.
- $$A^2 - B^2 = (A + B)(A + B)$
  - _why:_ Using two addition brackets gives $(A+B)^2=A^2+2AB+B^2$, so neither the middle term nor the sign of $B^2$ matches. One bracket must subtract $B$.
- $$A^2 - B^2 = (B - A)(A - B)$
  - _why:_ These brackets expand to $A^2-B^2$ only when the signs are opposite. Two identical mixed brackets produce a non-zero middle term instead of cancellation.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Factorise $3x^2 - 75$ fully.

**Back:** $$3(x - 5)(x + 5)$

**Choices:**

- $$3(x - 5)(x + 5)$ ✔
  - _why:_ First take out the common factor $3$ to obtain $3(x^2-25)$. Since $25=5^2$, the difference of squares factorises as $(x-5)(x+5)$.
- $$3(x - 25)(x + 25)$
  - _why:_ This treats $25$ as though its square root were $25$. After taking out $3$, use $x^2-25=x^2-5^2$, giving $3(x-5)(x+5)$.
- $$3(x - 5)(x - 5)$
  - _why:_ This confuses a difference of squares with a repeated bracket. Squaring $(x-5)$ introduces a $-10x$ term, whereas conjugate brackets give $3(x-5)(x+5)$.
- $$(3x - 5)(x + 5)$
  - _why:_ Placing the coefficient $3$ inside only one bracket introduces non-cancelling $x$ terms when expanded. Take out $3$ first, then factorise the difference of squares.

**Choices:**

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What identifies a perfect-square quadratic?

**Back:** It factorises into two identical brackets (e.g., $(x+3)^2$).

**Choices:**

- It factorises into two identical brackets (e.g., $(x+3)^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ A perfect-square quadratic is produced by squaring one linear expression, so its factorised form contains the same bracket twice, such as $(x+3)(x+3)$.
- It factorises into two brackets with opposite signs (e.g., $(x+3)(x-3)$).
  - _why:_ Brackets with opposite signs produce a difference of squares with no middle term. A perfect-square quadratic instead factorises into two identical brackets.
- It factorises into two different brackets (e.g., $(x+2)(x+3)$).
  - _why:_ Two different brackets can produce an ordinary factorisable quadratic, but not a perfect square. A perfect-square quadratic must have two identical brackets.
- It cannot be factorised into two linear brackets (e.g., $x^2+1$).
  - _why:_ Failure to factorise into linear brackets describes a non-factorisable quadratic over the relevant number system. A perfect-square quadratic factorises into two identical brackets.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expression $x^2 - 8xy + 16y^2$ is a perfect square that factorises to $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The expression $x^2 - 8xy + 16y^2$ is a perfect square that factorises to $\boxed{(x - 4y)^2}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(x - 4y)^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding gives $x^2-8xy+16y^2$: the cross-term is $2(x)(-4y)=-8xy$, and the final term is $(-4y)^2=16y^2$.
- $$(x + 4y)^2$
  - _why:_ A positive sign produces the cross-term $+8xy$, not $-8xy$. The negative middle term requires the factorisation $(x - 4y)^2$.
- $$(x - 8y)^2$
  - _why:_ Using $8y$ confuses the middle-term coefficient with the bracket coefficient and produces $-16xy+64y^2$. Halving $8$ gives the correct coefficient $4$.
- $$(x - 2y)^2$
  - _why:_ Using $2y$ produces $x^2-4xy+4y^2$, so both non-leading coefficients are too small. The correct squared bracket is $(x - 4y)^2$.

**Choices:**

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the difference between factorising an expression and solving an equation?

**Back:** Factorising: Writing as a product of brackets.Solving: Finding the specific values of the variable.

**Choices:**

- Factorising: Writing as a product of brackets.Solving: Finding the specific values of the variable. ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising changes an expression's form without choosing variable values. Solving applies to an equation and determines which variable values make that equation true.
- Factorising: Finding the specific values of the variable.Solving: Writing as a product of brackets.
  - _why:_ These definitions have been swapped. Factorising rewrites an expression as a product of brackets, while solving finds the variable values that satisfy an equation.
- Factorising: Multiplying out all brackets.Solving: Collecting like terms in an expression.
  - _why:_ Multiplying out brackets is expanding, the reverse of factorising, while collecting like terms is simplifying rather than solving. Solving finds values that satisfy an equation.
- Factorising: Substituting a value for the variable.Solving: Rearranging an expression without an equals sign.
  - _why:_ Substitution evaluates an expression, while an expression without an equals sign cannot be solved as an equation. Factorising forms a product; solving finds valid variable values.
