## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a single point represent on a scatter graph?

**Back:** A pair of data (two pieces of information) for the same object or event.

**Choices:**

- A pair of data (two pieces of information) for the same object or event. ✔
  - _why:_ Each point combines the values of the two variables for one object or event, with one coordinate read from each axis.
- A single value (one piece of information) for the same object or event.
  - _why:_ Treating a point as one value ignores its second coordinate. Each point represents a pair of data values for the same object or event.
- A pair of data (two pieces of information) from two different objects or events.
  - _why:_ The two coordinates must describe the same object or event, not two different ones. Together, they form one paired data point.
- An average value (a summary of information) for the whole data set.
  - _why:_ A point records an individual pair of values rather than an average of the data set. Each coordinate gives one variable for the same case.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** How do variables change in positive correlation?

**Back:** As one variable increases, the other variable tends to increase.

**Choices:**

- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to increase. ✔
  - _why:_ Positive correlation means that higher values of one variable are generally associated with higher values of the other, although individual points may not follow the trend exactly.
- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to decrease.
  - _why:_ This describes negative correlation, where the variables generally move in opposite directions. In positive correlation, the other variable tends to increase.
- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to stay constant.
  - _why:_ A variable staying constant would not create an upward trend. Positive correlation occurs when the other variable generally increases as the first increases.
- As one variable increases, the other variable changes with no pattern.
  - _why:_ Random change with no overall pattern indicates no correlation. Positive correlation produces a general upward trend from left to right.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do variables change in negative correlation?

**Back:** As one variable increases, the other variable tends to decrease.

**Choices:**

- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to decrease. ✔
  - _why:_ Negative correlation means that higher values of one variable are generally associated with lower values of the other, producing a downward trend from left to right.
- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to increase.
  - _why:_ This is positive correlation because both variables generally move in the same direction. In negative correlation, the other variable tends to decrease.
- As one variable increases, the other variable tends to stay constant.
  - _why:_ A constant second variable would not produce the downward trend required for negative correlation. The other variable should generally decrease as the first increases.
- As one variable increases, the other variable changes with no pattern.
  - _why:_ An absence of any pattern indicates no correlation. Negative correlation has a recognisable downward trend because one variable tends to decrease as the other increases.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** To identify the type of correlation, you should look at the overall direction of the points from [ ___ ].

**Back:** To identify the type of correlation, you should look at the overall direction of the points from left to right.

**Choices:**

- left to right ✔
  - _why:_ Scatter graphs are conventionally read from left to right. An upward overall direction indicates positive correlation, while a downward direction indicates negative correlation.
- top to bottom
  - _why:_ Correlation is classified by following the points horizontally from left to right, not vertically from top to bottom. This reveals whether the overall trend rises or falls.
- centre outwards
  - _why:_ Looking from the centre outwards does not give the standard direction needed to classify the trend. Follow the points from left to right instead.
- point to point
  - _why:_ Following individual points encourages joining the dots and misses the overall pattern. Correlation is identified from the general direction of the points from left to right.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Does correlation between two variables prove that one causes the other?

**Back:** No.It shows they are linked in the data, but not causation.

**Choices:**

- No.It shows they are linked in the data, but not causation. ✔
  - _why:_ Correlation identifies an association between variables, but another variable or coincidence may explain it. Therefore, correlation alone cannot establish that one variable causes the other.
- Yes.It shows one variable is responsible for the change in the other.
  - _why:_ This confuses association with causation. A correlation shows that variables are linked in the data, but it does not prove that either variable causes the other.
- Yes.It proves the variables have a cause-and-effect relationship.
  - _why:_ Even a strong correlation cannot prove a cause-and-effect relationship because other factors may affect both variables. It establishes a link in the data only.
- No.It shows the variables are unrelated and have no connection.
  - _why:_ Saying there is no connection ignores the observed correlation. The variables are linked in the data, but that link alone does not demonstrate causation.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** If points are spread randomly with no clear direction, the graph shows [ ___ ].

**Back:** If points are spread randomly with no clear direction, the graph shows no correlation.

**Choices:**

- no correlation ✔
  - _why:_ No correlation occurs when the points have no recognisable upward or downward trend and appear randomly spread across the scatter graph.
- positive correlation
  - _why:_ Positive correlation requires a general upward trend from left to right. Randomly spread points with no clear direction show no correlation.
- negative correlation
  - _why:_ Negative correlation requires a general downward trend from left to right. If the points have no clear direction, there is no correlation.
- perfect correlation
  - _why:_ Perfect correlation would place all points exactly on a straight line. A random spread with no overall direction instead indicates no correlation.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** State two rules for drawing a sensible line of best fit.

**Back:** Follow the overall trend/direction of the points.Keep roughly the same number of points on each side of the line.

**Choices:**

- Follow the overall trend/direction of the points.Keep roughly the same number of points on each side of the line. ✔
  - _why:_ A sensible line of best fit represents the overall pattern rather than individual points. Balancing the points approximately evenly on either side helps prevent bias.
- Join every point in the order plotted.Make sure the line passes through all of the points.
  - _why:_ Joining every point creates a line graph rather than a line of best fit, and scattered data rarely lie on one line. The line should follow the overall trend.
- Follow the steepest possible direction through the points.Keep roughly all of the points below the line.
  - _why:_ Choosing the steepest line and placing all points below it biases the result. The line should follow the overall trend with roughly equal numbers on each side.
- Draw from the origin to the furthest point on the graph.Make sure the line passes through the origin.
  - _why:_ A line of best fit need not pass through the origin or the furthest point. Its position should be determined by the overall distribution of all points.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it incorrect to "join the dots" on a scatter graph?

**Back:** A scatter graph is not a line graph; you must use a single straight line of best fit to show the trend.

**Choices:**

- A scatter graph is not a line graph; you must use a single straight line of best fit to show the trend. ✔
  - _why:_ The points are separate paired observations and have no required sequence for joining. A single line of best fit summarises their overall trend.
- A scatter graph is a line graph; you must connect consecutive points with straight line segments to show the trend.
  - _why:_ This confuses scatter graphs with line graphs. Scatter points are not consecutive values to be joined; one straight line of best fit should represent the trend.
- A scatter graph is not a line graph; you must draw a smooth curve through every point to show the trend.
  - _why:_ A curve through every point overfits individual observations rather than summarising the relationship. At GCSE, a single straight line of best fit is used to show the trend.
- A scatter graph is a coordinate grid; you must join each point to the origin of the axes to show the trend.
  - _why:_ Lines from each point to the origin do not represent correlation and have no role in a scatter graph. The overall trend is shown with one line of best fit.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Must the line of best fit always pass through the origin $(0,0)$?

**Back:** No, only if the points suggest it should.

**Choices:**

- No, only if the points suggest it should. ✔
  - _why:_ A line of best fit should follow the pattern of the plotted points. It passes through $(0,0)$ only when the observed trend makes the origin appropriate.
- Yes, whenever both of the axes start at zero.
  - _why:_ Starting both axes at zero does not force the data trend through the origin. The correct answer is no; the line passes through $(0,0)$ only if the points suggest it should.
- Yes, every line of best fit must pass through the origin.
  - _why:_ A line of best fit represents the data trend rather than following a fixed rule about the origin. It need not pass through $(0,0)$ unless the points support this.
- No, a line of best fit should never pass through the origin.
  - _why:_ Avoiding the origin automatically is just as mistaken as always using it. A line of best fit may pass through $(0,0)$ when that matches the pattern of points.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How is a line of best fit used to estimate a $y$-value from a given $x$-value?

**Back:** Start at the $x$-value on the horizontal axis and move up to the line.Move across to the vertical axis to read the value.

**Choices:**

- Start at the $x$-value on the horizontal axis and move up to the line.Move across to the vertical axis to read the value. ✔
  - _why:_ Moving vertically from the given $x$-value locates the corresponding point on the line of best fit. Moving horizontally to the vertical axis then gives the estimated $y$-value.
- Start at the $x$-value on the horizontal axis and move across to the line.Move up to the horizontal axis to read the value.
  - _why:_ Moving across from the horizontal axis changes the $x$-coordinate instead of keeping the given one fixed. Move up to the line first, then across to the vertical axis.
- Start at a $y$-value on the vertical axis and move across to the line.Move down to the horizontal axis to read the value.
  - _why:_ The method must begin with the given $x$-value, not a chosen $y$-value. Move up from the horizontal axis to the line, then across to the vertical axis.
- Start at the $x$-value on the horizontal axis and move up to the nearest point.Move across to the vertical axis to read the value.
  - _why:_ Using the nearest plotted point ignores the line of best fit and may give an unrepresentative estimate. Move up to the line itself, then across to the vertical axis.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the exam benefit of drawing construction lines when estimating?

**Back:** They make the method clear even if the final estimate is slightly off.

**Choices:**

- They make the method clear even if the final estimate is slightly off. ✔
  - _why:_ Construction lines show how the value was obtained from the axes and line of best fit. This can demonstrate a correct method despite a small reading or drawing error.
- They guarantee the estimate is exact even if the line of best fit is slightly off.
  - _why:_ Construction lines record the reading process but cannot make an estimate exact. Their benefit is showing a clear method, which may earn credit despite a slightly inaccurate final value.
- They make the graph more accurate even if some of the plotted points are slightly off.
  - _why:_ Construction lines do not alter the plotted data or improve the graph’s underlying accuracy. They show the method used to obtain an estimate from the line of best fit.
- They remove the need for a line of best fit when the final estimate is slightly off.
  - _why:_ Construction lines are used alongside, not instead of, a line of best fit. Their exam benefit is making the estimation method clear when the final reading is slightly inaccurate.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** [ ___ ] means estimating within the range of the data, while extrapolation means estimating outside the range.

**Back:** Interpolation means estimating within the range of the data, while extrapolation means estimating outside the range.

**Choices:**

- Interpolation ✔
  - _why:_ Interpolation is the process of estimating a value between known values and within the observed data range, where the existing trend is supported by plotted evidence.
- Extrapolation
  - _why:_ Extrapolation means estimating beyond the observed data range, not within it. The required term is interpolation because the estimate lies inside the range of known data.
- Correlation
  - _why:_ Correlation describes the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables; it is not an estimation method. Estimating within the data range is called interpolation.
- Causation
  - _why:_ Causation means that one variable directly produces a change in another, rather than estimating a value from a graph. The correct term for estimation within the range is interpolation.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** Interpolation means estimating within the range of the data, while [ ___ ] means estimating outside the range.

**Back:** Interpolation means estimating within the range of the data, while extrapolation means estimating outside the range.

**Choices:**

- extrapolation ✔
  - _why:_ Extrapolation estimates values beyond the smallest or largest observed data values. Such predictions are less reliable because the existing trend might not continue outside the plotted range.
- interpolation
  - _why:_ Interpolation estimates within the observed data range, which is the opposite of the definition required here. Estimating beyond the range of plotted data is called extrapolation.
- correlation
  - _why:_ Correlation describes how two variables are associated and does not mean making an estimate outside the data range. The correct term for such an estimate is extrapolation.
- causation
  - _why:_ Causation is a direct cause-and-effect relationship, not a type of graphical estimation. An estimate made outside the observed range of data is called extrapolation.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is extrapolation considered unreliable?

**Back:** There is no guarantee the trend will continue beyond the plotted data.

**Choices:**

- There is no guarantee the trend will continue beyond the plotted data. ✔
  - _why:_ Extrapolation assumes that the observed relationship continues into an area where there are no plotted points to support it. The pattern may change beyond the recorded range.
- The line of best fit ends at the final plotted point in the data.
  - _why:_ A line of best fit can be extended beyond the final point, so its graphical endpoint is not the problem. Extrapolation is unreliable because the trend itself may not continue.
- All values beyond the plotted range are impossible for the given data.
  - _why:_ Values outside the observed range are not necessarily impossible; they are simply unsupported by the available evidence. Extrapolation is unreliable because the existing trend might not continue.
- Not every point lies exactly on the line within the plotted data.
  - _why:_ Scatter graphs rarely have every point exactly on the line, but that is not the specific problem with extrapolation. Its unreliability comes from assuming the trend continues beyond the evidence.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** Estimating within the data range is [ ___ ] than estimating outside of it.

**Back:** Estimating within the data range is more reliable than estimating outside of it.

**Choices:**

- more reliable ✔
  - _why:_ Interpolation uses a trend within the region supported by observed data points. Extrapolation extends the trend beyond that evidence, so estimating within the range is generally more reliable.
- less reliable
  - _why:_ This reverses the reliability relationship. Estimates within the data range are more reliable because nearby plotted points support the trend, whereas values beyond the range depend on an unsupported continuation.
- equally reliable
  - _why:_ The two methods are not generally equally dependable. Interpolation is more reliable because it estimates among observed data, while extrapolation assumes the trend continues into an unobserved region.
- more uncertain
  - _why:_ This wrongly suggests that estimates supported by surrounding data involve greater uncertainty. Estimating within the range is more reliable because the plotted evidence supports the local trend.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Can you use a line of best fit if the graph shows no correlation?

**Back:** No, it will not provide a meaningful or useful estimate.

**Choices:**

- No, it will not provide a meaningful or useful estimate. ✔
  - _why:_ With no correlation, changes in $x$ do not give a consistent indication of $y$. Any fitted line would therefore produce estimates unsupported by a meaningful pattern.
- Yes, drawing it through the centre will provide a useful estimate.
  - _why:_ A central line does not represent a predictive relationship when the points show no correlation. The correct answer is no because such a line cannot provide a meaningful estimate.
- Yes, having enough plotted points will make the estimate meaningful.
  - _why:_ A large number of points cannot create a relationship when the scatter still shows no correlation. A line of best fit is not useful because $x$ does not meaningfully predict $y$.
- Yes, provided that both axes begin at zero on the graph.
  - _why:_ Starting the axes at zero affects how the graph is displayed, not whether the variables are correlated. With no correlation, a line of best fit cannot produce useful estimates.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** In a scatter graph context, what defines the range of the data?

**Back:** From the smallest plotted value to the largest plotted value on an axis.

**Choices:**

- From the smallest plotted value to the largest plotted value on an axis. ✔
  - _why:_ The range covered by plotted data extends from the lowest observed value to the highest observed value on the relevant axis.
- From zero to the largest numbered value shown on an axis.
  - _why:_ Starting at zero confuses the data range with the full axis scale. The data range runs from the smallest plotted value to the largest plotted value.
- From the first plotted value to the last plotted value on an axis.
  - _why:_ Plotting order does not determine the range. The relevant endpoints are the smallest and largest plotted values, regardless of which points were plotted first or last.
- From the smallest axis label to the largest axis label shown on an axis.
  - _why:_ This describes the displayed axis scale rather than the data range. The range of the data extends from the smallest plotted value to the largest plotted value.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** When plotting a point with coordinates $(12, 30)$, you move [ ___ ] along the horizontal axis and 30 up the vertical axis.

**Back:** When plotting a point with coordinates $(12, 30)$, you move 12 along the horizontal axis and 30 up the vertical axis.

**Choices:**

- 12 ✔
  - _why:_ Coordinates are written in the order $(x, y)$, so the first coordinate gives the horizontal movement. Therefore, $(12, 30)$ requires moving 12 along the horizontal axis.
- 30
  - _why:_ This swaps the two coordinates, a common plotting error. In $(12, 30)$, the first coordinate is horizontal, so the required horizontal movement is 12.
- 18
  - _why:_ Subtracting the coordinates does not determine either position on a scatter graph. The first coordinate directly gives the horizontal movement, which is 12.
- 42
  - _why:_ Adding the two coordinates does not give the horizontal position. Coordinates are read separately, and the first coordinate shows that the point moves 12 horizontally.

**Back:** When plotting a point with coordinates $(12, 30)$, you move 12 along the horizontal axis and 30 up the vertical axis.

**Choices:**

- 30 ✔
  - _why:_ Coordinates are written in the order $(x, y)$, so the second coordinate gives the vertical movement. Therefore, $(12, 30)$ requires moving 30 up the vertical axis.

  - _why:_ Subtracting the first coordinate from the second does not give the vertical position. The second coordinate directly states the vertical movement, which is 30.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What determines whether a variable is placed on the horizontal or vertical axis?

**Back:** The labels provided in the question or table (e.g., hours revised on horizontal).

**Choices:**

- The labels provided in the question or table (e.g., hours revised on horizontal). ✔
  - _why:_ Axis labels identify which variable belongs on each axis. The question, table or partially labelled graph indicates the intended placement, such as hours revised on the horizontal axis.
- The size of each variable’s values (e.g., larger values on horizontal).
  - _why:_ Variables are not assigned to axes according to numerical size. Their placement is determined by the labels or instructions provided in the question or table.
- The order of the collected results (e.g., first result on horizontal).
  - _why:_ The order in which results were collected does not assign variables to axes. The labels in the question or table show which variable belongs on each axis.
- The units used for each variable (e.g., hours always on horizontal).
  - _why:_ No unit is permanently linked to one axis, so hours need not always be horizontal. The supplied labels or instructions determine the placement of each variable.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** A variable is something that [ ___ ], such as height or price.

**Back:** A variable is something that can change, such as height or price.

**Choices:**

- can change ✔
  - _why:_ A variable is a quantity or characteristic whose value can differ between observations, people or situations, such as height, price or time.
- stays fixed
  - _why:_ A quantity that stays fixed is a constant, not a variable. A variable can change or take different values, such as height or price.
- has no value
  - _why:_ Variables do have values, although those values may differ between observations. The defining feature is that a variable can change, not that it lacks a value.
- must increase
  - _why:_ A variable may increase, decrease or remain unchanged between particular observations. It is called a variable because it can change, not because it must increase.
