## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What specific part of an algebraic fraction is the only part allowed to be cancelled?

**Back:** Common factors (expressions that multiply the whole numerator/denominator).

**Choices:**

- Common factors (expressions that multiply the whole numerator/denominator). ✔
  - _why:_ Cancellation divides the numerator and denominator by the same expression. Therefore, only factors multiplying the whole numerator and denominator can be cancelled.
- Common terms (expressions that are added or subtracted in the numerator/denominator).
  - _why:_ Terms within a sum or difference cannot be cancelled individually. The numerator and denominator must first be factorised, because only common factors multiplying the whole expressions can cancel.
- Common variables (letters that appear anywhere in both numerator/denominator).
  - _why:_ A shared variable cannot be cancelled merely because it appears in both expressions. It must be a factor multiplying the whole numerator and denominator.
- Common coefficients (numbers attached to terms in both numerator/denominator).
  - _why:_ Coefficients attached to separate terms cannot automatically be cancelled. Only a common factor of the entire numerator and entire denominator may be divided out.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In algebra, a [ ___ ] is something being multiplied, whereas a term is part of a sum or difference.

**Back:** In algebra, a factor is something being multiplied, whereas a term is part of a sum or difference.

**Choices:**

- factor ✔
  - _why:_ A factor is an expression or quantity involved in multiplication. For example, $x$ and $x+2$ are the two factors in $x(x+2)$.
- term
  - _why:_ A term is a component separated by addition or subtraction, rather than something necessarily being multiplied. The required word is factor.
- coefficient
  - _why:_ A coefficient is the numerical multiplier of a variable, such as $3$ in $3x$. The general name for something being multiplied is a factor.
- variable
  - _why:_ A variable is a letter representing a value, such as $x$. Something involved in multiplication is called a factor, whether or not it contains a variable.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** In algebra, a factor is something being multiplied, whereas a [ ___ ] is part of a sum or difference.

**Back:** In algebra, a factor is something being multiplied, whereas a term is part of a sum or difference.

**Choices:**

- term ✔
  - _why:_ A term is one part of an expression separated from other parts by addition or subtraction, such as $3x$ and $4$ in $3x+4$.

- coefficient

- expression

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it incorrect to cancel the $x$ in the expression $\frac{x+4}{x^2-16}$?

**Back:** The $x$ is a term in a sum, not a factor multiplying the whole numerator.

**Choices:**

- The $x$ is a term in a sum, not a factor multiplying the whole numerator. ✔
  - _why:_ In $x+4$, the $x$ is joined to $4$ by addition and does not multiply the whole numerator. Only common factors of complete expressions may cancel.
- The $x$ is a factor in the numerator, but does not appear anywhere in the denominator.
  - _why:_ The denominator does contain $x$, but mere appearance in both parts is insufficient. In the numerator, $x$ is a term in the sum $x+4$, not a factor of the whole numerator.
- The $x$ can only be cancelled when it has a coefficient of $1$ in both parts.
  - _why:_ Cancellation does not depend on coefficients being $1$. It fails here because $x$ is a term in $x+4$, rather than a factor multiplying the entire numerator.
- The $x$ is part of a quadratic, and quadratic expressions can never have factors cancelled.
  - _why:_ Quadratic expressions can have factors cancelled after factorisation. The problem here is that $x$ is a term in the numerator’s sum, not a factor of the complete numerator.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** To simplify an algebraic fraction, you must [ ___ ] the numerator and denominator before cancelling common factors.

**Back:** To simplify an algebraic fraction, you must factorise the numerator and denominator before cancelling common factors.

**Choices:**

- factorise ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising rewrites sums or differences as products, revealing factors shared by the numerator and denominator. Those common factors can then be cancelled.
- expand
  - _why:_ Expanding turns products into sums and can hide common factors rather than reveal them. The numerator and denominator should be factorised before cancellation.
- calculate
  - _why:_ An algebraic fraction containing variables usually cannot simply be calculated to one number. It must be factorised to identify common factors that can cancel.
- rearrange
  - _why:_ Rearranging terms does not convert a sum into a product or expose cancellable factors. The required process is to factorise the numerator and denominator.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** To simplify an algebraic fraction, you must factorise the numerator and denominator before [ ___ ] common factors.

**Back:** To simplify an algebraic fraction, you must factorise the numerator and denominator before cancelling common factors.

**Choices:**

- cancelling ✔
  - _why:_ Once both expressions are factorised, any identical factor multiplying the whole numerator and denominator can be cancelled by dividing both by that factor.
- expanding
  - _why:_ Expanding common factors would turn products back into sums and prevent direct cancellation. After factorising, the next step is cancelling identical factors.
- adding
  - _why:_ Common factors are not combined by addition. They are cancelled because dividing both the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero factor preserves the fraction’s value.
- subtracting
  - _why:_ Subtracting common factors would change the value of the fraction. After factorisation, shared factors are cancelled by division from the numerator and denominator.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the general factorisation for a difference of two squares $a^2 - b^2$?

**Back:** $$(a - b)(a + b)$

**Choices:**

- $$(a - b)(a + b)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying the conjugate factors gives $a^2+ab-ab-b^2=a^2-b^2$, because the two middle terms cancel.
- $$(a - b)^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring a difference produces $a^2-2ab+b^2$, which includes a middle term and has a positive $b^2$. The correct factorisation is $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$(a + b)^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring a sum produces $a^2+2ab+b^2$, not a difference of two squares. The required conjugate factors are $(a-b)(a+b)$.
- $$(a + b)(b - a)$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction in the second factor changes the sign: this product equals $b^2-a^2$. The correct order is $(a-b)(a+b)$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Factorise the denominator of $\frac{4x^2+16x}{x^2-16}$ using the difference of two squares.

**Back:** $$(x - 4)(x + 4)$

**Choices:**

- $$(x - 4)(x + 4)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The denominator is $x^2-4^2$, a difference of two squares. Applying $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$ gives $(x-4)(x+4)$.
- $$(x - 4)^2$
  - _why:_ This treats the expression as a squared difference and expands to $x^2-8x+16$. The difference of squares $x^2-16$ factorises as $(x-4)(x+4)$.
- $$(x + 4)^2$
  - _why:_ This treats the expression as a squared sum and expands to $x^2+8x+16$. Using conjugate factors instead gives $(x-4)(x+4)$.
- $$(x - 8)(x + 2)$
  - _why:_ Although the constants multiply to $-16$, these factors produce an unwanted $-6x$ term. Opposite constants cancel the middle terms, giving $(x-4)(x+4)$.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** The [ ___ ] of a fraction is the fraction turned upside down, such as $\frac{x-3}{x+1}$ for $\frac{x+1}{x-3}$.

**Back:** The reciprocal of a fraction is the fraction turned upside down, such as $\frac{x-3}{x+1}$ for $\frac{x+1}{x-3}$.

**Choices:**

- reciprocal ✔
  - _why:_ The reciprocal is formed by interchanging a fraction’s numerator and denominator, so the reciprocal of $\frac{x+1}{x-3}$ is $\frac{x-3}{x+1}$.
- denominator
  - _why:_ The denominator is only the expression below the fraction bar, not the result of turning a fraction upside down. The required term is reciprocal.
- numerator
  - _why:_ The numerator is the expression above the fraction bar and does not describe swapping the two parts. A fraction turned upside down is its reciprocal.
- quotient
  - _why:_ A quotient is the result of division, rather than a fraction with its numerator and denominator interchanged. The correct mathematical term is reciprocal.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the 3-step process for dividing algebraic fractions.

**Back:** Keep the first fractionChange division to multiplicationFlip the second fraction (reciprocal)

**Choices:**

- Keep the first fractionChange division to multiplicationFlip the second fraction (reciprocal) ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal. Therefore, keep the first fraction, replace division with multiplication, and flip only the second fraction.
- Flip the first fractionChange division to multiplicationKeep the second fraction unchanged
  - _why:_ Flipping the first fraction changes the dividend rather than converting the divisor into its reciprocal. Keep the first fraction unchanged and flip only the second fraction.
- Keep the first fractionChange multiplication to divisionFlip the second fraction (reciprocal)
  - _why:_ Changing multiplication to division reverses the required procedure. For fraction division, keep the first fraction, change division to multiplication, and flip the second fraction.
- Keep the first fractionChange division to multiplicationKeep the second fraction unchanged
  - _why:_ Replacing division with multiplication without taking the reciprocal gives a different value. The second fraction must be flipped after the operation is changed to multiplication.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** When simplifying a division problem like $\frac{A}{B} \div \frac{C}{D}$, which fraction is flipped?

**Back:** The second fraction ($\frac{C}{D}$) only.

**Choices:**

- The second fraction ($\frac{C}{D}$) only. ✔
  - _why:_ To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal. Thus $\frac{A}{B}$ remains unchanged, while the divisor $\frac{C}{D}$ becomes $\frac{D}{C}$.
- The first fraction ($\frac{A}{B}$) only.
  - _why:_ The first fraction is the dividend and must remain unchanged. It is the second fraction, $\frac{C}{D}$, that is flipped to form its reciprocal.
- The first and second fractions ($\frac{A}{B}$ and $\frac{C}{D}$).
  - _why:_ Flipping both fractions alters the dividend as well as the divisor, producing the wrong expression. Only the second fraction, $\frac{C}{D}$, is flipped.
- The neither fraction; both remain unchanged.
  - _why:_ Neither fraction remains unflipped: division is converted into multiplication by taking the reciprocal of the divisor. Therefore, only the second fraction is flipped.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** When solving an equation with algebraic fractions, the primary goal is to [ ___ ] by multiplying every term by the common denominator.

**Back:** When solving an equation with algebraic fractions, the primary goal is to remove the denominators by multiplying every term by the common denominator.

**Choices:**

- remove the denominators ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term by a common denominator cancels each fractional denominator, converting the equation into one without algebraic fractions and making it easier to solve.
- expand the numerators
  - _why:_ Multiplication by a common denominator cancels denominators rather than expanding numerators. The primary goal is to remove the denominators and obtain an equation without fractions.
- factor the numerators
  - _why:_ Factoring numerators may help with later simplification, but it is not achieved simply by multiplying every term by the common denominator. The goal is to remove the denominators.
- cross-multiply the fractions
  - _why:_ Cross-multiplication applies directly to an equation containing one fraction on each side, not generally to every term. Multiplying by the common denominator removes the denominators.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** When solving an equation with algebraic fractions, the primary goal is to remove the denominators by multiplying every term by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** When solving an equation with algebraic fractions, the primary goal is to remove the denominators by multiplying every term by the common denominator.

**Choices:**

- common denominator ✔
  - _why:_ A common denominator contains all denominator factors required to cancel the fractions. Multiplying every term by it produces an equivalent equation with no fractional denominators.
- greatest common factor
  - _why:_ A greatest common factor identifies shared factors but may not contain everything needed to cancel each denominator. Every term must instead be multiplied by the common denominator.
- common numerator
  - _why:_ Numerators do not determine which multiplier clears the fractions. The required multiplier is the common denominator, which contains the necessary factors from all original denominators.
- largest denominator
  - _why:_ Choosing whichever denominator appears largest does not guarantee that all denominator factors will cancel. The multiplier must be a common denominator of every fraction.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** If solving $\frac{3}{x+2} + \frac{4}{x+7} = 1$, what is the common denominator used to clear the fractions?

**Back:** $$(x+2)(x+7)$

**Choices:**

- $$(x+2)(x+7)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The denominators $x+2$ and $x+7$ have no common algebraic factor, so their product $(x+2)(x+7)$ is the common denominator used to clear both fractions.
- $$(x+2)+(x+7)$
  - _why:_ Adding the denominators is a common slip, but $2x+9$ is not divisible by either original denominator. The required common denominator is $(x+2)(x+7)$.
- $$(x+2)(x-7)$
  - _why:_ Changing $x+7$ to $x-7$ introduces a sign error and would not cancel the original denominator. The common denominator is $(x+2)(x+7)$.
- $$x+9$
  - _why:_ Adding only the constants ignores the variable terms and does not produce a multiple of either denominator. The common denominator needed is $(x+2)(x+7)$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Why must you identify excluded values (e.g., $x \neq -2$) before solving an algebraic fraction equation?

**Back:** To ensure the final solution does not make any original denominator equal to zero.

**Choices:**

- To ensure the final solution does not make any original denominator equal to zero. ✔
  - _why:_ Division by zero is undefined, so any value that makes an original denominator zero cannot be a solution, even if it appears after the equation is rearranged.
- To ensure the final solution does not make any original numerator equal to zero.
  - _why:_ A numerator may equal zero without making a fraction undefined; it usually makes the fraction equal zero. Exclusions prevent an original denominator from equalling zero.
- To ensure the final solution does not make any original denominator equal to one.
  - _why:_ Denominators are allowed to equal one, since division by one is defined. Excluded values specifically prevent any original denominator from becoming zero.
- To ensure the final solution does not make every original denominator have the same value.
  - _why:_ Different denominators may take the same value without making the equation undefined. Excluded values are identified because no original denominator is permitted to equal zero.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** In an equation with three terms like $\frac{5}{x-1} + \frac{2}{x+3} = 1$, you must multiply [ ___ ] (including the 1) by the common denominator.

**Back:** In an equation with three terms like $\frac{5}{x-1} + \frac{2}{x+3} = 1$, you must multiply every term (including the 1) by the common denominator.

**Choices:**

- every term ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying every term preserves the equation’s balance and allows all fractional denominators to cancel. This includes the constant $1$ on the right-hand side.
- only the fractional terms
  - _why:_ Multiplying only the fractions changes one side without applying the same operation to the constant $1$. Every term in the equation must be multiplied by the common denominator.
- only the left-hand side
  - _why:_ Applying the multiplier only to the left-hand side breaks the equality because the right-hand side is unchanged. Every term, including $1$, must receive the multiplier.
- only the denominators
  - _why:_ Multiplying denominators in isolation changes the fractions rather than performing an equivalent operation on the equation. The common denominator must multiply every complete term.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you convert the ratio statement $A:B = C:D$ into an equation?

**Back:** $$\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A ratio $A:B$ is written as the fraction $\frac{A}{B}$, while $C:D$ becomes $\frac{C}{D}$. Since the ratios are equal, the fractions are equal.
- $$\frac{A}{B} = \frac{D}{C}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the second ratio, changing $C:D$ into $D:C$. The order of each ratio must be preserved, giving $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$.
- $$\frac{B}{A} = \frac{C}{D}$
  - _why:_ This reverses only the first ratio, treating $A:B$ as $B:A$. Keeping both quantities in their original order gives $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$.
- $$\frac{A}{D} = \frac{C}{B}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly pairs terms from different ratios as numerators and denominators. Each ratio is converted separately, so the equation is $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the technique of "cross-multiplication" when solving $\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}$?

**Back:** Multiplying each numerator by the opposite denominator ($AD = BC$).

**Choices:**

- Multiplying each numerator by the opposite denominator ($AD = BC$). ✔
  - _why:_ Cross-multiplication forms diagonal products across the equals sign: $A$ multiplies $D$, and $C$ multiplies $B$. This produces the equivalent equation $AD = BC$.
- Multiplying each numerator by its own denominator ($AB = CD$).
  - _why:_ This multiplies vertically within each fraction rather than diagonally across the equation. Cross-multiplication uses the opposite denominators, so the resulting equation is $AD = BC$.
- Multiplying both numerators and both denominators together ($AC = BD$).
  - _why:_ This groups like positions instead of forming diagonal products. Cross-multiplication pairs each numerator with the denominator on the other side, producing $AD = BC$.
- Dividing each numerator by the opposite denominator ($A \div D = C \div B$).
  - _why:_ The word “cross” describes the diagonal pairing, but the operation is multiplication rather than division. The two diagonal products are equated to give $AD = BC$.

  - _why:_ Fractions can represent non-whole values, whereas the stated restriction requires whole-number values. The letters $a$, $b$ and $c$ therefore represent integers.
- irrationals
  - _why:_ Irrational numbers cannot be written as exact fractions and include values such as $\sqrt{2}$. The form instead specifies that $a$, $b$ and $c$ are integers.
- variables
  - _why:_ Although letters often denote variables, here $a$, $b$ and $c$ are fixed numerical coefficients or constants. The stated whole-number condition means they represent integers.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you verify your factorisation is correct before cancelling factors in a fraction?

**Back:** Quickly expand the brackets to see if they return to the original expression.

**Choices:**

- Quickly expand the brackets to see if they return to the original expression. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding reverses factorisation. If multiplying out the brackets reproduces every term of the original expression with the correct coefficients and signs, the factorisation is correct.
- Quickly add the brackets to see if they return to the original expression.
  - _why:_ Bracketed factors are multiplied, not added, so adding them cannot reverse factorisation. Expanding the brackets correctly checks whether their product returns to the original expression.
- Quickly cancel the brackets to see if they return to the original expression.
  - _why:_ Cancelling before checking risks removing factors based on an incorrect factorisation. The brackets should first be expanded to confirm that their product matches the original expression.
- Quickly differentiate the brackets to see if they return to the original expression.
  - _why:_ Differentiation changes an expression and is not the reverse of factorisation. Expanding multiplies the factors back together, allowing direct comparison with the original expression.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Factorise $2x^2 + 11x + 5$.

**Back:** $$(2x + 1)(x + 5)$

**Choices:**

- $$(2x + 1)(x + 5)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding gives $2x^2 + 10x + x + 5 = 2x^2 + 11x + 5$. Therefore these brackets factorise the quadratic exactly.
- $$(2x + 5)(x + 1)$
  - _why:_ These brackets expand to $2x^2 + 7x + 5$, so the middle coefficient is too small. The correct factorisation is $(2x + 1)(x + 5)$.
- $$(2x - 1)(x - 5)$
  - _why:_ Using two negative signs keeps the constant positive but produces a negative middle term: $2x^2 - 11x + 5$. The required factors are $(2x + 1)(x + 5)$.
- $$(2x + 1)(x + 11)$
  - _why:_ This treats the middle coefficient as a bracket constant and expands to $2x^2 + 23x + 11$. The correct factor pair gives $(2x + 1)(x + 5)$.

## Card 22 — `cloze`

**Front:** Factorising $3x^2 + 9x$ by taking out a common factor results in $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** Factorising $3x^2 + 9x$ by taking out a common factor results in $\boxed{3x(x+3)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$3x(x+3)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms share the greatest common factor $3x$. Dividing $3x^2$ and $9x$ by $3x$ leaves $x$ and $3$, giving $3x(x+3)$.
- $$3x(x+6)$
  - _why:_ Dividing $9x$ by the common factor $3x$ gives $3$, not $6$. Taking out $3x$ correctly produces $3x(x+3)$.
- $$3(x+3)$
  - _why:_ This loses a factor of $x$ and expands to $3x+9$, not the original quadratic expression. The greatest common factor is $3x$, giving $3x(x+3)$.
- $$x(3x+3)$
  - _why:_ The second term inside the brackets should be $9$ if only $x$ is extracted, so this expands incorrectly. Fully extracting $3x$ gives $3x(x+3)$.
