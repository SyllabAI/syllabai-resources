# The Nth Term of a Quadratic Sequence Flashcards

_Lane: `gcse-maths-edexcel` · grade 8 · 2 cards (source: MathsGenie flashcards)_

## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** How is a quadratic sequence identified from its differences?

**Back:** It has a constant second difference.

**Choices:**

- It has a constant second difference. ✔
  - _why:_ Taking two successive sets of differences from a quadratic sequence produces the same value throughout. Therefore, a constant second difference identifies the sequence as quadratic.
- It has a constant first difference.
  - _why:_ A constant first difference identifies a linear sequence, not a quadratic one. A quadratic sequence is identified by having a constant second difference.
- It has a constant ratio between terms.
  - _why:_ A constant ratio is characteristic of a geometric sequence rather than a quadratic sequence. Quadratic sequences are identified by a constant second difference.
- It has an alternating second difference.
  - _why:_ An alternating second difference changes rather than remaining fixed, so it does not identify a quadratic sequence. The defining feature is a constant second difference.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In the general quadratic nth term $an^2 + bn + c$, what is the relationship between $a$ and the second difference?

**Back:** The second difference is equal to $2a$ ($a$ is half the second difference).

**Choices:**

- The second difference is equal to $2a$ ($a$ is half the second difference). ✔
  - _why:_ The second difference of $n^2$ is $2$, while the linear and constant terms contribute zero. Multiplying by $a$ therefore gives a second difference of $2a$.
- The second difference is equal to $a$ ($a$ is the second difference).
  - _why:_ This forgets that the second difference of $n^2$ is $2$, not $1$. Therefore, the second difference is $2a$, making $a$ half its value.
- The second difference is equal to $\frac{a}{2}$ ($a$ is twice the second difference).
  - _why:_ This reverses the halving relationship. Since $a$ is half the second difference, the second difference must be twice $a$, giving $2a$.
- The second difference is equal to $2b$ ($b$ is half the second difference).
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the coefficient of the linear term. The term $bn$ has zero second difference, while $an^2$ produces the second difference $2a$.
