## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the minimum and maximum possible values for any probability?

**Back:** 0 and 1

**Choices:**

- 0 and 1 ✔
  - _why:_ Probabilities range from 0 for an impossible event to 1 for a certain event, so every probability must lie between these values inclusive.
- -1 and 1
  - _why:_ The scale is sometimes confused with scales that include negative values, but probabilities cannot be negative. Their minimum is 0 and their maximum is 1.
- 0 and 100
  - _why:_ This mixes decimal probabilities with percentage probabilities. Percentages range from 0% to 100%, but probabilities in decimal form range from 0 to 1.
- 1 and 10
  - _why:_ Probability is not measured on a ten-point scale. An impossible event has probability 0, while a certain event has probability 1.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In probability, a value of 0 means the event is [ ___ ] and a value of 1 means it is certain.

**Back:** In probability, a value of 0 means the event is impossible and a value of 1 means it is certain.

**Choices:**

- impossible ✔
  - _why:_ A probability of 0 means there is no chance that the event will occur, so the event is described as impossible.
- unlikely
  - _why:_ An unlikely event can still happen and therefore has a probability greater than 0. A probability of exactly 0 means the event is impossible.
- likely
  - _why:_ A likely event has a relatively high probability, not a probability of 0. A value of 0 specifically describes an impossible event.
- certain
  - _why:_ This reverses the ends of the probability scale. A certain event has probability 1, while an impossible event has probability 0.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** In probability, a value of 0 means the event is impossible and a value of 1 means it is [ ___ ].

**Back:** In probability, a value of 0 means the event is impossible and a value of 1 means it is certain.

**Choices:**

- certain ✔
  - _why:_ A probability of 1 represents the maximum possible probability, meaning the event is guaranteed to happen and is therefore certain.
- likely
  - _why:_ A likely event has a high probability but may still fail to happen. A probability of exactly 1 means the event is certain.
- impossible
  - _why:_ This reverses the probability scale: an impossible event has probability 0. An event with probability 1 is certain to occur.
- unlikely
  - _why:_ An unlikely event has a low probability close to 0, not the maximum probability. A value of 1 means the event is certain.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the difference between an outcome and an event?

**Back:** Outcome: one possible result (e.g. landing on blue).Event: the specific result you are interested in (e.g. 'landing on blue').

**Choices:**

- Outcome: one possible result (e.g. landing on blue).Event: the specific result you are interested in (e.g. 'landing on blue'). ✔
  - _why:_ An outcome is an individual possible result of an experiment. An event describes the result or collection of outcomes whose probability is being considered.
- Outcome: the result you are interested in (e.g. landing on blue).Event: every possible result of the experiment (e.g. 'all spinner colours').
  - _why:_ This confuses an event with the full sample space. An outcome is one possible result, while an event is the result or outcomes being investigated.
- Outcome: a collection of possible results (e.g. landing on a cool colour).Event: one go of the experiment (e.g. 'spinning once').
  - _why:_ One go of an experiment is a trial, not an event. An outcome is one possible result, while an event identifies the result or outcomes of interest.
- Outcome: the chance of a result (e.g. a probability of 0.5).Event: the equipment being used (e.g. 'a spinner').
  - _why:_ A probability measures chance and the spinner is the experimental equipment. An outcome is a possible result, while an event is the result or outcomes being considered.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean if a dice or spinner is biased?

**Back:** The outcomes are not equally likely.

**Choices:**

- The outcomes are not equally likely. ✔
  - _why:_ A biased dice or spinner favours some results over others, so its possible outcomes do not all have the same probability.
- The outcomes are always equally likely.
  - _why:_ Equally likely outcomes describe a fair dice or spinner, which is the opposite of biased. With biased equipment, some outcomes are more likely than others.
- The outcomes are completely impossible.
  - _why:_ Bias changes the relative chances of outcomes; it does not necessarily make every outcome impossible. The defining feature is that the outcomes are not equally likely.
- The outcomes are known in advance.
  - _why:_ Bias does not make the next outcome predictable with certainty. It means only that the possible outcomes do not all have equal probabilities.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If a table lists every possible outcome of an experiment, what must the probabilities add up to?

**Back:** 1

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ Exactly one of the possible outcomes must occur, so the probabilities across a complete table of mutually exclusive outcomes must add up to 1.
- 100
  - _why:_ This confuses decimal probabilities with percentages. Probabilities written as percentages total 100%, but probabilities written in the usual decimal or fractional form total 1.
- 0
  - _why:_ A total of 0 would mean every possible outcome was impossible, so the experiment could not produce a result. All possible outcomes have probabilities totalling 1.
- 2
  - _why:_ Adding to more than 1 would represent more than a 100% total chance. A complete set of possible outcomes must have probabilities that sum to 1.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate a missing probability from a table of all possible outcomes?

**Back:** Add all known probabilities.Subtract that total from 1.

**Choices:**

- Add all known probabilities.Subtract that total from 1. ✔
  - _why:_ All probabilities in a complete outcome table total 1. Adding the known values and subtracting their sum from 1 therefore gives the missing probability.
- Add all known probabilities.Subtract that total from 100.
  - _why:_ Subtracting from 100 incorrectly treats decimal probabilities as whole percentages. The known probabilities should be added, then their total should be subtracted from 1.
- Multiply all known probabilities.Subtract that product from 1.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is used for some combined-event calculations, not for completing a probability table. Add the known probabilities and subtract their total from 1.
- Add all known probabilities.Use that total as the missing value.
  - _why:_ The sum of the known probabilities is the probability already accounted for, not the missing value. Subtract that sum from 1 to find the remainder.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** A single go of an experiment (like one roll of a dice) is called a [ ___ ].

**Back:** A single go of an experiment (like one roll of a dice) is called a trial.

**Choices:**

- trial ✔
  - _why:_ A trial is one performance or go of a probability experiment, such as rolling a dice once or spinning a spinner once.
- experiment
  - _why:_ An experiment is the overall process that can be performed, such as rolling a dice repeatedly. One individual go of that experiment is called a trial.
- outcome
  - _why:_ An outcome is the result produced, such as rolling a four, rather than the act of rolling. A single go of an experiment is a trial.
- event
  - _why:_ An event is the result or group of outcomes being investigated, not one performance of the experiment. A single go is called a trial.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the estimated number of times an event will happen?

**Back:** $$$\text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying the probability by the number of trials gives the expected frequency, which estimates how many times the event should occur across all the trials.
- $$$\text{probability} + \text{number of trials}$$
  - _why:_ Adding quantities with different meanings does not produce an expected frequency. Multiply the probability by the number of trials to estimate how often the event will happen.
- $$$\frac{\text{number of trials}}{\text{probability}}$$
  - _why:_ Dividing the number of trials by the probability makes less likely events appear more frequent. The correct calculation is probability multiplied by the number of trials.
- $$$\frac{\text{probability}}{\text{number of trials}}$$
  - _why:_ Dividing by the number of trials produces a very small value rather than an expected frequency. Multiply the probability by the number of trials instead.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** An estimate is a [ ___ ], not a guarantee.

**Back:** An estimate is a sensible prediction, not a guarantee.

**Choices:**

- sensible prediction ✔
  - _why:_ An estimate uses available information to make a reasonable prediction, but the actual result may differ because probability describes uncertainty rather than certainty.
- certain result
  - _why:_ Treating an estimate as certain ignores the natural variation in random events. An estimate is a sensible prediction, so the actual result is not guaranteed.
- random guess
  - _why:_ A random guess need not use any evidence or probability. An estimate is a sensible prediction based on the information available, although it may not be exact.
- exact calculation
  - _why:_ An exact calculation would give a fixed result, whereas an estimate predicts an uncertain outcome. The missing term is sensible prediction because actual frequencies can vary.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** An estimate is a sensible prediction, not a [ ___ ].

**Back:** An estimate is a sensible prediction, not a guarantee.

**Choices:**

- guarantee ✔
  - _why:_ An estimate predicts what is likely or expected, but random variation means the observed result can differ. It therefore cannot guarantee the actual outcome.
- prediction
  - _why:_ An estimate is itself a prediction based on available information, so it is wrong to exclude prediction. The sentence contrasts a sensible prediction with a guarantee.
- forecast

- approximation
  - _why:_ An estimate can be an approximation rather than an exact value, so excluding approximation reverses its meaning. The missing word is guarantee because estimates remain uncertain.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What does mutually exclusive mean?

**Back:** Outcomes that cannot happen at the same time.

**Choices:**

- Outcomes that cannot happen at the same time. ✔
  - _why:_ Mutually exclusive outcomes have no overlap, so one trial cannot produce both outcomes. For example, a single coin toss cannot be both heads and tails.
- Outcomes that can happen at the same time.
  - _why:_ This describes overlapping outcomes, not mutually exclusive ones. Mutually exclusive outcomes cannot occur together in the same trial, so their intersection has probability zero.
- Outcomes that are equally likely to happen.
  - _why:_ Equal likelihood concerns whether outcomes have the same probability, not whether they overlap. Mutually exclusive outcomes are those that cannot happen at the same time.
- Outcomes that have probabilities adding to one.
  - _why:_ Probabilities add to one only when outcomes are both mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Mutual exclusivity alone means the outcomes cannot happen at the same time.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the probability of one or another mutually exclusive outcome happening?

**Back:** Add their individual probabilities together.

**Choices:**

- Add their individual probabilities together. ✔
  - _why:_ For mutually exclusive outcomes, there is no overlap to count twice. Therefore, the probability of one or the other occurring is the sum of their probabilities.
- Multiply their individual probabilities together.
  - _why:_ Multiplication is commonly used for independent events linked by “and”, not mutually exclusive outcomes linked by “or”. Here, add the individual probabilities together.
- Subtract their individual probabilities together.
  - _why:_ Subtraction may be used for complements or differences, but it does not calculate either mutually exclusive outcome occurring. Their individual probabilities must be added together.
- Divide their individual probabilities together.
  - _why:_ Division compares probabilities rather than combining possible outcomes. Because the outcomes are mutually exclusive and connected by “or”, add their individual probabilities together.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** If outcomes are in the ratio $5:4:3$, the total number of parts is [ ___ ].

**Back:** If outcomes are in the ratio $5:4:3$, the total number of parts is 12.

**Choices:**

- 12 ✔
  - _why:_ The total number of ratio parts is found by addition: $5+4+3=12$. Each outcome’s probability can then be written over this total.
- 9
  - _why:_ This results from adding only $5+4$ and overlooking the third part. All ratio parts must be included, giving $5+4+3=12$.
- 60
  - _why:_ Multiplying $5\times4\times3$ does not find the total number of ratio parts. Ratio parts are added, so the correct total is $12$.
- 15
  - _why:_ This comes from multiplying the largest part, $5$, by the three outcomes. The parts can differ, so they must be added to obtain $12$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you convert a ratio into probabilities?

**Back:** Add the ratio parts to find the total.Put each part over the total as a fraction.

**Choices:**

- Add the ratio parts to find the total.Put each part over the total as a fraction. ✔
  - _why:_ The total number of ratio parts represents all possible outcomes. Dividing each individual part by that total gives its probability, and the resulting probabilities sum to one.
- Multiply the ratio parts to find the product.Put each part over the product as a fraction.
  - _why:_ Multiplying ratio parts does not give the total share and produces probabilities that do not sum to one. Add the parts, then place each part over that total.
- Add the ratio parts to find the total.Put the total over each part as a fraction.
  - _why:_ Reversing each fraction can produce values greater than one, which cannot be probabilities. After adding the parts, place each individual part over the total.
- Count the ratio parts to find the outcomes.Put each part over the outcomes as a fraction.
  - _why:_ Using the number of categories as the denominator wrongly assumes equal probabilities. Add the numerical ratio parts, then divide each part by that total.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** If the probability of outcome A is twice the probability of outcome B, what ratio describes their relationship?

**Back:** $$2:1$

**Choices:**

- $$2:1$ ✔
  - _why:_ If A is twice as probable as B, A has two equal probability parts for every one part belonging to B. Therefore, $A:B=2:1$.
- $$1:2$
  - _why:_ This reverses the order and makes B twice as probable as A. Since A has twice B’s probability, the correct ratio is $A:B=2:1$.
- $$2:2$
  - _why:_ This ratio simplifies to $1:1$, meaning A and B are equally probable. A must have two parts for every one part of B, giving $2:1$.
- $$1:1$
  - _why:_ A ratio of $1:1$ represents equal probabilities, not one probability being twice the other. Twice as likely gives the relationship $A:B=2:1$.

**Front:** If one missing probability is "three times" another, the leftover probability must be split into [ ___ ] equal parts.

**Back:** If one missing probability is "three times" another, the leftover probability must be split into 4 equal parts.

**Choices:**

- 4 ✔
  - _why:_ The probabilities are in the ratio 3:1, so the ratio contains 3 + 1 = 4 equal parts altogether.
- 3
  - _why:_ This counts only the three parts belonging to the larger probability. The smaller probability contributes one additional part, so the leftover must be divided into 4 equal parts.
- 2
  - _why:_ Splitting into two equal parts would make the missing probabilities the same. A 3:1 relationship has four parts altogether, so the leftover must be divided into 4 parts.
- 6
  - _why:_ Doubling the multiplier does not give the total number of ratio parts. The ratio is 3:1, whose entries sum to 4, so there are 4 equal parts.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** When two missing probabilities are described as "the same", they are split in a [ ___ ] ratio.

**Back:** When two missing probabilities are described as "the same", they are split in a 1:1 ratio.

**Choices:**

- 1:1 ✔
  - _why:_ Equal probabilities contain the same number of parts. Each receives one identical share, so their simplest ratio is 1:1.
- 1:2
  - _why:_ This makes the second probability twice the first rather than making them equal. Equal probabilities each receive one identical share, giving the ratio 1:1.
- 2:1
  - _why:_ This makes the first probability twice the second, reversing an unequal 1:2 relationship. The word “same” requires equal shares, represented by 1:1.
- 0:1
  - _why:_ A zero part would make the first probability impossible rather than equal to the second. Two equal missing probabilities are split in the ratio 1:1.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the total number of items if you know the count of one item and its probability?

**Back:** $$$\frac{\text{number of items}}{\text{probability}}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{\text{number of items}}{\text{probability}}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Probability equals the number of the chosen items divided by the total. Rearranging this relationship gives the total as the known number of items divided by its probability.
- $$$\text{number of items} \times \text{probability}$$
  - _why:_ Multiplication finds an expected count when the total is already known. To recover the total from a known count and probability, divide the number of items by the probability.
- $$$\frac{\text{probability}}{\text{number of items}}$$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required division and does not produce a total number of items. Rearranging probability = count ÷ total gives total = count ÷ probability.
- $$$\frac{\text{number of items}}{1-\text{probability}}$$
  - _why:_ Using the complementary probability would estimate the total from the count of items not in the stated category. For the given category, divide its count by its probability.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** If 30 green pens represent a probability of 0.15, the total number of pens is [ ___ ].

**Back:** If 30 green pens represent a probability of 0.15, the total number of pens is 200.

**Choices:**

- 200 ✔
  - _why:_ The total is found by dividing the observed count by its probability: $30 \div 0.15 = 200$. Therefore, the full collection contains 200 pens.
- 4.5
  - _why:_ This results from multiplying $30$ by $0.15$, which would find 15% of 30 rather than the total. The required calculation is $30 \div 0.15 = 200$.
- 20
  - _why:_ This comes from treating $0.15$ as $1.5$, a decimal-place error. Dividing correctly by $0.15$ gives $30 \div 0.15 = 200$ pens.
- 2000
  - _why:_ This places the decimal one position too far when dividing by $0.15$. Since 30 is 15% of the total, the correct total is $30 \div 0.15 = 200$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the probability of outcome $X$ written in mathematical notation?

**Back:** $$P(X)$

**Choices:**

- $$P(X)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The notation $P(X)$ means the probability that outcome or event $X$ occurs, with the event written inside parentheses after $P$.
- $$P(X')$
  - _why:_ A prime commonly denotes the complement, so this represents the probability that $X$ does not occur. The probability of $X$ itself is written $P(X)$.
- $$n(X)$
  - _why:_ The notation $n(X)$ represents the number of elements or outcomes in set $X$, not its probability. The probability of the outcome is written $P(X)$.
- $$P(X^c)$
  - _why:_ A superscript $c$ denotes the complement of an event, so this is the probability that $X$ does not occur. The required notation is $P(X)$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** A gardener plants 50 seeds with a growth probability of 0.88. What is the estimated number of seeds that grow?

**Back:** 44 ($0.88 \times 50$)

**Choices:**

- 44 ($0.88 \times 50$) ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying the number of trials by the growth probability gives the expected frequency: $50 \times 0.88 = 44$, so an estimated 44 seeds grow.
- 6 ($0.12 \times 50$)
  - _why:_ This uses the failure probability, $1 - 0.88 = 0.12$, so it estimates seeds that do not grow. The growing estimate is $0.88 \times 50 = 44$.
- 45 ($0.9 \times 50$)
  - _why:_ Rounding $0.88$ to $0.9$ before calculating loses accuracy and overestimates growth. Using the given probability directly gives $0.88 \times 50 = 44$.
- 43 ($0.88 \times 49$)
  - _why:_ Subtracting one from the number of trials creates an off-by-one error. All 50 seeds are planted, so the estimate is $0.88 \times 50 = 44$.
