# Proof of the Circle Theorems Flashcards

_Lane: `gcse-maths-edexcel` · grade 8 · 21 cards (source: MathsGenie flashcards)_

## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** All radii of the same circle are [ ___ ], so a triangle made from two radii is [ ___ ].

**Back:** All radii of the same circle are equal, so a triangle made from two radii is isosceles.

**Choices:**

- All radii of the same circle are equal, so a triangle made from two radii is isosceles. ✔
  - _why:_ Radii from the centre to the circumference have the same length. Therefore, a triangle containing two radii has two equal sides and is isosceles.
- All radii of the same circle are equal, so a triangle made from two radii is equilateral.
  - _why:_ Two radii provide only two known equal sides; the chord joining their endpoints need not have the same length. The triangle is therefore isosceles, not necessarily equilateral.
- All radii of the same circle are unequal, so a triangle made from two radii is scalene.
  - _why:_ This reverses the defining property of a circle: every radius of one circle has equal length. The two equal radii therefore form an isosceles triangle.
- All radii of the same circle are perpendicular, so a triangle made from two radii is right-angled.
  - _why:_ Radii are equal in length, but they are not generally perpendicular to one another. Their equality makes the triangle isosceles; the central angle can have many values.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In triangle $AOB$ where $OA=OB$, what angle equality follows?

**Back:** $\angle OAB=\angle ABO$.

**Choices:**

- $\angle OAB=\angle ABO$. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $OA=OB$, triangle $AOB$ is isosceles with base $AB$. The angles opposite those equal sides, $\angle OAB$ and $\angle ABO$, are equal.
- $\angle AOB=\angle OAB$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly equates the vertex angle with a base angle. Since $OA=OB$, the equal opposite angles are $\angle OAB$ and $\angle ABO$.
- $\angle AOB=\angle ABO$.
  - _why:_ Equal sides produce equal angles opposite them, not an equality involving the included angle at $O$. Therefore $\angle OAB=\angle ABO$.
- $\angle OAB=\angle AOB$.
  - _why:_ This would require $OB=AB$, which is not given. The stated equality $OA=OB$ makes their opposite angles equal, so $\angle OAB=\angle ABO$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** The angle at the centre is [ ___ ] the angle at the circumference when both stand on the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc.

**Choices:**

- The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc. ✔
  - _why:_ The centre-angle theorem states that angles subtended by the same arc satisfy this $2:1$ relationship: the central angle is twice the corresponding circumference angle.
- The angle at the centre is equal to the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc.
  - _why:_ This confuses the theorem with equal angles in the same segment. For the same arc, the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference.
- The angle at the centre is three times the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc.
  - _why:_ The multiplier is incorrectly taken as three. Splitting the proof into isosceles triangles shows that the central angle is exactly twice the circumference angle.
- The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference when both stand on the different arcs.
  - _why:_ The $2:1$ relationship applies only when both angles are subtended by the same arc. Angles standing on different arcs do not generally have this relationship.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** In the centre-angle proof, if $\angle PQO=x$, why is $\angle OPQ=x$?

**Back:** $OP=OQ$, so triangle $OPQ$ is isosceles.

**Choices:**

- $OP=OQ$, so triangle $OPQ$ is isosceles. ✔
  - _why:_ Both $OP$ and $OQ$ are radii of the same circle, so they are equal. Their opposite base angles, $\angle PQO$ and $\angle OPQ$, are therefore equal.
- $OP=PQ$, so triangle $OPQ$ is isosceles.
  - _why:_ This selects the wrong pair of equal sides and would equate different opposite angles. The radii are $OP$ and $OQ$, so $OP=OQ$ gives the required base-angle equality.
- $OQ=PQ$, so triangle $OPQ$ is isosceles.
  - _why:_ The chord $PQ$ is not generally equal to the radius $OQ$. The known equal sides are the radii $OP$ and $OQ$, making the angles at $P$ and $Q$ equal.
- $OP\perp OQ$, so triangle $OPQ$ is right-angled.
  - _why:_ The radii need not meet at a right angle, and right-angled triangles do not automatically have equal acute angles. The equality follows from $OP=OQ$ and the isosceles triangle theorem.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** In triangle $OPQ$, if the base angles are both $x$, what is $\angle POQ$?

**Back:** $180^\circ-2x$.

**Choices:**

- $180^\circ-2x$. ✔
  - _why:_ The angles in a triangle total $180^\circ$. Subtracting the two base angles, $x+x=2x$, leaves $\angle POQ=180^\circ-2x$.
- $180^\circ-x$.
  - _why:_ This subtracts only one of the two base angles. Since both base angles equal $x$, their total is $2x$, giving $\angle POQ=180^\circ-2x$.
- $360^\circ-2x$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the $360^\circ$ total around a point instead of the $180^\circ$ angle sum of a triangle. The correct angle is $180^\circ-2x$.
- $90^\circ-x$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly assumes the triangle’s angles total $90^\circ$. A triangle’s interior angles total $180^\circ$, so subtracting both base angles gives $180^\circ-2x$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** In the centre-angle proof, what central angle is obtained after subtracting $180^\circ-2x$ and $180^\circ-2y$ from $360^\circ$?

**Back:** $2x+2y$.

**Choices:**

- $2x+2y$. ✔
  - _why:_ The required central angle is $360^\circ-(180^\circ-2x)-(180^\circ-2y)$. The constant terms cancel, leaving $2x+2y$.
- $x+y$.
  - _why:_ This loses the factors of two when simplifying the two isosceles-triangle angles. Subtracting both expressions from $360^\circ$ gives $2x+2y$, twice the circumference angle.
- $360^\circ-2x-2y$.
  - _why:_ This subtracts the variable terms instead of subtracting each entire bracket. Expanding the subtraction changes their signs, so the result simplifies to $2x+2y$.
- $180^\circ-2x-2y$.
  - _why:_ This uses $180^\circ$ rather than the full $360^\circ$ around the centre and mishandles the bracket signs. Correct subtraction gives the central angle $2x+2y$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If $\angle PQO=x$ and $\angle OQR=y$, what is $\angle PQR$?

**Back:** $x+y$.

**Choices:**

- $x+y$. ✔
  - _why:_ The ray $QO$ divides $\angle PQR$ into the adjacent angles $\angle PQO=x$ and $\angle OQR=y$. Adding them gives $\angle PQR=x+y$.
- $x-y$.
  - _why:_ Adjacent parts of the same larger angle must be added, not subtracted. Since $QO$ splits $\angle PQR$ into angles $x$ and $y$, the total is $x+y$.
- $2x+2y$.
  - _why:_ This mistakenly applies the centre-angle doubling rule to the circumference angle itself. The two adjacent components at $Q$ simply add, so $\angle PQR=x+y$.
- $180^\circ-x-y$.
  - _why:_ This treats the three angles as though they formed a triangle angle sum. Here $x$ and $y$ are adjacent parts of $\angle PQR$, so the angle is $x+y$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a line passes through [ ___ ] and both ends lie on the circle, it is a [ ___ ].

**Back:** If a line passes through the centre and both ends lie on the circle, it is a diameter.

**Choices:**

- If a line passes through the centre and both ends lie on the circle, it is a diameter. ✔
  - _why:_ A chord whose endpoints lie on the circumference and which passes through the circle’s centre is a diameter. It is also the longest possible chord.
- If a line passes through the centre and both ends lie on the circle, it is a radius.
  - _why:_ A radius has only one endpoint on the circumference and ends at the centre. With both endpoints on the circle and the centre between them, the line is a diameter.
- If a line passes through a point inside and both ends lie on the circle, it is a diameter.
  - _why:_ Passing through any interior point is insufficient; many ordinary chords do this. The line is specifically a diameter only when it passes through the centre.
- If a line passes through the centre and both ends lie on the circle, it is a tangent.
  - _why:_ A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point and does not have two endpoints on the circumference. A chord through the centre is a diameter.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** In the semicircle proof, why does $\angle AOB+\angle BOC=180^\circ$?

**Back:** $AOC$ is a straight line.

**Choices:**

- $AOC$ is a straight line. ✔
  - _why:_ Points $A$, $O$ and $C$ are collinear because $AC$ is a diameter. Therefore the adjacent central angles $ngle AOB$ and $ngle BOC$ form a straight angle of $180^\circ$.
- $AB$ is a straight line.
  - _why:_ The chord $AB$ does not generally pass through the centre, so it is not necessarily a straight line through $O$. The angles total $180^\circ$ because $AOC$ is a straight line.
- $AOB$ is an isosceles triangle.
  - _why:_ The radii $OA$ and $OB$ make triangle $AOB$ isosceles, but this does not make the two central angles supplementary. Their sum is $180^\circ$ because $AOC$ is a straight line.
- $OA$ and $OC$ are equal radii.
  - _why:_ Equal radii make $OA=OC$, but equality of lengths does not establish a sum of angles. The adjacent angles total $180^\circ$ because $AOC$ is a straight line.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What does $(180^\circ-2x)+(180^\circ-2y)=180^\circ$ give for $x+y$?

**Back:** $x+y=90^\circ$.

**Choices:**

- $x+y=90^\circ$. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding and simplifying gives $360^\circ-2x-2y=180^\circ$, so $2x+2y=180^\circ$. Dividing every term by $2$ gives $x+y=90^\circ$.
- $x+y=180^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This misses the final division by $2$. Simplification gives $2x+2y=180^\circ$, and dividing both sides by $2$ gives the correct result $x+y=90^\circ$.
- $x+y=360^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This treats the original $180^\circ$ total as though it were $360^\circ$. Correctly collecting terms gives $2x+2y=180^\circ$, hence $x+y=90^\circ$.
- $x+y=45^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly halves the result twice. Once $2x+2y=180^\circ$ has been obtained, a single division by $2$ gives $x+y=90^\circ$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a semicircle, the angle on the circumference opposite the diameter is [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a semicircle, the angle on the circumference opposite the diameter is $90^\circ$.

**Choices:**

- $$90^\circ$ ✔
  - _why:_ The angle in a semicircle theorem states that an angle subtended by a diameter at any point on the remaining circumference is a right angle, so it equals $90^\circ$.
- $$180^\circ$
  - _why:_ A diameter subtends $180^\circ$ at the centre, not at the circumference. The corresponding angle on the circumference is half the central angle, so it is $90^\circ$.
- $$45^\circ$
  - _why:_ This results from halving the circumference angle rather than the central angle. The diameter subtends $180^\circ$ at the centre, so the circumference angle is $90^\circ$.
- $$60^\circ$
  - _why:_ This confuses the semicircle theorem with an equilateral-triangle angle. A diameter subtends a right angle at the circumference, so the required angle is $90^\circ$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What can you conclude about two angles standing on the same chord on the same side of it?

**Back:** They are equal.

**Choices:**

- They are equal. ✔
  - _why:_ Angles subtended by the same chord in the same segment are equal. Both angles intercept the same endpoints and lie on the same side of the chord.
- They are supplementary.
  - _why:_ Supplementary angles arise as opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral, not as angles in the same segment. Angles standing on the same chord on the same side are equal.
- They are complementary.
  - _why:_ Complementary angles sum to $90^\circ$, which is associated with an angle in a semicircle. Angles standing on the same chord in the same segment are instead equal.
- They are perpendicular.
  - _why:_ Perpendicular describes lines meeting at $90^\circ$, not a relationship between two angle sizes. Angles standing on the same chord on the same side are equal.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Why are $\angle ABD$ and $\angle ACD$ equal when both stand on chord $AD$?

**Back:** Both are half of the same central angle, $\angle AOD$.

**Choices:**

- Both are half of the same central angle, $\angle AOD$. ✔
  - _why:_ Each circumference angle standing on chord $AD$ is half the central angle subtended by that chord. Since both are half of $ngle AOD$, they are equal.
- Both are twice the same central angle, $ngle AOD$.
  - _why:_ The central angle theorem has the relationship reversed here: the central angle is twice each circumference angle. Therefore both circumference angles are half of the same central angle, $ngle AOD$.
- Both are equal to the same central angle, $ngle AOD$.
  - _why:_ A circumference angle is not equal to the central angle subtended by the same chord. Each is half of the same central angle, $ngle AOD$, which proves they are equal.
- Both are half of the same central angle, $ngle BOC$.
  - _why:_ Chord $AD$ subtends the central angle $ngle AOD$, not $ngle BOC$. Both circumference angles are equal because each is half of $ngle AOD$.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on the [ ___ ] of a circle.

**Back:** A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on the circumference of a circle.

**Choices:**

- circumference ✔
  - _why:_ A quadrilateral is cyclic precisely when each of its four vertices lies on one circle’s circumference. Its sides are chords joining pairs of those points.
- centre
  - _why:_ The centre is a single point and cannot contain four distinct vertices of a quadrilateral. In a cyclic quadrilateral, all four vertices lie on the circle’s circumference.
- diameter
  - _why:_ The diameter is one particular chord through the centre, so four vertices cannot all lie on it and form a quadrilateral. They must lie on the circumference.
- radius
  - _why:_ The radius is a line segment from the centre to the circumference, not the boundary containing all four vertices. A cyclic quadrilateral’s vertices lie on the circumference.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** In cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, what is $\angle ABC+\angle ADC$?

**Back:** $180^\circ$.

**Choices:**

- $180^\circ$. ✔
  - _why:_ Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary because the arcs subtending them together form the complete circle. Therefore, their angle sum is $180^\circ$.
- $90^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This confuses supplementary opposite angles with complementary angles. In a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles subtend arcs totalling $360^\circ$, so their angles sum to $180^\circ$.
- $360^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This uses the angle sum around a point rather than the cyclic quadrilateral theorem. Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, so the required sum is $180^\circ$.
- $270^\circ$.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly subtracts a right angle from a full turn. The opposite angles of any cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, meaning their sum is exactly $180^\circ$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Why do opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to $180^\circ$?

**Back:** Their central angles add to $360^\circ$, and circumference angles are half.

**Choices:**

- Their central angles add to $360^\circ$, and circumference angles are half. ✔
  - _why:_ The central angles corresponding to the two opposite circumference angles make one full turn, totalling $360^\circ$. Each circumference angle is half its central angle, so their sum is $180^\circ$.
- Their central angles add to $180^\circ$, and circumference angles are half.
  - _why:_ The two relevant central angles complete a full turn, not a straight angle, so they total $360^\circ$. Halving that total gives the opposite circumference angles’ sum of $180^\circ$.
- Their central angles add to $360^\circ$, and circumference angles are double.
  - _why:_ The central angle is twice the angle at the circumference, so the relationship has been reversed. The central angles total $360^\circ$, and their half-sized circumference angles total $180^\circ$.
- Their central angles add to $180^\circ$, and circumference angles are double.
  - _why:_ Both relationships are reversed: the relevant central angles make a full $360^\circ$ turn, and circumference angles are half the corresponding central angles, producing a sum of $180^\circ$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What angle does a radius make with a tangent at the point of contact?

**Back:** $90^\circ$.

**Choices:**

- $90^\circ$. ✔
  - _why:_ A radius is perpendicular to a tangent where the tangent touches the circle. Therefore, the angle between them at the point of contact is $90^\circ$.
- $45^\circ$.
  - _why:_ Confusing the radius-tangent angle with an angle from an isosceles triangle can suggest $45^\circ$. A radius and tangent are perpendicular, so the correct angle is $90^\circ$.
- $180^\circ$.
  - _why:_ An angle of $180^\circ$ forms a straight line, but a tangent does not continue along the radius. The radius meets the tangent perpendicularly at $90^\circ$.
- $0^\circ$.
  - _why:_ An angle of $0^\circ$ would make the radius and tangent parallel or coincident. They are perpendicular at the point of contact, giving an angle of $90^\circ$.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** The tangent-chord theorem is also called the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The tangent-chord theorem is also called the alternate segment theorem.

**Choices:**

- alternate segment theorem ✔
  - _why:_ The alternate segment theorem states that the angle between a tangent and chord equals the angle subtended by that chord in the opposite segment.
- same segment theorem
  - _why:_ The angle in the same segment theorem compares two angles subtended by the same chord. The tangent-chord result is instead called the alternate segment theorem.
- cyclic quadrilateral theorem
  - _why:_ The cyclic quadrilateral theorem concerns opposite angles adding to $180^\circ$, not an angle between a tangent and chord. The required name is the alternate segment theorem.
- angle at the centre theorem
  - _why:_ The angle at the centre theorem compares central and circumferential angles subtended by one arc. The tangent-chord theorem is also called the alternate segment theorem.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the tangent-chord theorem say?

**Back:** The tangent-chord angle equals the angle in the opposite segment.

**Choices:**

- The tangent-chord angle equals the angle in the opposite segment. ✔
  - _why:_ For a chord through the point of contact, the angle between that chord and the tangent equals the angle subtended by the chord in the opposite segment.
- The tangent-chord angle equals the angle at the centre.
  - _why:_ The angle at the centre is generally twice the corresponding angle at the circumference, so it does not equal the tangent-chord angle. The opposite-segment angle does.
- The tangent-chord angle equals twice the angle in the opposite segment.
  - _why:_ The factor of two belongs to the angle at the centre theorem, not the tangent-chord theorem. The tangent-chord angle equals, rather than doubles, the opposite-segment angle.
- The tangent-chord angle equals half the angle in the opposite segment.
  - _why:_ Halving is used when deriving a circumference angle from the corresponding central angle. The tangent-chord angle directly equals the angle in the opposite segment.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the tangent-chord angle $90^\circ-y$ when $\angle OCB=y$?

**Back:** $$OC$ is perpendicular to the tangent, leaving $90^\circ-y$.

**Choices:**

- $$OC$ is perpendicular to the tangent, leaving $90^\circ-y$. ✔
  - _why:_ A radius meets a tangent perpendicularly, making a total angle of $90^\circ$. Since $\angle OCB=y$ occupies part of it, the remaining tangent-chord angle is $90^\circ-y$.
- $$OC$ is perpendicular to the tangent, leaving $180^\circ-y$.
  - _why:_ Perpendicular lines form $90^\circ$, not $180^\circ$. Subtracting $y$ from the right angle means the tangent-chord angle is $90^\circ-y$, rather than $180^\circ-y$.
- $$OC$ is parallel to the tangent, leaving $90^\circ-y$.
  - _why:_ A radius to the point of contact is perpendicular, not parallel, to the tangent. The right angle is split into $y$ and the remaining angle $90^\circ-y$.
- $$OC$ is perpendicular to the tangent, leaving $90^\circ+y$.
  - _why:_ The two adjacent angles must total $90^\circ$, so $y$ must be subtracted rather than added. Consequently, the tangent-chord angle is $90^\circ-y$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** In the tangent proof, why is $\angle BAC=90^\circ-y$?

**Back:** It is half of $\angle BOC$, where $\angle BOC=180^\circ-2y$.

**Choices:**

- It is half of $\angle BOC$, where $\angle BOC=180^\circ-2y$. ✔
  - _why:_ The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference subtended by the same chord. Halving $180^\circ-2y$ gives $\angle BAC=90^\circ-y$.
- It is half of $\angle BOC$, where $\angle BOC=180^\circ-y$.
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $2$ multiplying $y$ in the central angle. Since $\angle BOC=180^\circ-2y$, halving it correctly gives $\angle BAC=90^\circ-y$.
- It is equal to $\angle BOC$, where $\angle BOC=180^\circ-2y$.
  - _why:_ An angle at the circumference is half, not equal to, the angle at the centre subtended by the same chord. Therefore, half of $180^\circ-2y$ gives $90^\circ-y$.
- It is half of $\angle BOC$, where $\angle BOC=360^\circ-2y$.
  - _why:_ Using $360^\circ$ incorrectly treats the relevant central angle as reflex. The interior central angle is $180^\circ-2y$, whose half is $90^\circ-y$.
