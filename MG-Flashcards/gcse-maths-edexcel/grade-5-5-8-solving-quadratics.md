## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the primary goal when factorising a quadratic expression?

**Back:** To rewrite it as a product of brackets.

**Choices:**

- To rewrite it as a product of brackets. ✔
  - _why:_ Factorising reverses expansion by expressing a quadratic as brackets multiplied together. Expanding those brackets should reproduce every term of the original quadratic expression.
- To rewrite it as a sum of like terms.
  - _why:_ Collecting like terms simplifies an expression by combining matching terms, but it does not factorise it. Factorising rewrites the quadratic as a product of brackets.
- To rewrite it as a single linear term.
  - _why:_ A quadratic cannot generally be converted into one equivalent linear term because its squared term must remain represented. Factorising instead rewrites it as a product of brackets.
- To rewrite it as a quotient of brackets.
  - _why:_ Using a quotient means dividing brackets, which is not the goal of factorising a quadratic. Factorising expresses the original expression as a product of brackets.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$, what relationship must the two chosen numbers have with the constant term $c$?

**Back:** They must multiply to make $c$.

**Choices:**

- They must multiply to make $c$. ✔
  - _why:_ In $(x+m)(x+n)$, the constant term produced by expansion is $mn$. Therefore, the two chosen numbers must multiply to make $c$.
- They must divide to make $c$.
  - _why:_ Division is not used to match the constant term in this factorisation method. The two bracket constants must multiply to give $c$ when the brackets are expanded.
- They must add to make $c$.
  - _why:_ This confuses the two conditions: the chosen numbers add to make the coefficient $b$. They must multiply to make the constant term $c$.
- They must subtract to make $c$.
  - _why:_ Considering the difference may seem useful when signs differ, but it is not the constant-term condition. The two chosen numbers must multiply to make $c$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** When factorising $x^2 + bx + c$, what relationship must the two chosen numbers have with the coefficient $b$?

**Back:** They must add to make $b$.

**Choices:**

- They must add to make $b$. ✔
  - _why:_ In $(x+m)(x+n)$, the middle terms are $mx$ and $nx$, which combine to $(m+n)x$. Therefore, the chosen numbers must add to make $b$.
- They must multiply to make $b$.
  - _why:_ This swaps the two factorisation conditions. The numbers multiply to make the constant term $c$, but they must add to make the coefficient $b$.
- They must subtract to make $b$.
  - _why:_ A difference may appear relevant when the numbers have opposite signs, but the signed numbers are still added. Their sum must equal the coefficient $b$.
- They must divide to make $b$.
  - _why:_ Division does not produce the middle coefficient when two brackets are expanded. The cross terms combine by addition, so the chosen numbers must add to make $b$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the zero product rule state regarding $(x+a)(x+b) = 0$?

**Back:** Either $x+a = 0$ or $x+b = 0$.

**Choices:**

- Either $x+a = 0$ or $x+b = 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ A product equals zero when at least one of its factors equals zero. Setting each bracket equal to zero therefore gives all possible solutions.
- Both $x+a = 0$ and $x+b = 0$.
  - _why:_ Requiring both factors to be zero is too restrictive; only one factor needs to be zero for the product to vanish. Each bracket must be considered separately.
- Either $x+a = 1$ or $x+b = 1$.
  - _why:_ The product is given as zero, so the zero product rule sets each possible factor equal to zero, not one. This produces the roots $x=-a$ and $x=-b$.
- The sum $x+a+x+b = 0$.
  - _why:_ The equation contains a product of factors, not their sum. The zero product rule requires $x+a=0$ or $x+b=0$, giving the two possible roots.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the solutions or roots of a quadratic equation?

**Back:** The values of the variable that make the equation equal to zero.

**Choices:**

- The values of the variable that make the equation equal to zero. ✔
  - _why:_ A root is a variable value that makes the quadratic expression zero. Graphically, these values are the $x$-coordinates where the graph meets the $x$-axis.
- The values of the variable that make the equation equal to one.
  - _why:_ Roots are found by setting the quadratic equal to zero, not one. They identify where the graph meets the $x$-axis because the corresponding $y$-value is zero.
- The values of the variable that make the equation reach its maximum.
  - _why:_ A maximum is the turning point of a downward-opening quadratic and is not generally a root. Roots are the variable values that make the equation equal zero.
- The values of the variable that make the equation cross the y-axis.
  - _why:_ The graph crosses the $y$-axis when the input is zero, which gives the constant term. Roots instead occur where the output is zero, on the $x$-axis.

**Back:** It must be rearranged into the form $x^2 + bx + c = 0$.

**Choices:**

- It must be rearranged into the form $x^2 + bx + c = 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ The zero-product rule can be applied after one side has been made zero. Subtracting $k$ from both sides produces a quadratic expression equal to zero for factorising.
- It must be rearranged into the form $x^2 + bx + c = 1$.
  - _why:_ Factorising an expression equal to one does not allow the zero-product rule. The equation must be rearranged so the quadratic expression equals $0$.
- It must be rearranged into the form $x^2 + bx + c = kx$.
  - _why:_ Moving the constant to a term involving $x$ does not put the equation in the required zero form. All terms must be collected on one side, equal to $0$.
- It must be rearranged into the form $x^2 + bx = 0$.
  - _why:_ The constant term cannot simply be removed unless it cancels through valid rearrangement. The complete quadratic must be collected into the form $x^2+bx+c=0$.
