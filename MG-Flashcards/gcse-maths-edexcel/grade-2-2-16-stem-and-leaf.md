## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** In 47, place value makes 4 worth [ ___ ] and 7 worth [ ___ ].

**Back:** In 47, place value makes 4 worth 40 and 7 worth 7.

**Choices:**

- In 47, place value makes 4 worth 40 and 7 worth 7. ✔
  - _why:_ The 4 occupies the tens column, so its value is 4 tens, or 40. The 7 occupies the ones column, so its value is 7.
- In 47, place value makes 4 worth 4 and 7 worth 70.
  - _why:_ This reverses the place values of the digits. In 47, the 4 is in the tens column and is worth 40, while the 7 is worth 7.
- In 47, place value makes 4 worth 400 and 7 worth 7.
  - _why:_ This places the 4 in the hundreds column rather than the tens column. The number is 47, so the 4 is worth 40 and the 7 is worth 7.
- In 47, place value makes 4 worth 40 and 7 worth 70.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats both digits as tens. Only the 4 is in the tens column and worth 40; the 7 is in the ones column and worth 7.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a stem and leaf diagram, the [ ___ ] is usually all digits except the last, and the [ ___ ] is the last digit.

**Back:** In a stem and leaf diagram, the stem is usually all digits except the last, and the leaf is the last digit.

**Choices:**

- In a stem and leaf diagram, the stem is usually all digits except the last, and the leaf is the last digit. ✔
  - _why:_ The stem contains the leading digit or digits of each value, while the leaf records its final digit. A key shows how these parts combine.
- In a stem and leaf diagram, the leaf is usually all digits except the last, and the stem is the last digit.
  - _why:_ This swaps the two parts of the diagram. The stem contains all digits except the last, while the final digit is recorded as the leaf.
- In a stem and leaf diagram, the key is usually all digits except the last, and the leaf is the last digit.
  - _why:_ The key explains how to interpret an entry; it does not hold the leading digits. Those digits form the stem, while the final digit forms the leaf.
- In a stem and leaf diagram, the stem is usually all digits except the last, and the key is the last digit.
  - _why:_ The final digit of a value is its leaf, not its key. The key is a separate statement explaining how a stem and leaf combine, including any units.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** If the key is 4 | 7 = 47 seconds, what value is 4 | 7?

**Back:** 47 seconds

**Choices:**

- 47 seconds ✔
  - _why:_ The key states that stem 4 followed by leaf 7 represents 47 seconds. Therefore, the entry 4 | 7 has the value 47 seconds.
- 4.7 seconds
  - _why:_ This incorrectly interprets the separator as a decimal point. The given key explicitly defines 4 | 7 as 47 seconds, so the value is 47 seconds.
- 407 seconds
  - _why:_ This mistakenly inserts a zero between the stem and leaf. The key joins 4 and 7 directly, so 4 | 7 represents 47 seconds.
- 74 seconds
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the stem and leaf. The stem comes first and the leaf second, so 4 | 7 represents 47 seconds.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** For 190 cm, what leaf must be recorded?

**Back:** The leaf is 0.

**Choices:**

- The leaf is 0. ✔
  - _why:_ The leaf is the final digit of the value. For 190, the stem is 19 and the last digit, 0, is recorded as the leaf.
- The leaf is 9.
  - _why:_ This takes the tens digit rather than the final digit. In 190, the stem is 19 and the leaf is the last digit, which is 0.
- The leaf is 1.
  - _why:_ This takes the hundreds digit rather than the final digit. The leading digits 19 form the stem, while the final digit 0 is the leaf.
- The leaf is 19.
  - _why:_ This confuses the stem with the leaf. For 190, the leading digits 19 form the stem, and only the final digit 0 forms the leaf.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What must a stem and leaf key include?

**Back:** What one stem and one leaf mean, including units.

**Choices:**

- What one stem and one leaf mean, including units. ✔
  - _why:_ A key must show how one example stem and leaf combine into a complete value, and it must state the units so the data can be interpreted accurately.
- What the largest stem and leaf mean, including units.
  - _why:_ A key does not need to identify the largest entry. It must demonstrate what one stem and one leaf mean together and include the measurement units.
- What one stem and one leaf mean, excluding units.
  - _why:_ Leaving out units makes the values ambiguous, even if the digits are interpreted correctly. A complete key explains one stem-leaf entry and includes the relevant units.
- What all stems and leaves total, including units.
  - _why:_ A key explains the place-value meaning of a single stem-leaf entry; it does not calculate a total. It must also state the units of the data.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** In an [ ___ ] stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from [ ___ ].

**Back:** In an ordered stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from smallest to largest.

**Choices:**

- In an ordered stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from smallest to largest. ✔
  - _why:_ An ordered stem and leaf diagram arranges the leaves in ascending order within each stem row. This makes values, medians and ranges easier to identify.
- In an ordered stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from largest to smallest.
  - _why:_ This uses descending order, but GCSE ordered stem and leaf diagrams conventionally list leaves in ascending order. They should run from smallest to largest in each row.
- In an unordered stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from smallest to largest.
  - _why:_ Leaves arranged from smallest to largest make the diagram ordered, not unordered. An ordered diagram sorts the leaves in ascending order within every stem row.
- In an unordered stem and leaf diagram, leaves in each row go from largest to smallest.
  - _why:_ Neither term matches the required convention: the diagram should be ordered, and its leaves should be written in ascending order from smallest to largest.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What should happen to repeated values in a stem and leaf diagram?

**Back:** They appear as repeated leaves.

**Choices:**

- They appear as repeated leaves. ✔
  - _why:_ Each original data value must be represented separately. Therefore, when a value occurs more than once, its leaf is written once for every occurrence.
- They appear as a single leaf.
  - _why:_ Writing the leaf only once loses information about frequency. A repeated data value must appear as repeated leaves, with one leaf for each occurrence.
- They appear as repeated stems.
  - _why:_ The stem row is not duplicated for each repeated value. Instead, every occurrence is recorded by repeating the appropriate leaf within the same stem row.
- They appear as one leaf and a tally.
  - _why:_ Standard stem and leaf diagrams record frequency by repeating leaves, not by adding tally marks. Each occurrence of the value requires its own leaf.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** In an ordered diagram, rewrite 4 | 7 1 2 correctly.

**Back:** 4 | 1 2 7

**Choices:**

- 4 | 1 2 7 ✔
  - _why:_ An ordered diagram lists leaves from smallest to largest while keeping the stem unchanged. Sorting 7, 1 and 2 gives 1, 2 and 7.
- 4 | 7 2 1
  - _why:_ This arranges the leaves in descending order. An ordered stem and leaf diagram requires ascending order, so the leaves should be written as 1, 2, 7.
- 4 | 1 7 2
  - _why:_ Moving only the smallest leaf to the front does not fully order the row. All leaves must ascend, giving 1, 2, 7 after stem 4.
- 4 | 2 1 7
  - _why:_ The first two leaves remain out of ascending order because 2 comes before 1. Correctly sorting all three leaves gives 4 | 1 2 7.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Why count the leaves after drawing a stem and leaf diagram?

**Back:** To catch missing or extra values.

**Choices:**

- To catch missing or extra values. ✔
  - _why:_ Each data value should produce exactly one leaf, so counting the leaves checks that every value was entered once and reveals omissions or accidental duplicates.
- To check the leaves are in order.
  - _why:_ Ordering is checked by seeing whether leaves increase from left to right within each row. Counting leaves instead detects missing or extra values because each value contributes one leaf.
- To calculate the range of the data.
  - _why:_ The range is calculated by subtracting the smallest value from the largest value. Counting leaves checks for missing or extra values, not the spread of the data.
- To identify the diagram’s key.
  - _why:_ The key explains how stems and leaves represent values and must be written separately. Counting leaves verifies that no data values have been omitted or added.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** If the data has 15 values, how many leaves should the diagram have?

**Back:** 15 leaves

**Choices:**

- 15 leaves ✔
  - _why:_ Every data value is represented by one leaf, so a data set containing 15 values must produce exactly 15 leaves across the complete diagram.
- 14 leaves
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one counting error. Since every one of the 15 data values contributes one leaf, the diagram must contain 15 leaves.
- 16 leaves
  - _why:_ This adds an extra leaf, often by counting a stem as though it were another data value. Only leaves represent individual values, so there should be 15.
- 5 leaves
  - _why:_ This confuses the number of data values with the number of stems or groups. One leaf is needed for each value, giving 15 leaves in total.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Where does 47 go in a stem and leaf diagram using tens stems?

**Back:** Stem 4, leaf 7.

**Choices:**

- Stem 4, leaf 7. ✔
  - _why:_ With tens as stems, the tens digit identifies the stem and the units digit identifies the leaf. Therefore, 47 is represented by stem 4 and leaf 7.
- Stem 7, leaf 4.
  - _why:_ This reverses the place values. In a diagram using tens stems, 4 is the tens digit and must be the stem, while 7 is the leaf.
- Stem 47, leaf 0.
  - _why:_ Using 47 as the stem would treat the whole number as the tens part and lose the required units leaf. The correct placement is stem 4, leaf 7.
- Stem 4, leaf 4.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly places both digits in the tens stem. The stem records only the tens digit, so 47 belongs on stem 4 with leaf 7.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go [ ___ ], [ ___ ].

**Back:** To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go left to right, row by row.

**Choices:**

- To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go left to right, row by row. ✔
  - _why:_ In an ordered diagram, leaves increase from left to right within each stem. After finishing one row, continue to the next row, reading row by row.
- To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go right to left, row by row.
  - _why:_ Reading right to left reverses the ordered leaves within each stem. Values should be read from left to right and then continued row by row.
- To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go left to right, column by column.
  - _why:_ Columns do not form a continuous ordered sequence because each row has a different stem. Read leaves from left to right, completing each row before moving on.
- To read an ordered stem and leaf diagram, go right to left, column by column.
  - _why:_ This reverses each row and incorrectly groups values by columns. An ordered stem and leaf diagram is read left to right, row by row.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** With 10 ordered values, which positions are the two middle values?

**Back:** The 5th and 6th values.

**Choices:**

- The 5th and 6th values. ✔
  - _why:_ An even-sized set has two central values. With 10 ordered values, five lie in the lower half and five in the upper half, making positions 5 and 6 central.
- The 4th and 5th values.
  - _why:_ This pair is one position too early and does not divide the 10 ordered values into equal halves. The central positions are the 5th and 6th values.
- The 6th and 7th values.
  - _why:_ This pair is one position too late and does not sit at the centre of 10 ordered values. The two middle positions are the 5th and 6th.
- The 5th and 5th values.
  - _why:_ This applies the single middle-position rule incorrectly to an even number of values. Ten values have two middle positions: the 5th and the 6th.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** If the two middle values are 25 and 26, what is the median?

**Back:** 25.5

**Choices:**

- 25.5 ✔
  - _why:_ For an even number of data values, the median is the mean of the two middle values. Calculating $(25+26)\div2$ gives 25.5.
- 25
  - _why:_ This selects the lower middle value instead of averaging both middle values. The median is $(25+26)\div2$, which equals 25.5.
- 26
  - _why:_ This selects the upper middle value instead of finding the mean of the pair. Averaging 25 and 26 gives the median of 25.5.
- 51
  - _why:_ This adds the two middle values but forgets to divide their sum by 2. The required mean is $51\div2=25.5$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** The range is the [ ___ ] value minus the [ ___ ] value.

**Back:** The range is the largest value minus the smallest value.

**Choices:**

- The range is the largest value minus the smallest value. ✔
  - _why:_ Range measures the total spread between the two extremes of a data set. Subtracting the smallest value from the largest gives a non-negative difference.
- The range is the smallest value minus the largest value.
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction produces a negative result when the values differ. Range is found by subtracting the smallest value from the largest value.
- The range is the median value minus the smallest value.
  - _why:_ The median describes the centre rather than the full spread, while the smallest value is only one extreme. Range uses the largest value minus the smallest value.
- The range is the largest value minus the mode value.
  - _why:_ The mode is the most frequent value, not an endpoint of the data. To measure the full spread, subtract the smallest value from the largest value.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** If the smallest value is 18 and the largest is 34, what is the range?

**Back:** 16

**Choices:**

- 16 ✔
  - _why:_ The range is the largest value minus the smallest value. Subtracting gives $34-18=16$, so the data spans 16 units.
- 52
  - _why:_ This is an addition error: the extreme values have been added rather than subtracted. The range is $34-18$, which equals 16.
- 24
  - _why:_ This results from subtracting the digits inconsistently rather than subtracting the complete values. Correctly calculating $34-18$ gives a range of 16.
- 17
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one error caused by counting both endpoints as separate steps. Range is the numerical difference $34-18=16$, not 17.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Why might adding a new high score not increase the median?

**Back:** After re-ordering, the middle value may stay the same.

**Choices:**

- After re-ordering, the middle value may stay the same. ✔
  - _why:_ The median depends on the middle position after all scores are ordered, not on the size of the highest score. The value occupying that position can therefore remain unchanged.
- After re-ordering, the new high score may be removed.
  - _why:_ A high score is not automatically removed as an outlier when calculating the median. The median may remain unchanged because the middle value after re-ordering can stay the same.
- After re-ordering, the new score is always ignored.
  - _why:_ Every value, including a newly added one, affects the ordered list and its middle position. However, the middle value may still stay the same, so the median need not increase.
- After re-ordering, the number of middle values stays fixed.
  - _why:_ Adding one value changes whether there is one middle value or two, so the number of middle values does not necessarily stay fixed. The middle value itself may stay unchanged.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** For one-decimal data, the stem is the [ ___ ] and the leaf is the [ ___ ].

**Back:** For one-decimal data, the stem is the whole number and the leaf is the tenths digit.

**Choices:**

- For one-decimal data, the stem is the whole number and the leaf is the tenths digit. ✔
  - _why:_ For values recorded to one decimal place, the whole-number part groups the data as the stem, while the final tenths digit is recorded as the leaf.
- For one-decimal data, the stem is the whole number and the leaf is the units digit.
  - _why:_ The leaf is not the units digit when the key represents one-decimal values. The stem gives the whole number, and the leaf gives the tenths digit.
- For one-decimal data, the stem is the tens digit and the leaf is the tenths digit.
  - _why:_ Using only the tens digit as the stem would lose the units information. The complete whole number is the stem, while the tenths digit is the leaf.
- For one-decimal data, the stem is the tenths digit and the leaf is the whole number.
  - _why:_ This reverses the roles of the two parts. For one-decimal data, the whole number forms the stem and the tenths digit forms the leaf.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** When each value is equally likely, how do you find a feature probability?

**Back:** $$P=\frac{\text{number wanted}}{\text{total number}}$

**Choices:**

- $$P=\frac{\text{number wanted}}{\text{total number}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ For equally likely outcomes, probability is the proportion of all outcomes that satisfy the required feature. Divide the number wanted by the total number of outcomes.
- $$P=\frac{\text{total number}}{\text{number wanted}}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the probability fraction, which can produce values greater than 1. Probability is the number wanted divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes.
- $$P=\frac{\text{number wanted}}{\text{number unwanted}}$
  - _why:_ The denominator must include every equally likely outcome, not only those that are unwanted. The correct calculation divides the number wanted by the total number.
- $$P=\frac{\text{number unwanted}}{\text{total number}}$
  - _why:_ This calculates the probability of not having the feature by counting unwanted outcomes. To find the feature probability, use the number wanted over the total number.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Does “more than 80” include 80?

**Back:** No - it means values above 80.

**Choices:**

- No - it means values above 80. ✔
  - _why:_ The phrase “more than 80” represents the strict inequality $x>80$. Since equality is not allowed, 80 itself is excluded and only larger values qualify.
- Yes - it includes 80 and values above 80.
  - _why:_ Including 80 confuses “more than” with “at least”. More than 80 means $x>80$, so only values strictly above 80 are included.
- Yes - it means values of 80 or above.
  - _why:_ The phrase “80 or above” describes $x\geq80$, not “more than 80”. More than 80 is strict, so 80 is excluded.
- No - it means values below 80.
  - _why:_ Although 80 is correctly excluded, “more than” points to larger values rather than smaller ones. It means values strictly above 80.
