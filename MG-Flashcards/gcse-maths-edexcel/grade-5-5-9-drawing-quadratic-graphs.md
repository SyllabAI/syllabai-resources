## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** When substituting $x = -3$ into $y = x^2$, what is the resulting value of $y$?

**Back:** $$9$

**Choices:**

- $$9$ ✔
  - _why:_ Substituting gives $y = (-3)^2 = (-3) \times (-3) = 9$. The product of two negative numbers is positive.
- $$-9$
  - _why:_ This comes from applying the square only to $3$ and then retaining the negative sign. The whole value $-3$ is squared, so $(-3)^2 = 9$.
- $$6$
  - _why:_ This treats squaring as multiplying the number by $2$. Squaring means multiplying the number by itself, so $(-3) \times (-3) = 9$.
- $$-6$
  - _why:_ This combines doubling with incorrectly retaining the negative sign. The operation is squaring, not doubling, and $(-3)^2$ equals $9$.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** When squaring a negative number, always use [ ___ ] to ensure the result is positive.

**Back:** When squaring a negative number, always use brackets to ensure the result is positive.

**Choices:**

- brackets ✔
  - _why:_ Brackets show that the negative sign is part of the number being squared. For example, $(-3)^2 = (-3) \times (-3) = 9$.
- minus signs
  - _why:_ A minus sign alone does not ensure the entire negative number is squared. Brackets are required: $(-3)^2 = 9$, whereas $-3^2$ is interpreted as $-(3^2)$.
- square roots
  - _why:_ Square roots reverse squaring rather than showing that a negative number is being squared. Brackets enclose the whole negative number, as in $(-3)^2 = 9$.
- decimal points
  - _why:_ Decimal points indicate place value and do not control which parts of an expression are squared. Brackets correctly group the negative number, giving $(-3)^2 = 9$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the specific name for the smooth curve formed by a quadratic graph?

**Back:** Parabola

**Choices:**

- Parabola ✔
  - _why:_ The graph of a quadratic function is called a parabola. It is a smooth, symmetrical curve that may open upwards or downwards.
- Hyperbola
  - _why:_ A hyperbola is associated with relationships such as inverse proportion, not ordinary quadratic functions. The smooth curve formed by a quadratic graph is called a parabola.
- Ellipse
  - _why:_ An ellipse is a closed oval-shaped curve and is not the graph of a quadratic function in the form $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. That graph is a parabola.
- Straight line
  - _why:_ A straight-line graph represents a linear relationship with no $x^2$ term. A quadratic relationship produces a curved graph specifically called a parabola.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic graph opens upwards (like a smile) if the $x^2$ coefficient is [ ___ ].

**Back:** A quadratic graph opens upwards (like a smile) if the $x^2$ coefficient is positive.

**Choices:**

- positive ✔
  - _why:_ When the coefficient of $x^2$ is positive, increasing $|x|$ makes the quadratic term positive, so the parabola opens upwards like a smile.
- negative
  - _why:_ This reverses the relationship between the coefficient’s sign and the graph’s direction. A negative $x^2$ coefficient makes the parabola open downwards; a positive coefficient makes it open upwards.
- zero
  - _why:_ If the $x^2$ coefficient is zero, the expression is not quadratic and does not form a parabola. An upward-opening quadratic requires a positive $x^2$ coefficient.
- squared
  - _why:_ The variable $x$ is squared, but the coefficient itself need not be a square number. Its sign determines direction: a positive coefficient makes the graph open upwards.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic graph opens downwards (like a frown) if the $x^2$ coefficient is [ ___ ].

**Back:** A quadratic graph opens downwards (like a frown) if the $x^2$ coefficient is negative.

**Choices:**

- negative ✔
  - _why:_ A negative coefficient of $x^2$ makes the quadratic term decrease as $|x|$ increases, so the parabola opens downwards like a frown.
- positive
  - _why:_ This reverses the rule governing the parabola’s direction. A positive $x^2$ coefficient produces an upward-opening graph, while a negative coefficient produces a downward-opening graph.
- zero
  - _why:_ A zero $x^2$ coefficient removes the quadratic term, so the graph is no longer a parabola. A downward-opening quadratic must have a negative $x^2$ coefficient.
- squared
  - _why:_ The fact that $x$ is squared does not decide whether the graph opens upwards or downwards. The coefficient must specifically be negative for it to open downwards.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you join the plotted points when drawing a quadratic graph?

**Back:** With one smooth curve (do not use a ruler).

**Choices:**

- With one smooth curve (do not use a ruler). ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic changes gradient continuously, so its plotted points should be connected by one smooth curve. Straight ruler segments would incorrectly introduce sharp changes in gradient.
- With one straight line (use a ruler).
  - _why:_ A straight line represents a linear relationship and cannot show the changing gradient of a quadratic. The points should be joined with one smooth curve without using a ruler.
- With separate unjoined points (do not draw a curve).
  - _why:_ The plotted points are samples from a continuous quadratic relationship, so leaving them unjoined does not show the complete graph. They should be connected with one smooth curve.
- With several sharp corners (join each point exactly).
  - _why:_ Joining points with sharp corners creates piecewise straight sections and sudden gradient changes that a quadratic does not have. The correct graph uses one continuous smooth curve.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the "turning point" of a quadratic graph?

**Back:** The lowest or highest point of the parabola.

**Choices:**

- The lowest or highest point of the parabola. ✔
  - _why:_ The turning point is where the parabola changes direction. It is the minimum point for an upward-opening parabola or the maximum point for a downward-opening parabola.
- The leftmost or rightmost point of the parabola.
  - _why:_ A standard quadratic parabola continues indefinitely to the left and right, so it has no leftmost or rightmost point. Its turning point is its lowest or highest point.
- The first or second crossing of the x-axis.
  - _why:_ Crossings of the x-axis are roots, not turning points. The turning point is where the parabola changes direction and reaches its lowest or highest value.
- The lowest or highest crossing of the y-axis.
  - _why:_ A quadratic crosses the y-axis only once, and that intercept is not generally an extreme value. The turning point is the parabola’s lowest or highest point.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How are the "roots" of a quadratic identified from its graph?

**Back:** They are the $x$-values where the curve crosses the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- They are the $x$-values where the curve crosses the x-axis. ✔
  - _why:_ On the x-axis, $y = 0$, so the corresponding $x$-coordinates solve the quadratic equation. These x-axis intersections are therefore the roots of the quadratic.
- They are the $y$-values where the curve crosses the x-axis.
  - _why:_ Every point on the x-axis has $y = 0$, so the y-values do not distinguish the roots. The roots are the corresponding $x$-values where the curve crosses the x-axis.
- They are the $x$-values where the curve crosses the y-axis.
  - _why:_ The y-axis has $x = 0$, so its intersection gives the y-intercept rather than the roots. Roots are the $x$-values where the curve crosses the x-axis.
- They are the $x$-values where the curve reaches its turning point.
  - _why:_ The turning point identifies the parabola’s maximum or minimum, not generally where $y = 0$. Roots are the $x$-values at which the graph crosses the x-axis.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** At any root on a quadratic graph, what is the value of $y$?

**Back:** $$0$

**Choices:**

- $$0$ ✔
  - _why:_ A root is where the quadratic curve crosses or touches the $x$-axis. Every point on the $x$-axis has a $y$-coordinate of $0$.
- $$1$
  - _why:_ This confuses a root with a point one unit above the $x$-axis. At a root, the curve lies on the $x$-axis, so $y=0$.
- $$-1$
  - _why:_ A negative $y$-value places the point below the $x$-axis rather than at a root. Roots occur where the curve has $y=0$.
- $$x$
  - _why:_ This confuses the variable coordinate with its value at an intercept. A root can have various $x$-coordinates, but its $y$-coordinate is always $0$.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a table of values for a quadratic, the $y$-values usually form a [ ___ ] or a consistent curve.

**Back:** In a table of values for a quadratic, the $y$-values usually form a symmetrical pattern or a consistent curve.

**Choices:**

- symmetrical pattern ✔
  - _why:_ Quadratic graphs are symmetrical about their vertical axis, so equally spaced $x$-values on opposite sides of that axis produce matching $y$-values.
- linear pattern
  - _why:_ A linear pattern has constant first differences and represents a straight line. Quadratic values instead show symmetry and usually have constant second differences.
- random pattern
  - _why:_ Quadratic values are determined by a fixed equation, so they are not random. They form a symmetrical pattern around the graph’s vertical axis.
- alternating pattern
  - _why:_ Alternating values repeatedly switch according to position, which is not a general property of quadratics. Their $y$-values form a symmetrical pattern around the axis.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you solve $x^2 - x - 4 = -2$ using the graph of $y = x^2 - x - 4$?

**Back:** Find the $x$-coordinates where the curve intersects the horizontal line $y = -2$.

**Choices:**

- Find the $x$-coordinates where the curve intersects the horizontal line $y = -2$. ✔
  - _why:_ The curve represents $y=x^2-x-4$, so solutions to the expression equalling $-2$ occur where the curve reaches $y=-2$.
- Find the $x$-coordinates where the curve intersects the horizontal line $y = 0$.
  - _why:_ Using $y=0$ would find the roots of $x^2-x-4=0$. Here the expression equals $-2$, so the required horizontal line is $y=-2$.
- Find the $x$-coordinates where the curve intersects the horizontal line $y = 2$.
  - _why:_ This loses the negative sign on the right-hand side and would solve $x^2-x-4=2$. The given equation requires intersections with $y=-2$.
- Find the $x$-coordinates where the curve intersects the vertical line $x = -2$.
  - _why:_ A vertical line fixes an $x$-value rather than locating where the quadratic has a specified output. Draw the horizontal line $y=-2$ and read the intersection $x$-coordinates.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What must be done to the equation $x^2 = 3x + 4$ before solving it using the roots of a graph?

**Back:** Rearrange it to $x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$ so one side equals zero.

**Choices:**

- Rearrange it to $x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$ so one side equals zero. ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting $3x+4$ from both sides gives $x^2-3x-4=0$. The graph’s roots then provide the solutions because roots occur when $y=0$.
- Rearrange it to $x^2 - 3x + 4 = 0$ so one side equals zero.
  - _why:_ The constant changes incorrectly here. Subtracting $4$ from both sides produces $-4$, so the correct rearrangement is $x^2-3x-4=0$.
- Rearrange it to $x^2 + 3x - 4 = 0$ so one side equals zero.
  - _why:_ Moving $3x$ from the right to the left changes its sign to negative, not positive. The required equation is $x^2-3x-4=0$.
- Rearrange it to $x^2 - 3x - 3 = 0$ so one side equals zero.
  - _why:_ This introduces an off-by-one error in the constant term. Subtracting the original $4$ gives $x^2-3x-4=0$, whose roots solve the equation.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** To solve an equation where the quadratic expression equals a number, you must draw or imagine a [ ___ ] at that $y$-value.

**Back:** To solve an equation where the quadratic expression equals a number, you must draw or imagine a horizontal line at that $y$-value.

**Choices:**

- horizontal line ✔
  - _why:_ A fixed $y$-value is represented by a horizontal line. Its intersections with the quadratic identify the $x$-values for which the expression equals that number.
- vertical line
  - _why:_ A vertical line represents a fixed $x$-value, not a fixed output. To solve where the expression equals a number, use a horizontal line at that $y$-value.
- tangent line
  - _why:_ A tangent only touches the curve locally and is not generally used to represent a specified output. A fixed $y$-value requires a horizontal line.
- diagonal line
  - _why:_ A diagonal line makes $y$ vary with $x$, so it does not represent one fixed value. The correct construction is a horizontal line at the required $y$-value.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** For the graph $y = x^2 - 2x - 1$, calculate $y$ when $x = -1$.

**Back:** $$2$ (calculated as $1 + 2 - 1$)

**Choices:**

- $$2$ (calculated as $1 + 2 - 1$) ✔
  - _why:_ Substituting $x=-1$ gives $(-1)^2=1$ and $-2(-1)=2$. Therefore, $1+2-1=2$.
- $$-2$ (calculated as $1 - 2 - 1$)
  - _why:_ This misses that multiplying two negative values gives a positive value: $-2(-1)=2$, not $-2$. The correct calculation is $1+2-1=2$.
- $$4$ (calculated as $1 + 2 + 1$)
  - _why:_ This changes the final constant from $-1$ to $+1$. Keeping its original sign gives $1+2-1=2$.
- $$-4$ (calculated as $-1 - 2 - 1$)
  - _why:_ This mishandles both $(-1)^2$, which equals $1$, and $-2(-1)$, which equals $2$. The correct result is $1+2-1=2$.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coordinate of the turning point for $y = x^2 - 4x + 3$ if the lowest point is at $x = 2$ and $y = -1$?

**Back:** $$(2, -1)$

**Choices:**

- $$(2, -1)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Coordinates are written in the order $(x,y)$. With the lowest point at $x=2$ and $y=-1$, the turning point is $(2,-1)$.
- $$(-1, 2)$
  - _why:_ This reverses the coordinate order. Since $x=2$ and $y=-1$ at the turning point, the coordinate must be written as $(2,-1)$.
- $$(2, 1)$
  - _why:_ This loses the negative sign of the turning point’s $y$-value. The stated lowest point has $x=2$ and $y=-1$, giving $(2,-1)$.
- $$(-2, -1)$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate. The turning point is stated to occur at $x=2$, so its coordinate is $(2,-1)$.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a quadratic graph has a negative $x^2$ term, the turning point will be the [ ___ ] point on the curve.

**Back:** If a quadratic graph has a negative $x^2$ term, the turning point will be the highest point on the curve.

**Choices:**

- highest ✔
  - _why:_ A negative coefficient of $x^2$ makes the parabola open downwards. Its turning point is therefore a maximum, meaning it is the highest point on the curve.
- lowest
  - _why:_ The lowest turning point occurs when the parabola opens upwards, usually with a positive $x^2$ coefficient. A negative coefficient makes the turning point the highest point.
- leftmost
  - _why:_ The sign of the $x^2$ coefficient controls whether the curve opens upwards or downwards, not its horizontal extreme. A negative coefficient gives a highest point.
- rightmost
  - _why:_ A standard vertical parabola continues indefinitely in horizontal directions, so its turning point is not a rightmost point. With a negative $x^2$ coefficient, it is the highest point.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** In the coordinate $(x, y)$, which direction does the $x$ value represent?

**Back:** The horizontal position (across).

**Choices:**

- The horizontal position (across). ✔
  - _why:_ The $x$-coordinate shows how far left or right a point lies from the origin, so it represents the horizontal position.
- The vertical position (upwards).
  - _why:_ This confuses the two coordinate axes. The $y$-coordinate gives the vertical position, while the $x$-coordinate gives the horizontal position across the graph.
- The diagonal position (across).
  - _why:_ Coordinates do not directly specify a diagonal position. The $x$ value represents horizontal movement across the graph, while the $y$ value represents vertical movement.
- The vertical position (downwards).
  - _why:_ The direction of the $y$-axis has been confused with the role of the $x$-axis. The $x$ value represents the horizontal position across the graph.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a value in your table of values looks wildly out of place compared to the curve, you should [ ___ ] it.

**Back:** If a value in your table of values looks wildly out of place compared to the curve, you should recalculate it.

**Choices:**

- recalculate ✔
  - _why:_ An isolated value that does not follow the curve may result from an arithmetic or substitution error, so calculating it again checks and corrects the result.
- estimate
  - _why:_ Replacing the value with an estimate could hide the original calculation error. You should recalculate it carefully using the corresponding $x$ value and the quadratic equation.
- ignore
  - _why:_ Ignoring an unusual result may leave an incorrect point in the table or graph. You should recalculate it to check for a substitution or arithmetic error.
- plot
  - _why:_ Plotting the suspicious value without checking it may distort the quadratic curve. You should recalculate it first to confirm whether the table entry is accurate.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the two solutions to $x^2 - x - 4 = 2$ if the graph crosses $y = 2$ at $x = -2$ and $x = 3$?

**Back:** $x = -2$$x = 3$

**Choices:**

- $x = -2$$x = 3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Solutions at a specified $y$ value are the $x$-coordinates of the intersections. The graph meets $y=2$ at $x=-2$ and $x=3$.
- $x = -3$$x = 2$
  - _why:_ This reverses the signs associated with the two factors. Solving $x^2-x-6=0$ gives $(x-3)(x+2)=0$, so $x=-2$ and $x=3$.
- $x = -2$$x = 2$
  - _why:_ This assumes the intersections are symmetrical about the $y$-axis. The quadratic’s axis of symmetry is $x=0.5$, so the solutions are $x=-2$ and $x=3$.
- $x = 2$$x = 3$
  - _why:_ This mistakes the horizontal line’s value, $y=2$, for one of the $x$-coordinates. The actual intersection coordinates give $x=-2$ and $x=3$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $y = 7x - x^2$ a "frown" shaped graph?

**Back:** The coefficient of the $x^2$ term is negative.

**Choices:**

- The coefficient of the $x^2$ term is negative. ✔
  - _why:_ A negative coefficient of $x^2$ makes a quadratic open downwards, producing an upside-down U or “frown” shape with a maximum point.
- The coefficient of the $x^2$ term is positive.
  - _why:_ A positive coefficient would make the parabola open upwards in a “smile” shape. Here the coefficient is $-1$, so the graph opens downwards.
- The coefficient of the $x^2$ term is zero.
  - _why:_ A zero coefficient would remove the quadratic term entirely, producing a straight-line equation. The coefficient is $-1$, which makes the parabola open downwards.
- The coefficient of the $x^2$ term is 7.
  - _why:_ This confuses the coefficient of $x$ with the coefficient of $x^2$. The quadratic coefficient is $-1$, and its negative sign creates the frown shape.
