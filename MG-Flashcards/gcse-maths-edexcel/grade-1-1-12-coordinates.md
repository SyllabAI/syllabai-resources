## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a coordinate grid, which axis is the horizontal line?

**Back:** x-axis

**Choices:**

- x-axis ✔
  - _why:_ The x-axis runs horizontally across a coordinate grid. Values of x increase to the right and decrease to the left.
- y-axis
  - _why:_ The y-axis is the vertical line, not the horizontal one. The horizontal line is the x-axis, showing movement left or right.
- origin
  - _why:_ The origin is the single point where the two axes intersect. The horizontal line through that point is the x-axis.
- line y = x
  - _why:_ The line $y = x$ is diagonal because both coordinates change together. The horizontal axis of a coordinate grid is the x-axis.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In a coordinate grid, which axis is the vertical line?

**Back:** y-axis

**Choices:**

- y-axis ✔
  - _why:_ The y-axis runs vertically through a coordinate grid. Values of y increase upwards and decrease downwards.
- x-axis
  - _why:_ The x-axis is the horizontal line, not the vertical one. The vertical line is the y-axis, showing movement up or down.
- origin
  - _why:_ The origin is a point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect, rather than an axis itself. The vertical line is the y-axis.
- line y = x
  - _why:_ The line $y = x$ slopes diagonally rather than running vertically. The y-axis is the vertical line on a coordinate grid.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard order for reading or writing a coordinate pair?

**Back:** Across (x) first, then up/down (y).

**Choices:**

- Across (x) first, then up/down (y). ✔
  - _why:_ A coordinate pair is written $(x,y)$, so the horizontal x-coordinate is read first and the vertical y-coordinate is read second.
- Up/down (y) first, then across (x).
  - _why:_ This reverses the standard coordinate order. Coordinates are written $(x,y)$, so move across using x first, then up or down using y.
- Across (y) first, then up/down (x).
  - _why:_ This swaps the meanings of x and y. The x-coordinate describes movement across, while the y-coordinate describes movement up or down.
- Up/down (x) first, then across (y).
  - _why:_ This assigns each direction to the wrong axis. Movement across is controlled by x and comes first; vertical movement is controlled by y.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What direction does a negative x-coordinate indicate?

**Back:** Move left from the origin.

**Choices:**

- Move left from the origin. ✔
  - _why:_ Negative x-values lie to the left of the origin because x-values decrease as you move left along the horizontal axis.
- Move right from the origin.
  - _why:_ Moving right produces a positive x-coordinate, not a negative one. A negative x-coordinate means moving left from the origin.
- Move up from the origin.
  - _why:_ Moving up changes the y-coordinate rather than the x-coordinate. A negative x-coordinate requires horizontal movement to the left from the origin.
- Move down from the origin.
  - _why:_ Moving down represents a negative y-coordinate, not a negative x-coordinate. Negative x-values are reached by moving left from the origin.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What direction does a negative y-coordinate indicate?

**Back:** Move down from the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- Move down from the x-axis. ✔
  - _why:_ Negative y-values lie below the x-axis because y-values decrease as you move downwards on a coordinate grid.
- Move up from the x-axis.
  - _why:_ Moving up from the x-axis produces a positive y-coordinate. A negative y-coordinate is found by moving down from the x-axis.
- Move left from the y-axis.
  - _why:_ Moving left changes the x-coordinate rather than the y-coordinate. A negative y-coordinate requires downward movement from the x-axis.
- Move right from the y-axis.
  - _why:_ Moving right changes the x-coordinate and usually represents a positive x-value. Negative y-values are reached by moving down from the x-axis.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** To plot the point $(-1, 3)$, you start at the origin and move [ ___ ] and then 3 squares up.

**Back:** To plot the point $(-1, 3)$, you start at the origin and move 1 square left and then 3 squares up.

**Choices:**

- 1 square left ✔
  - _why:_ The first coordinate is $-1$, so move one square in the negative x-direction, which is left, before using the y-coordinate.
- 1 square right
  - _why:_ Moving right treats $-1$ as positive. The negative first coordinate means moving 1 square left before moving 3 squares up.
- 3 squares left
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the y-coordinate, 3, for the horizontal movement. The x-coordinate is $-1$, so the correct move is 1 square left.
- 1 square down
  - _why:_ Moving down changes the y-coordinate rather than the x-coordinate. The first coordinate $-1$ requires a move of 1 square left.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** To plot the point $(-1, 3)$, you start at the origin and move 1 square left and then [ ___ ].

**Back:** To plot the point $(-1, 3)$, you start at the origin and move 1 square left and then 3 squares up.

**Choices:**

- 3 squares up ✔
  - _why:_ The second coordinate is positive 3, so after the horizontal movement you move three squares upwards in the positive y-direction.
- 3 squares down
  - _why:_ Moving down would represent a y-coordinate of $-3$. Since the second coordinate is positive 3, the correct movement is 3 squares up.
- 1 square up
  - _why:_ This mistakenly reuses the magnitude of the x-coordinate. The y-coordinate is 3, so the vertical movement must be 3 squares up.
- 3 squares right
  - _why:_ Moving right changes the x-coordinate instead of the y-coordinate. The second coordinate is positive 3, requiring a vertical move of 3 squares up.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the midpoint of a line segment?

**Back:** The point exactly halfway between two endpoints.

**Choices:**

- The point exactly halfway between two endpoints. ✔
  - _why:_ A midpoint divides a line segment into two equal lengths, so it is exactly halfway between the segment’s two endpoints.
- The point one quarter of the way between two endpoints.
  - _why:_ A point one quarter of the way does not divide the segment equally. The midpoint is exactly halfway between the two endpoints.
- The point where the segment crosses the x-axis.
  - _why:_ Crossing the x-axis is unrelated to being a midpoint and may not occur at all. The midpoint is exactly halfway between the endpoints.
- The point at either end of the line segment.
  - _why:_ An endpoint marks a boundary of the segment rather than its centre. The midpoint lies exactly halfway between the two endpoints.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the midpoint of two coordinates?

**Back:** Average the x-coordinates.Average the y-coordinates.

**Choices:**

- Average the x-coordinates.Average the y-coordinates. ✔
  - _why:_ The midpoint lies halfway between the points in both directions, so calculate the mean of the two x-coordinates and separately calculate the mean of the two y-coordinates.
- Add the x-coordinates.Add the y-coordinates.
  - _why:_ Adding gives the coordinate totals but omits division by 2. The midpoint is found by averaging each pair, so add the x-coordinates and divide by 2, then repeat for y.
- Subtract the x-coordinates.Subtract the y-coordinates.
  - _why:_ Subtracting coordinates finds the change between the points, not their midpoint. Average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates to locate the point halfway between them.
- Average the x-coordinates.Add the y-coordinates.
  - _why:_ Only the x-coordinate has been averaged here. Both components must be treated alike: average the x-coordinates and also average the y-coordinates to obtain the midpoint.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the middle of two coordinates, you [ ___ ] the values together and divide by 2.

**Back:** To find the middle of two coordinates, you add the values together and divide by 2.

**Choices:**

- add ✔
  - _why:_ The mean of two coordinate values is their sum divided by 2. Therefore, the first step in finding the middle value is to add the two values.
- subtract
  - _why:_ Subtracting finds the difference or displacement between two coordinate values, not their middle. To find the midpoint, add the values together and then divide the result by 2.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplying the coordinate values does not calculate their mean. The midpoint coordinate is found by adding the two corresponding values and dividing their sum by 2.
- square
  - _why:_ Squaring is used in calculations such as distances, but it does not find a middle coordinate. Add the corresponding coordinate values together before dividing by 2.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the middle of two coordinates, you add the values together and [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find the middle of two coordinates, you add the values together and divide by 2.

**Choices:**

- divide by 2 ✔
  - _why:_ There are two coordinate values being averaged, so their sum must be divided by 2. This gives the coordinate exactly halfway between the original values.
- multiply by 2
  - _why:_ Multiplying the sum by 2 makes it larger rather than finding its mean. Because two values are being averaged, their sum must be divided by 2.
- divide by 4
  - _why:_ Dividing by 4 would be appropriate when averaging four values, not two. A midpoint uses two corresponding coordinates, so their sum is divided by 2.
- subtract 2
  - _why:_ Subtracting 2 is not the same as calculating an average and produces no consistent midpoint. Add the two corresponding coordinate values, then divide their sum by 2.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Calculate the midpoint of $B(-3, 3)$ and $C(3, 0)$.

**Back:** $$(0, 1.5)$

**Choices:**

- $$(0, 1.5)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Averaging the x-coordinates gives $(-3+3)/2=0$, while averaging the y-coordinates gives $(3+0)/2=1.5$. Therefore, the midpoint is $(0, 1.5)$.
- $$(0, 3)$
  - _why:_ The x-coordinate has been averaged correctly, but the y-values were only added. Dividing their sum by 2 gives $(3+0)/2=1.5$, so the midpoint is $(0, 1.5)$.
- $$(3, 1.5)$
  - _why:_ Treating $-3$ as $3$ loses its negative sign and gives the wrong x-coordinate. The correct average is $(-3+3)/2=0$, so the midpoint is $(0, 1.5)$.
- $$(0, -1.5)$
  - _why:_ The negative y-value results from subtracting the y-coordinates instead of adding them. Their average is $(3+0)/2=1.5$, giving the midpoint $(0, 1.5)$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the orientation of a line with an equation like $x = 3$?

**Back:** Vertical

**Choices:**

- Vertical ✔
  - _why:_ The equation fixes the x-coordinate at 3 while the y-coordinate can take any value. The resulting points form a vertical line through $x=3$.
- Horizontal
  - _why:_ This confuses equations of the forms $x=a$ and $y=b$. A fixed x-coordinate produces a vertical line; a fixed y-coordinate produces a horizontal line.
- Diagonal
  - _why:_ A diagonal line requires both coordinates to vary according to a relationship. In $x=3$, the x-coordinate remains fixed, so the line is vertical.
- Curved
  - _why:_ An equation fixing one coordinate at a constant produces a straight line, not a curve. Since $x$ is always 3, the line is vertical.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the orientation of a line with an equation like $y = 6$?

**Back:** Horizontal

**Choices:**

- Horizontal ✔
  - _why:_ The equation fixes the y-coordinate at 6 while the x-coordinate can take any value. These points form a horizontal line through $y=6$.
- Vertical
  - _why:_ This reverses the roles of x and y. A fixed y-coordinate produces a horizontal line, whereas a fixed x-coordinate produces a vertical line.
- Diagonal
  - _why:_ A diagonal line requires the y-coordinate to change as the x-coordinate changes. Here y remains fixed at 6, so the line is horizontal.
- Curved
  - _why:_ A constant y-coordinate creates a straight line rather than a curve. Because every point has $y=6$, the line runs horizontally across the coordinate grid.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** Every point on the line $y = 4$ has a [ ___ ] of 4.

**Back:** Every point on the line $y = 4$ has a y-coordinate of 4.

**Choices:**

- y-coordinate ✔
  - _why:_ In an equation of the form $y=a$, every point has the same y-coordinate, $a$. Therefore, all points on $y=4$ have a y-coordinate of 4.
- x-coordinate
  - _why:_ The x-coordinate is free to vary along this line, so it is not always 4. The equation fixes the y-coordinate at 4, creating a horizontal line.
- gradient
  - _why:_ A horizontal line has gradient 0, not 4. The number 4 in $y=4$ specifies the y-coordinate shared by every point on the line.
- distance from y-axis
  - _why:_ Distance from the y-axis depends on the magnitude of the x-coordinate, which varies along the line. It is the y-coordinate that remains fixed at 4.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** Every point on the line $y = 4$ has a y-coordinate of [ ___ ].

**Back:** Every point on the line $y = 4$ has a y-coordinate of 4.

**Choices:**

- 4 ✔
  - _why:_ The equation $y=4$ directly states that the y-coordinate of every point is 4. The x-coordinate may vary, producing a horizontal line.
- 0
  - _why:_ A y-coordinate of 0 would place points on the x-axis, whose equation is $y=0$. On the given line, every y-coordinate is 4.
- -4
  - _why:_ The negative sign would describe the different horizontal line $y=-4$, below the x-axis. The stated equation fixes every y-coordinate at positive 4.
- 8
  - _why:_ Doubling the constant changes the line to $y=8$ and is not part of interpreting the equation. Every point on $y=4$ has y-coordinate 4.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you draw the line $x = -2$?

**Back:** Draw a vertical line through -2 on the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- Draw a vertical line through -2 on the x-axis. ✔
  - _why:_ An equation of the form $x = a$ fixes the x-coordinate while the y-coordinate can vary, producing a vertical line through $a$ on the x-axis.
- Draw a vertical line through 2 on the x-axis.
  - _why:_ Using $2$ loses the negative sign and gives the line $x = 2$. The required line is vertical and crosses the x-axis at $-2$.
- Draw a horizontal line through -2 on the y-axis.
  - _why:_ A horizontal line represents a constant y-coordinate, not a constant x-coordinate. Since $x$ is fixed at $-2$, draw a vertical line through $-2$ on the x-axis.
- Draw a horizontal line through 2 on the y-axis.
  - _why:_ This confuses $x = -2$ with $y = 2$ and also loses the negative sign. The correct graph is vertical through $-2$ on the x-axis.

**Choices:**

- Draw a horizontal line through 4 on the y-axis. ✔

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** In a square $ABCD$, if the move from $B$ to $C$ is 1 left and 4 down, how do you find corner $D$?

**Back:** Move 1 left and 4 down starting from point A.

**Choices:**

- Move 1 left and 4 down starting from point A. ✔
  - _why:_ Opposite sides of a square are equal and parallel, so the movement from $A$ to $D$ matches the movement from $B$ to $C$: 1 left and 4 down.
- Move 1 left and 4 down starting from point B.
  - _why:_ Starting from $B$ and repeating the movement locates $C$, not $D$. To find $D$, apply the same movement of 1 left and 4 down from point $A$.
- Move 1 right and 4 down starting from point A.
  - _why:_ This reverses the horizontal direction and therefore does not make $AD$ equal and parallel to $BC$. From $A$, move 1 left and 4 down.
- Move 1 left and 4 up starting from point A.
  - _why:_ This reverses the vertical direction, using an upward movement instead of copying the vector from $B$ to $C$. From $A$, move 1 left and 4 down.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the midpoint of $(4, -2)$ and $(4, 6)$?

**Back:** $$(4, 2)$

**Choices:**

- $$(4, 2)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Average the coordinates separately: the x-coordinate remains $(4+4)/2=4$, while the y-coordinate is $(-2+6)/2=2$. Therefore the midpoint is $(4,2)$.
- $$(4, 4)$
  - _why:_ The value $4$ comes from adding the y-coordinates without dividing by $2$. Their mean is $(-2+6)/2=2$, while the mean x-coordinate is $4$.
- $$(0, 2)$
  - _why:_ This treats the common x-coordinate as though opposite values cancel. Both x-coordinates are $4$, so their mean is $4$; the mean y-coordinate is $2$.
- $$(8, 4)$
  - _why:_ This adds each pair of coordinates but does not divide by $2$. A midpoint uses coordinate averages, giving $((4+4)/2,(-2+6)/2)=(4,2)$.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** A [ ___ ] is a straight line between two endpoints.

**Back:** A line segment is a straight line between two endpoints.

**Choices:**

- line segment ✔
  - _why:_ A line segment is the finite part of a line bounded by two endpoints. This distinguishes it from a line, which continues indefinitely in both directions.
- ray
  - _why:_ A ray has exactly one endpoint and continues indefinitely in one direction. A finite straight path bounded by two endpoints is called a line segment.
- line
  - _why:_ A line extends indefinitely in both directions and therefore has no endpoints. The part bounded by two endpoints is specifically called a line segment.
- perpendicular bisector
  - _why:_ A perpendicular bisector crosses a segment at its midpoint at a right angle; it is not the finite straight path between two endpoints. That path is a line segment.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** If point A is 4 squares right and 2 squares below the origin, what are its coordinates?

**Back:** $$(4, -2)$

**Choices:**

- $$(4, -2)$ ✔
  - _why:_ Moving 4 squares right makes the x-coordinate positive $4$, while moving 2 squares below the origin makes the y-coordinate negative $-2$.
- $$(4, 2)$
  - _why:_ This makes the vertical coordinate positive, as if the point were above the origin. Moving below the origin gives a negative y-coordinate, so the coordinates are $(4,-2)$.
- $$(-4, -2)$
  - _why:_ This makes the horizontal coordinate negative, as if the point were left of the origin. Moving right gives positive $4$, so the coordinates are $(4,-2)$.
- $$(-2, 4)$
  - _why:_ This swaps the horizontal and vertical movements. Coordinates are written as $(x,y)$, so 4 right gives $x=4$ and 2 below gives $y=-2$.
