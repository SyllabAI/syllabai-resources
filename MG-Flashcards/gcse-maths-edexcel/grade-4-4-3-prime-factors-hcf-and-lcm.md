## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** $$42 \div 6$ gives 7 exactly. What does this show about 6?

**Back:** 6 is a factor of 42.

**Choices:**

- 6 is a factor of 42. ✔
  - _why:_ A factor divides a number exactly with no remainder. Since $42 \div 6 = 7$, the number 6 is therefore a factor of 42.
- 6 is a multiple of 42.
  - _why:_ This reverses the factor-multiple relationship. Since $42 \div 6$ is an integer, 6 is a factor of 42, while 42 is a multiple of 6.
- 42 is a factor of 6.
  - _why:_ This reverses the direction of divisibility: 42 does not divide 6 exactly. The calculation shows that 6 divides 42 exactly, so 6 is a factor of 42.
- 7 is a factor of 6.
  - _why:_ The quotient 7 is not automatically a factor of the divisor 6. The exact division $42 \div 6 = 7$ shows that 6 is a factor of 42.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is 1 not a prime number?

**Back:** 1 has only one factor.

**Choices:**

- 1 has only one factor. ✔
  - _why:_ A prime number must have exactly two distinct positive factors: 1 and itself. For the number 1 these are the same factor, so it has only one.
- 1 has exactly two factors.
  - _why:_ Counting 1 twice as both 1 and itself is a common mistake. Distinct factors are counted only once, so 1 has one factor and is not prime.
- 1 has no positive factors.
  - _why:_ The number 1 divides itself exactly, so it does have a positive factor. It is not prime because it has only one distinct factor, rather than exactly two.
- 1 is an even number.
  - _why:_ Evenness is determined by divisibility by 2, and 1 is odd. Its actual reason for not being prime is that it has only one distinct positive factor.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** A prime number has exactly [ ___ ] factors: 1 and [ ___ ].

**Back:** A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.

**Choices:**

- A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. ✔
  - _why:_ By definition, a prime number has exactly two distinct positive factors. These are always 1 and the prime number itself.
- A prime number has exactly one factors: 1 and itself.
  - _why:_ Treating 1 and the number itself as one factor confuses prime numbers with the number 1. A prime has two distinct factors: 1 and itself.
- A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and 2.
  - _why:_ Only the prime number 2 has 2 as its second factor. In general, every prime has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.
- A prime number has exactly three factors: 1 and itself.
  - _why:_ Having three factors does not satisfy the definition of a prime number. A prime has exactly two distinct positive factors: 1 and itself.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** A prime factorisation should never include [ ___ ].

**Back:** A prime factorisation should never include 1.

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ The number 1 is not prime, so it cannot appear as a prime factor. Including it also adds no useful information because multiplying by 1 changes nothing.
- 0
  - _why:_ Although 0 should not appear in a prime factorisation, it is not the intended defining exclusion here. Prime factorisations never include 1 because 1 is not a prime number.
- 2
  - _why:_ The number 2 is the smallest prime and is included whenever it divides the original number. It is 1 that must never appear because 1 is not prime.
- 3
  - _why:_ The number 3 is a valid prime factor and should be included when it divides the number. A prime factorisation never includes 1 because 1 is not prime.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What does $2^3$ mean in index notation?

**Back:** $2 \times 2 \times 2$

**Choices:**

- $2 \times 2 \times 2$ ✔
  - _why:_ An exponent tells you how many times the base is used as a factor. Therefore, $2^3$ means multiplying three factors of 2 together.
- $2 \times 3$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the base by the exponent is a common index error. The exponent 3 means use 2 as a factor three times, giving $2 \times 2 \times 2$.
- $3 \times 3 \times 3$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the exponent as the repeated factor. In $2^3$, the base 2 is repeated three times, so it means $2 \times 2 \times 2$.
- $2 + 2 + 2$
  - _why:_ An index represents repeated multiplication, not repeated addition. Since the base is 2 and the exponent is 3, the expression means $2 \times 2 \times 2$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Write 84 as a product of prime factors.

**Back:** $84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$

**Choices:**

- $84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing 84 gives $84 = 2 \times 42 = 2 \times 2 \times 21 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$, and every factor on the right is prime.
- $84 = 2 \times 3 \times 7$
  - _why:_ One factor of 2 has been omitted, so the right-hand side equals 42 rather than 84. The complete factorisation is $84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$.
- $84 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 7$
  - _why:_ The exponent on 3 has been increased incorrectly, making the product three times too large. There is only one factor of 3, so the correct power is $3^1$.
- $84 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 7$
  - _why:_ This includes one extra factor of 2, so the product is 168 rather than 84. There are exactly two factors of 2, giving $2^2 \times 3 \times 7$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** For HCF using prime factors, which primes do you keep?

**Back:** Primes that appear in every number.

**Choices:**

- Primes that appear in every number. ✔
  - _why:_ The HCF must divide every number, so only prime factors shared by all the numbers can be included. Any unshared prime would prevent the result dividing at least one number.
- Primes that appear in any number.
  - _why:_ Keeping every prime that appears in at least one number is the method associated with finding the LCM. For the HCF, retain only primes appearing in every number.
- Primes that appear in one number.
  - _why:_ A prime found in only one number cannot be part of a common factor. For the HCF, keep primes that occur in every number being considered.
- Primes that are below every number.
  - _why:_ A prime being smaller than each number does not mean it divides them. The HCF uses only prime factors that appear in every number’s prime factorisation.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** For HCF, choose the [ ___ ] power of each shared prime.

**Back:** For HCF, choose the smallest power of each shared prime.

**Choices:**

- smallest ✔
  - _why:_ A common factor cannot contain a prime more times than any number being compared. Therefore, the HCF uses the smallest exponent of each shared prime.
- largest
  - _why:_ Choosing the largest power is the rule used for the LCM, not the HCF. The HCF must divide every number, so it uses the smallest shared power.
- total
  - _why:_ Adding the powers would introduce too many copies of the prime and produce a result that may not divide every number. The HCF uses the smallest shared power.
- average
  - _why:_ Averaging exponents is not part of finding an HCF and could even produce a non-integer exponent. The HCF takes the smallest power of each shared prime.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Given $54 = 2 \times 3^3$ and $126 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7$, find the HCF.

**Back:** $18$

**Choices:**

- $18$ ✔
  - _why:_ For the HCF, use each common prime at its smallest power: $2^1 \times 3^2 = 2 \times 9 = 18$.
- $6$
  - _why:_ This uses only one factor of $3$ instead of the shared $3^2$. Taking the smallest common powers gives $2 \times 3^2 = 18$.
- $9$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor $2$, even though both numbers contain it. The HCF includes $2^1$ and $3^2$, giving $18$.
- $54$
  - _why:_ This copies the first number, but $3^3$ is not a factor of $126$. The smallest shared powers are $2^1$ and $3^2$, giving $18$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** For LCM using prime factors, which primes do you include?

**Back:** Every prime that appears.

**Choices:**

- Every prime that appears. ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM must be divisible by every original number, so it includes every prime appearing in any factorisation, using the greatest required power of each.
- Every prime shared by all.
  - _why:_ Including only shared primes is the method for finding an HCF. An LCM includes every prime that appears in any of the numbers.
- Every prime in the first number.
  - _why:_ Restricting the selection to the first number can omit factors needed for divisibility by the others. The LCM includes every prime that appears.
- Only the largest prime present.
  - _why:_ The numerical size of a prime does not determine whether it is included. The LCM requires every prime appearing in any of the factorisations.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** For LCM, choose the [ ___ ] power of each prime.

**Back:** For LCM, choose the largest power of each prime.

**Choices:**

- largest ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM must contain enough of each prime to be divisible by every number, so the largest power of each prime is selected.
- smallest
  - _why:_ Choosing the smallest power is the HCF method because it keeps only factors common to every number. For an LCM, choose the largest power.
- first
  - _why:_ The first power would include each prime only once, which may not provide enough factors. The LCM uses the largest power shown.
- common
  - _why:_ Restricting the choice to a common power confuses LCM with HCF. For the LCM, select the largest power of every prime that appears.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Given $45 = 3^2 \times 5$ and $60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5$, find the LCM.

**Back:** $180$

**Choices:**

- $180$ ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM uses the largest powers present: $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 4 \times 9 \times 5 = 180$.
- $15$
  - _why:_ This is the HCF, found from the shared factors $3 \times 5$. The LCM uses the greatest powers of all primes, giving $180$.
- $60$
  - _why:_ This keeps only $3^1$, so the result is not divisible by $45$, which contains $3^2$. Using $3^2$ gives an LCM of $180$.
- $900$
  - _why:_ This is obtained by multiplying $45$ and $60$ and then dividing incorrectly. Their shared factors must not be duplicated, so the LCM is $180$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Two regular events repeat at fixed intervals. What calculation gives the next time together?

**Back:** Find the LCM of the intervals.

**Choices:**

- Find the LCM of the intervals. ✔
  - _why:_ Each event occurs at multiples of its interval. The first positive time that is a multiple of both intervals is their lowest common multiple.
- Find the HCF of the intervals.
  - _why:_ The HCF measures the greatest interval that divides both values; it does not give their next shared occurrence. That time is found using the LCM.
- Add the two intervals together.
  - _why:_ Adding the intervals does not generally produce a multiple of each one. The next time both events occur together is given by their LCM.
- Multiply the two intervals together.
  - _why:_ The product is a common multiple but may not be the first one, especially when the intervals share factors. The next meeting uses the LCM.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** Before using LCM in a time question, put all intervals in [ ___ ].

**Back:** Before using LCM in a time question, put all intervals in the same units.

**Choices:**

- the same units ✔
  - _why:_ LCM compares whole-number intervals directly, so all times must first be expressed in one unit, such as converting hours and minutes entirely into minutes.
- different units
  - _why:_ Applying LCM to mixed units compares unlike quantities and gives an invalid result. Convert every interval into the same units before calculating the LCM.
- the largest units
  - _why:_ Choosing the largest unit can introduce fractions and is not the requirement. All intervals must be converted into the same units before finding the LCM.
- the smallest units
  - _why:_ Small units are often convenient, but they are not always necessary. The essential requirement is that every interval is expressed in the same units.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** A tram and coach both arrive at 10:00, then every 9 and 12 minutes. When next together?

**Back:** 10:36

**Choices:**

- 10:36 ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM of $9$ and $12$ is $36$, so both services return together $36$ minutes after 10:00, which is 10:36.
- 10:21
  - _why:_ This adds the two intervals, but $21$ is not a multiple of either $9$ or $12$. Their LCM is $36$, giving 10:36.
- 10:12
  - _why:_ This uses only the coach's interval. After $12$ minutes the tram is not due, since $12$ is not a multiple of $9$; both return at 10:36.
- 11:48
  - _why:_ This multiplies the intervals to get $108$ minutes, which is a shared return time but not the next one. The LCM is $36$ minutes, giving 10:36.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** For $P = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$ and $Q = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$, find the HCF.

**Back:** $12$

**Choices:**

- $12$ ✔
  - _why:_ The common primes are $2$ and $3$. Taking their smallest powers gives $2^2 \times 3^1 = 4 \times 3 = 12$.
- $6$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly reduces the shared power of $2$ from $2^2$ to $2^1$. The smallest powers actually present are $2^2$ and $3^1$, giving $12$.
- $36$
  - _why:_ This uses $3^2$ from $P$, but $Q$ contains only $3^1$, so $36$ cannot divide $Q$. Using the smaller powers gives $12$.
- $84$
  - _why:_ This includes the factor $7$, which appears only in $Q$ and is therefore not common. The shared prime powers produce an HCF of $12$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** For $P = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$ and $Q = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$, find the LCM.

**Back:** $2520$

**Choices:**

- $2520$ ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM uses the highest power of each prime present: $2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 = 2520$.
- $1260$
  - _why:_ Using $2^2$ instead of the higher power $2^3$ misses a factor of $2$. The LCM is $2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 = 2520$.
- $7560$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by an unnecessary extra factor of $3$ makes the result three times too large. Using each prime’s highest power gives an LCM of $2520$.
- $504$
  - _why:_ Omitting the factor $5$ gives $504$, but the LCM must contain every prime factor needed by either number. Including $5$ gives $2520$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Given $12 = 2^2 \times 3$, $18 = 2 \times 3^2$, and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, find the HCF.

**Back:** $6$

**Choices:**

- $6$ ✔
  - _why:_ The common prime factors with the lowest powers are $2^1$ and $3^1$. Therefore, the HCF is $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- $2$
  - _why:_ This includes the common factor $2$ but overlooks that all three numbers are also divisible by $3$. Their HCF is therefore $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- $3$
  - _why:_ This includes the common factor $3$ but overlooks that all three numbers are also divisible by $2$. Their HCF is therefore $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- $12$
  - _why:_ Taking the highest powers resembles the LCM method and makes the result too large; $12$ does not divide $18$ or $30$. The HCF is $6$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Given $12 = 2^2 \times 3$, $18 = 2 \times 3^2$, and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, find the LCM.

**Back:** $180$

**Choices:**

- $180$ ✔
  - _why:_ The LCM uses the highest power of every prime present: $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$, which is divisible by all three numbers.
- $60$
  - _why:_ Using only one factor of $3$ overlooks the $3^2$ in $18$, so $60$ is not divisible by $18$. The correct LCM is $180$.
- $90$
  - _why:_ Using only one factor of $2$ overlooks the $2^2$ in $12$, so $90$ is not divisible by $12$. The correct LCM is $180$.
- $360$
  - _why:_ Including an unnecessary extra factor of $2$ doubles the smallest common multiple. The highest required powers give $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** Two numbers are coprime if their HCF is [ ___ ].

**Back:** Two numbers are coprime if their HCF is 1.

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ Coprime numbers share no prime factors, so their only positive common factor is $1$. Consequently, their highest common factor is $1$.
- 0
  - _why:_ An HCF is a positive factor shared by the numbers, and every whole number has $1$ as a factor. Coprime numbers therefore have HCF $1$, not $0$.
- 2
  - _why:_ An HCF of $2$ means both numbers are even and share the prime factor $2$, so they are not coprime. Coprime numbers have HCF $1$.
- -1
  - _why:_ At GCSE, the HCF is defined as the greatest positive common factor, so it cannot be negative. For two coprime numbers, the HCF is $1$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Two numbers have HCF 6 and LCM 72. After taking out the HCF, what do the leftover parts multiply to?

**Back:** $72 \div 6 = 12$

**Choices:**

- $72 \div 6 = 12$ ✔
  - _why:_ Writing the numbers as $6a$ and $6b$, where $a$ and $b$ are coprime, gives an LCM of $6ab$. Therefore, $ab = 72 \div 6 = 12$.
- $72 \times 6 = 432$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by the HCF counts the common factor again instead of removing it. Dividing the LCM by the HCF gives the leftover product: $72 \div 6 = 12$.
- $72 \div 36 = 2$
  - _why:_ Dividing by $6^2$ removes the common factor twice. The LCM contains one shared factor of $6$, so the leftover parts multiply to $72 \div 6 = 12$.
- $72 - 6 = 66$
  - _why:_ Taking out a common factor requires division, not subtraction. Dividing the LCM by the HCF gives the product of the coprime leftover parts: $72 \div 6 = 12$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Two whole numbers greater than 8 have HCF 6 and LCM 72. Find them.

**Back:** 18 and 24

**Choices:**

- 18 and 24 ✔
  - _why:_ The numbers are $6 \times 3$ and $6 \times 4$. Since $3$ and $4$ are coprime and multiply to $12$, the HCF is $6$ and LCM is $72$.
- 12 and 36
  - _why:_ These numbers have HCF $12$ and LCM $36$, so neither required value is obtained. The pair $18$ and $24$ has HCF $6$ and LCM $72$.
- 12 and 72
  - _why:_ Although the LCM is $72$, both numbers are divisible by $12$, making their HCF $12$ rather than $6$. The required numbers are $18$ and $24$.
- 24 and 36
  - _why:_ Although the LCM is $72$, these numbers share a factor of $12$, so their HCF is $12$. The pair with HCF $6$ is $18$ and $24$.
