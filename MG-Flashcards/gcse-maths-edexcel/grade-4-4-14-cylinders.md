## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What must be done to the diameter before using the cylinder volume formula?

**Back:** Halve it to find the radius.

**Choices:**

- Halve it to find the radius. ✔
  - _why:_ The diameter is twice the radius, so dividing the diameter by 2 gives the radius needed in the cylinder volume formula.
- Double it to find the radius.
  - _why:_ Doubling confuses the relationship between radius and diameter. The diameter is twice the radius, so it must be halved to find the radius.
- Square it to find the radius.
  - _why:_ The radius is found before any squaring is performed. Halve the diameter first; the radius is then squared when using $V = \pi r^2h$.
- Quarter it to find the radius.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 4 makes the measurement half the actual radius. Since the diameter equals two radii, it must be divided by 2.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the volume of a cylinder.

**Back:** $$V = \pi r^2 h$

**Choices:**

- $$V = \pi r^2 h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cylinder’s volume is the area of its circular base, $\pi r^2$, multiplied by its perpendicular height, $h$.
- $$V = 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This calculates the curved surface area rather than volume. Volume requires the circular base area multiplied by the height, giving $V = \pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \pi d^2 h$
  - _why:_ Using the diameter in place of the radius makes the result four times too large. Halve the diameter first, then use $V = \pi r^2h$.
- $$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$
  - _why:_ The factor $\frac{1}{3}$ belongs to the volume formula for a cone. A cylinder has constant cross-section, so its volume is $V = \pi r^2h$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** The units for volume are [ ___ ] units, such as $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$.

**Back:** The units for volume are cubic units, such as $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$.

**Choices:**

- cubic ✔
  - _why:_ Volume measures three-dimensional space, so its units are raised to the third power and are described as cubic units.
- square
  - _why:_ Square units measure two-dimensional area, not three-dimensional volume. Volume is measured in cubic units because three dimensions are multiplied together.
- linear
  - _why:_ Linear units measure one-dimensional lengths such as height or radius. Volume combines three dimensions, so the correct description is cubic units.
- fourth-power
  - _why:_ Volume involves three spatial dimensions, not four, so its units have an exponent of 3. The correct description is cubic units.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** The units for volume are cubic units, such as $\text{[...]}$ or $\text{m}^3$.

**Back:** The units for volume are cubic units, such as $\boxed{\text{cm}^3}$ or $\text{m}^3$.

**Choices:**

- $$\text{cm}^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Centimetres cubed are valid volume units because volume combines three length dimensions, giving an exponent of 3.
- $$\text{cm}^2$
  - _why:_ Centimetres squared measure area, which has two dimensions. Volume has three dimensions, so the correct unit is $\text{cm}^3$.
- $$\text{cm}$
  - _why:_ Centimetres alone measure length rather than volume. A volume measured using centimetres must be written as $\text{cm}^3$.
- $$\text{cm}^4$
  - _why:_ An exponent of 4 does not represent ordinary three-dimensional volume. Multiplying three centimetre measurements produces the correct unit $\text{cm}^3$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** In the formula $V = \pi r^2 h$, what does the term $\pi r^2$ represent?

**Back:** The area of the circular base.

**Choices:**

- The area of the circular base. ✔
  - _why:_ The area of a circle is $\pi r^2$, so this part of the formula gives the cylinder’s base area before it is multiplied by the height.
- The circumference of the circular base.
  - _why:_ The circumference of the base is $2\pi r$, not $\pi r^2$. The term $\pi r^2$ represents the area of the circular base.
- The diameter of the circular base.
  - _why:_ The diameter is simply $2r$ and does not involve $\pi$ or squaring. The term $\pi r^2$ gives the area of the circular base.
- The volume of the circular base.
  - _why:_ A circular base is two-dimensional, so it has area rather than volume. Its area is $\pi r^2$, which is then multiplied by the cylinder’s height.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean to give an answer "in terms of $\pi$"?

**Back:** Do not calculate a decimal; leave the symbol $\pi$ in the final result.

**Choices:**

- Do not calculate a decimal; leave the symbol $\pi$ in the final result. ✔
  - _why:_ An exact answer in terms of $\pi$ keeps the symbol rather than replacing it with a decimal approximation such as 3.14.
- Calculate a decimal; replace the symbol $\pi$ with 3.14 in the final result.
  - _why:_ Replacing $\pi$ with 3.14 produces an approximate decimal answer. In terms of $\pi$ means keeping the symbol in the exact final result.
- Calculate a decimal; round the symbol $\pi$ to 3 in the final result.
  - _why:_ Rounding $\pi$ to 3 gives a particularly inaccurate approximation. An answer in terms of $\pi$ must retain the symbol and avoid decimal evaluation.
- Do not calculate a decimal; remove the symbol $\pi$ from the final result.
  - _why:_ Removing $\pi$ changes the value rather than preserving it exactly. An answer in terms of $\pi$ must leave the symbol in the final result.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the total surface area (TSA) of a solid closed cylinder.

**Back:** $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$

**Choices:**

- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$ ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has two circular ends with total area $2\pi r^2$ and a curved surface with area $2\pi rh$, so these are added.
- $$\text{TSA} = \pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$
  - _why:_ This includes only one circular end, as if the cylinder were open. A solid closed cylinder has two ends, giving $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$.
- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + \pi rh$
  - _why:_ The curved surface is circumference multiplied by height, so it is $2\pi r \times h = 2\pi rh$. The missing factor of 2 halves that area.
- $$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2h$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the end areas by the height confuses surface area with volume and gives cubic dimensions. The correct areas are added: $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** When a cylinder's curved surface is "unwrapped", what shape does it form?

**Back:** A rectangle.

**Choices:**

- A rectangle. ✔
  - _why:_ The unwrapped width is the circular base’s circumference, $2\pi r$, and the other dimension is the cylinder’s height, forming a rectangle.
- A triangle.
  - _why:_ A triangle does not have the constant width of a cylinder’s curved surface. Unwrapping that surface produces a rectangle with width $2\pi r$.
- A circle.
  - _why:_ The two ends of a cylinder are circles, but the unwrapped curved surface is a rectangle whose width equals the base’s circumference.
- A semicircle.
  - _why:_ A semicircle is not the net of the curved surface. Cutting and flattening the curved surface produces a rectangle with height $h$ and width $2\pi r$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the dimensions of the rectangle formed by unwrapping a cylinder's curved surface?

**Back:** Width: circumference ($2\pi r$)Height: cylinder height ($h$)

**Choices:**

- Width: circumference ($2\pi r$)Height: cylinder height ($h$) ✔
  - _why:_ The curved surface unwraps into a rectangle whose width is the circular base’s circumference, $2\pi r$, and whose height equals the cylinder’s perpendicular height, $h$.
- Width: diameter ($2r$)Height: cylinder height ($h$)
  - _why:_ Using the diameter for the width ignores the distance around the circular base. The width is the circumference, $2\pi r$, while the rectangle’s height is $h$.
- Width: circumference ($2\pi r$)Height: cylinder radius ($r$)
  - _why:_ The rectangle’s second dimension comes from the cylinder’s height, not its radius. Its dimensions are circumference $2\pi r$ by cylinder height $h$.
- Width: radius ($r$)Height: cylinder diameter ($2r$)
  - _why:_ The radius and diameter describe the circular ends, not the unwrapped curved surface’s dimensions. The rectangle is $2\pi r$ wide and $h$ high.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** The surface area of a [ ___ ] cylinder includes two circular ends and one curved surface.

**Back:** The surface area of a closed cylinder includes two circular ends and one curved surface.

**Choices:**

- closed ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has a circular face at each end, so its surface area includes two circles as well as the curved surface.
- open
  - _why:_ An open cylinder is missing at least one circular end, so it does not necessarily include two circular ends. The required term is closed.
- hollow
  - _why:_ A hollow cylinder usually has inner and outer curved surfaces and may describe a tube rather than this standard solid. A closed cylinder has the stated three surfaces.
- semi-circular
  - _why:_ A semi-cylindrical shape has semicircular ends and only half the original curved surface. The cylinder with two complete circular ends is closed.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** The surface area of a closed cylinder includes [ ___ ] circular ends and one curved surface.

**Back:** The surface area of a closed cylinder includes two circular ends and one curved surface.

**Choices:**

- two ✔
  - _why:_ A closed cylinder has one circular end at the top and another at the bottom, giving two circular ends in total.
- one
  - _why:_ Counting only one end is the common mistake made when treating the cylinder as open. A closed cylinder has two circular ends.
- three
  - _why:_ Three circles may be confused with the three surface components, but the curved surface is not circular. There are exactly two circular ends.
- zero
  - _why:_ Having no circular ends would describe an open tube consisting only of a curved surface. A closed cylinder has two circular ends.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the volume of a semi-cylinder?

**Back:** Calculate the volume of a full cylinder and halve it ($V = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 L$).

**Choices:**

- Calculate the volume of a full cylinder and halve it ($V = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 L$). ✔
  - _why:_ A semi-cylinder is half of a full cylinder cut lengthways, so its volume is half of $\pi r^2L$, giving $V=\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- Calculate the volume of a full cylinder and double it ($V = 2\pi r^2 L$).
  - _why:_ Doubling produces the volume of two full cylinders rather than half a cylinder. The full-cylinder volume must instead be halved to obtain $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- Calculate the volume of a full cylinder and quarter it ($V = \frac{1}{4}\pi r^2 L$).
  - _why:_ Quartering halves the full-cylinder volume twice, making the result too small by a factor of two. A semi-cylinder requires one halving, giving $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2L$.
- Calculate the volume of a full cylinder and keep it ($V = \pi r^2 L$).
  - _why:_ Leaving the volume unchanged calculates a complete cylinder, not a semi-cylinder. Since the cross-section is halved, the full-cylinder volume must also be halved.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** For a semi-cylinder with radius $r$ and length $L$, what are the dimensions of the flat rectangular face?

**Back:** Diameter ($2r$) by length ($L$).

**Choices:**

- Diameter ($2r$) by length ($L$). ✔
  - _why:_ The flat face spans straight across the semicircular cross-section, giving width $2r$, and extends along the entire cylinder length $L$.
- Radius ($r$) by length ($L$).
  - _why:_ Using $r$ gives only half the rectangle’s required width. The cut runs across the full diameter $2r$ and along the length $L$.
- Circumference ($2\pi r$) by length ($L$).
  - _why:_ The circumference measures around the curved surface rather than across the flat cut. The rectangular face measures diameter $2r$ by length $L$.

  - _why:_ The radius is not the dimension extending along the cylinder. The face spans the diameter $2r$ across the end and the length $L$ along it.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the final step when solving for a missing radius $r$ if the formula yields $r^2 = 49$?

**Back:** Take the square root (e.g. $\sqrt{49} = 7$).

**Choices:**

- Take the square root (e.g. $\sqrt{49} = 7$). ✔
  - _why:_ Taking the square root reverses the squaring operation. Although $r^2=49$ has numerical roots $\pm7$, a physical radius must be positive, so $r=7$.
- Take the square (e.g. $49^2 = 2401$).
  - _why:_ Squaring again applies the operation twice rather than reversing it, producing an excessively large value. Taking the square root gives the positive radius $r=7$.
- Divide by two (e.g. $49 \div 2 = 24.5$).
  - _why:_ Dividing by two does not undo squaring; it confuses a power with multiplication by two. Taking the square root of $49$ gives the radius $7$.
- Take the cube root (e.g. $\sqrt[3]{49} \approx 3.66$).
  - _why:_ A cube root reverses cubing, not squaring, so it cannot isolate $r$ from $r^2$. The required inverse operation is the square root, giving $7$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How many $\text{cm}^3$ are in 1 litre?

**Back:** 1000 $\text{cm}^3$

**Choices:**

- 1000 $\text{cm}^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ One litre equals one cubic decimetre, and $1\text{ dm}=10\text{ cm}$. Cubing the conversion gives $10^3=1000$, so one litre is $1000\text{ cm}^3$.
- 100 $\text{cm}^3$
  - _why:_ This uses a two-dimensional conversion factor, as though only two lengths were multiplied. Volume requires cubing the factor of $10$, so the answer is $1000\text{ cm}^3$.
- 10 $\text{cm}^3$
  - _why:_ This converts only one length from decimetres to centimetres and ignores that volume has three dimensions. Cubing $10$ gives $1000\text{ cm}^3$.
- 10000 $\text{cm}^3$
  - _why:_ This adds an unnecessary factor of ten to the cubic conversion. Since $1\text{ dm}^3=(10\text{ cm})^3$, one litre contains $1000\text{ cm}^3$.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** When converting area units, $1 \text{ m}^2 = \text{[...]} \text{ cm}^2$.

**Back:** When converting area units, $1 \text{ m}^2 = \boxed{10,000} \text{ cm}^2$.

**Choices:**

- $$10,000$ ✔
  - _why:_ Area conversion factors must be squared. Since $1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}$, it follows that $1\text{ m}^2=100^2\text{ cm}^2=10,000\text{ cm}^2$.
- $$100$
  - _why:_ This uses the linear conversion factor without squaring it. Since an area has two dimensions, $100$ must be squared, giving $1\text{ m}^2=10,000\text{ cm}^2$.
- $$1,000$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly multiplies the linear conversion factor by $10$. Area conversion requires $100^2$, so $1\text{ m}^2$ is equal to $10,000\text{ cm}^2$.
- $$1,000,000$
  - _why:_ This applies the conversion factor three times, as if converting cubic units. Area units require $100^2$, so the correct conversion is $10,000\text{ cm}^2$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** If a problem asks about "filling" a tank, which property should you calculate?

**Back:** Volume.

**Choices:**

- Volume. ✔
  - _why:_ Filling concerns the amount of three-dimensional space inside the tank, which is measured by its volume, usually in cubic units or capacity units.
- Surface area.
  - _why:_ Surface area measures the material covering the tank’s surfaces, not the space available inside it. Filling a tank therefore requires calculating its volume.
- Circumference.
  - _why:_ Circumference gives the distance around a circular cross-section and cannot measure how much the tank holds. The required property for filling is volume.
- Cross-sectional area.
  - _why:_ Cross-sectional area measures one flat slice of the tank. It must be combined with the tank’s length or height to obtain the required volume.

**Back:** Surface area.

**Choices:**

- Surface area. ✔
  - _why:_ Painting covers the exposed surfaces of the tank, so the amount of paint required depends on the total surface area rather than the space inside.

  - _why:_ Circumference measures only the distance around a circular end. Painting the whole tank requires the areas of its surfaces, so calculate surface area.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** What must be checked regarding units before substituting values into a cylinder formula?

**Back:** Ensure all measurements are in the same units.

**Choices:**

- Ensure all measurements are in the same units. ✔
  - _why:_ Cylinder formulas combine several measurements, so their units must be consistent before substitution. Otherwise, the calculated area or volume will have an invalid numerical value.
- Ensure all measurements are in the different units.
  - _why:_ Mixing units, such as centimetres and metres, makes direct substitution invalid. All measurements must first be converted so that they use the same units.
- Ensure all measurements are in the smallest units.
  - _why:_ Using the smallest available unit is unnecessary and may produce awkward numbers. The measurements may use any suitable unit, provided they all use the same units.
- Ensure all measurements are in the largest units.
  - _why:_ Using the largest available unit is not a mathematical requirement. Accuracy depends on converting every measurement into the same units before using the formula.

## Card 22 — `cloze`

**Front:** The distance all the way across a circle through the centre is the [ ___ ], while the distance from the centre to the edge is the radius.

**Back:** The distance all the way across a circle through the centre is the diameter, while the distance from the centre to the edge is the radius.

**Choices:**

- diameter ✔
  - _why:_ The diameter is a straight line across the full width of a circle through its centre. It is twice the length of the radius.
- circumference
  - _why:_ The circumference is the curved distance around the outside of a circle, not a straight distance across it. The straight line through the centre is the diameter.
- radius
  - _why:_ The radius extends only from the centre to the circle’s edge, so it covers half the width. The full distance through the centre is the diameter.
- chord
  - _why:_ A chord joins two points on a circle but does not necessarily pass through the centre. The chord through the centre and across the full circle is the diameter.
