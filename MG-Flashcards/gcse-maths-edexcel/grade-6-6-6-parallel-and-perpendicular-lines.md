## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, the [ ___ ] is represented by $m$ and the y-intercept is represented by $c$.

**Back:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, the gradient is represented by $m$ and the y-intercept is represented by $c$.

**Choices:**

- gradient ✔
  - _why:_ In $y=mx+c$, $m$ measures the change in $y$ for each unit change in $x$, so it represents the line’s gradient.
- y-intercept
  - _why:_ The y-intercept is represented by $c$, not $m$. The coefficient $m$ gives the gradient, which measures the line’s rate of change.
- x-intercept
  - _why:_ The x-intercept is found by setting $y=0$ and is not generally represented by $m$. In $y=mx+c$, $m$ represents the gradient.
- constant
  - _why:_ The constant is $c$, the term independent of $x$. The coefficient $m$ represents the gradient because it determines the line’s steepness and direction.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, the gradient is represented by $m$ and the [ ___ ] is represented by $c$.

**Back:** In the straight-line equation $y=mx+c$, the gradient is represented by $m$ and the y-intercept is represented by $c$.

**Choices:**

- y-intercept ✔
  - _why:_ In $y=mx+c$, setting $x=0$ gives $y=c$. Therefore, $c$ represents the y-intercept, where the line crosses the y-axis.
- gradient
  - _why:_ The gradient is represented by $m$, not $c$. The value $c$ is the y-intercept because it is the value of $y$ when $x=0$.
- x-intercept
  - _why:_ The x-intercept is found by setting $y=0$ and solving for $x$; it is not represented directly by $c$. Here, $c$ is the y-intercept.
- origin
  - _why:_ The origin is the point $(0,0)$ and is not represented by $c$ unless $c=0$. In this equation, $c$ represents the y-intercept.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the gradient of a line given the equation $2x+5y=10$?

**Back:** Rearrange the equation to make $y$ the subject ($y=mx+c$) before reading the coefficient of $x$.

**Choices:**

- Rearrange the equation to make $y$ the subject ($y=mx+c$) before reading the coefficient of $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging into $y=mx+c$ isolates $y$, making the gradient visible as the coefficient of $x$. Here, division by 5 gives a gradient of $-2/5$.
- Read the coefficient of $x$ directly from the equation, so the gradient is $2$.
  - _why:_ Reading 2 directly ignores the coefficient of $y$ and the rearrangement needed. Making $y$ the subject gives $y=-2x/5+2$, so the gradient is $-2/5$.
- Divide the coefficient of $x$ by $5$, so the gradient is $2/5$.
  - _why:_ Dividing the coefficient of $x$ by 5 misses the negative sign introduced when $2x$ moves across the equals sign. The correct gradient is $-2/5$.
- Set $x=0$ and solve for $y$ before reading the resulting value as the gradient.
  - _why:_ Setting $x=0$ finds the y-intercept rather than the gradient. Instead, rearrange to $y=-2x/5+2$ and read the gradient as $-2/5$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the gradient and y-intercept of the line $6y=3x-12$?

**Back:** Gradient: $1/2$y-intercept: $-2$

**Choices:**

- Gradient: $1/2$y-intercept: $-2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing every term by 6 gives $y=(3/6)x-12/6=(1/2)x-2$. Thus the coefficient of $x$ is $1/2$ and the y-intercept is $-2$.
- Gradient: $3$y-intercept: $-12$
  - _why:_ This reads the coefficients on the right without dividing them by 6. Dividing the entire equation by 6 gives a gradient of $1/2$ and a y-intercept of $-2$.
- Gradient: $1/2$y-intercept: $2$
  - _why:_ The gradient has been divided correctly, but the intercept’s sign has been lost. Since $-12/6=-2$, the gradient is $1/2$ and the y-intercept is $-2$.
- Gradient: $2$y-intercept: $-2$
  - _why:_ This reverses the gradient fraction when dividing by 6. Since $3/6=1/2$ and $-12/6=-2$, the correct values are $1/2$ and $-2$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the relationship between the gradients of two parallel lines?

**Back:** They have the same gradient.

**Choices:**

- They have the same gradient. ✔
  - _why:_ Parallel lines have identical steepness and direction, so their gradients are equal. Their y-intercepts may differ, allowing them to remain distinct without meeting.
- They have negative reciprocal gradients.
  - _why:_ Negative reciprocal gradients describe perpendicular lines, not parallel lines. Parallel lines point in the same direction and therefore have the same gradient.
- They have opposite gradients.
  - _why:_ Opposite gradients generally produce lines with different directions that intersect. Parallel lines must have equal gradients, although their y-intercepts are different for distinct lines.
- They have reciprocal gradients.
  - _why:_ Reciprocal gradients are not generally equal and do not ensure that lines remain the same distance apart. Parallel lines must have the same gradient.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** A line is parallel to $y=3x+1$ and has no specific point given. Give one possible equation.

**Back:** Any equation in the form $y=3x+c$ where $c \neq 1$ (e.g. $y=3x-4$).

**Choices:**

- Any equation in the form $y=3x+c$ where $c \neq 1$ (e.g. $y=3x-4$). ✔
  - _why:_ A parallel line must retain the gradient 3 while having a different y-intercept from the original line. Therefore, any $y=3x+c$ with $c\neq1$ is suitable.
- Any equation in the form $y=-\frac{1}{3}x+c$ where $c \neq 1$ (e.g. $y=-\frac{1}{3}x-4$).
  - _why:_ Changing the gradient to $-1/3$ produces a line perpendicular to the original, not parallel. A distinct parallel line has form $y=3x+c$ with $c\neq1$.
- Any equation in the form $y=\frac{1}{3}x+c$ where $c \neq 1$ (e.g. $y=\frac{1}{3}x-4$).
  - _why:_ Using $1/3$ confuses the gradient with its reciprocal, so the resulting line is not parallel. The gradient must stay 3, giving $y=3x+c$ with $c\neq1$.
- Any equation in the form $y=-3x+c$ where $c \neq 1$ (e.g. $y=-3x-4$).
  - _why:_ Changing the sign of the gradient changes the line’s direction, so it will intersect the original. A parallel line requires gradient 3 and a different intercept.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the relationship between the gradients $m_1$ and $m_2$ of two perpendicular lines?

**Back:** They are negative reciprocals ($m_1 \times m_2 = -1$).

**Choices:**

- They are negative reciprocals ($m_1 \times m_2 = -1$). ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular non-vertical lines have gradients whose product is $-1$. Consequently, each gradient is the negative reciprocal of the other.
- They are equal gradients ($m_1 \times m_2 = 1$).
  - _why:_ Equal gradients make two distinct lines parallel, not perpendicular. For perpendicular lines, the gradients are negative reciprocals and satisfy $m_1\times m_2=-1$.
- They are positive reciprocals ($m_1 \times m_2 = 1$).
  - _why:_ Taking only the reciprocal omits the required sign change. Perpendicular gradients are negative reciprocals, so their product must be $-1$, not $1$.
- They are opposite gradients ($m_1 + m_2 = 0$).
  - _why:_ Simply changing the sign does not usually create a right angle. Perpendicular gradients require both reversing the fraction and changing its sign, giving product $-1$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the negative reciprocal of 4?

**Back:** $-1/4$

**Choices:**

- $-1/4$ ✔
  - _why:_ Taking a negative reciprocal means changing the sign and inverting the number. Since $4=4/1$, its negative reciprocal is $-1/4$.
- $1/4$
  - _why:_ This takes the reciprocal but misses the required sign change. Writing $4$ as $4/1$, then inverting and negating, gives $-1/4$.
- $-4$
  - _why:_ This changes the sign but does not take the reciprocal. The fraction $4/1$ must also be inverted, making the negative reciprocal $-1/4$.
- $-1/16$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 4 again confuses the reciprocal with squaring the denominator. Inverting $4/1$ gives $1/4$, then changing the sign gives $-1/4$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the negative reciprocal of $-2/3$?

**Back:** $3/2$

**Choices:**

- $3/2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Taking the reciprocal of $-2/3$ gives $-3/2$, and changing its sign gives $3/2$. Its product with the original gradient is $-1$, as required.
- $2/3$
  - _why:_ This changes the sign but does not take the reciprocal. The fraction must first become $-3/2$ and then change sign, giving the negative reciprocal $3/2$.
- $-3/2$
  - _why:_ This takes the reciprocal but fails to change the sign. A negative reciprocal requires both operations, so the correct result is $3/2$, not $-3/2$.
- $-2/3$
  - _why:_ This leaves the original value unchanged. The reciprocal of $-2/3$ is $-3/2$, and reversing its sign gives the required answer $3/2$.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the gradient of a line perpendicular to $y=mx+c$, you must [ ___ ] and change the sign.

**Back:** To find the gradient of a line perpendicular to $y=mx+c$, you must flip the fraction and change the sign.

**Choices:**

- flip the fraction ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular non-vertical gradients multiply to $-1$, so the original gradient’s numerator and denominator must be interchanged before its sign is reversed.
- square the fraction
  - _why:_ Squaring the gradient does not produce a perpendicular gradient. You must flip the fraction and then change its sign so that the two gradients multiply to $-1$.
- simplify the fraction
  - _why:_ Simplifying changes only the fraction’s form, not its value, so it cannot create the required perpendicular gradient. The correct operation is to flip the fraction before changing the sign.
- double the fraction
  - _why:_ Doubling the gradient is not the negative-reciprocal operation and will not generally make the product of the gradients $-1$. You must flip the fraction and change its sign.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the gradient of a line perpendicular to $y=mx+c$, you must flip the fraction and [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find the gradient of a line perpendicular to $y=mx+c$, you must flip the fraction and change the sign.

**Choices:**

- double the denominator
  - _why:_ Changing the denominator alone does not reliably form the negative reciprocal. After flipping the whole fraction, the required final operation is to change the sign.
- square the result
  - _why:_ Squaring the gradient does not create a negative reciprocal and will not make the gradients multiply to $-1$. The correct final step is to change the sign.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What type of line is perpendicular to the horizontal line $y=4$?

**Back:** A vertical line (e.g. $x=-1$).

**Choices:**

- A vertical line (e.g. $x=-1$). ✔
  - _why:_ Every point on $y=4$ has the same $y$-coordinate, so the line is horizontal. A line meeting it at a right angle must therefore be vertical.
- A horizontal line (e.g. $y=-1$).
  - _why:_ Another horizontal line would be parallel to $y=4$, not perpendicular to it. A line perpendicular to a horizontal line must be vertical, such as $x=-1$.
- A diagonal line (e.g. $y=x$).
  - _why:_ A line with positive gradient is slanted and does not generally meet a horizontal line at $90°$. The perpendicular line must be vertical, such as $x=-1$.
- A parallel line (e.g. $y=-1$).
  - _why:_ A line parallel to $y=4$ is also horizontal, so the two lines never meet. Its perpendicular must instead be vertical, such as $x=-1$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** State the formula for the gradient ($m$) between two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$.

**Back:** $$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

**Choices:**

- $$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Gradient is the change in vertical coordinate divided by the corresponding change in horizontal coordinate. Subtracting both coordinate pairs in the same order gives this formula.
- $$m = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{y_2 - y_1}$$
  - _why:_ This reverses rise and run, giving the reciprocal of the gradient. Gradient must be change in $y$ divided by change in $x$, using a consistent subtraction order.
- $$m = \frac{y_2 + y_1}{x_2 + x_1}$$
  - _why:_ Adding the coordinates does not measure the change between the two points. Gradient uses their differences: $y_2-y_1$ divided by $x_2-x_1$.
- $$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}$$
  - _why:_ The numerator and denominator use opposite subtraction orders here, incorrectly reversing the gradient’s sign. Both differences must follow the same point order.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** When calculating gradient, the [ ___ ] must be the numerator and the change in $x$ must be the denominator.

**Back:** When calculating gradient, the change in $y$ must be the numerator and the change in $x$ must be the denominator.

**Choices:**

- change in $y$ ✔
  - _why:_ Gradient measures rise divided by run, so the vertical change, or change in $y$, belongs in the numerator above the horizontal change in $x$.
- change in $x$
  - _why:_ Putting the change in $x$ in the numerator reverses rise over run and gives the reciprocal gradient. The numerator must be the change in $y$.
- sum of the $y$-values
  - _why:_ The sum of the $y$-coordinates does not measure vertical displacement between the points. The numerator must be their difference, which is the change in $y$.
- sum of the $x$-values
  - _why:_ The sum of the $x$-coordinates is unrelated to the line’s vertical rise. The gradient’s numerator is the change in $y$, with change in $x$ below it.

**Back:** When calculating gradient, the change in $y$ must be the numerator and the change in $x$ must be the denominator.

  - _why:_ Adding the $x$-coordinates does not measure horizontal displacement between the points. The denominator must be their difference, which is the change in $x$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the y-intercept ($c$) when you know the gradient ($m$) and a point $(x, y)$?

**Back:** Substitute the values of $m$, $x$, and $y$ into $y=mx+c$ and solve for $c$.

**Choices:**

- Substitute the values of $m$, $x$, and $y$ into $y=mx+c$ and solve for $c$. ✔
  - _why:_ The known point satisfies the line’s equation, so inserting its $x$- and $y$-coordinates and the known gradient leaves $c$ as the only unknown to solve.
- Multiply the values of $m$, $x$, and $y$ together and use the result as $c$.
  - _why:_ Multiplying all three known values does not use the equation of the line and cannot isolate the intercept. Substitute them into $y=mx+c$ and solve for $c$.
- Substitute the values of $m$ and $x$ into $y=mx$ and use the result as $c$.
  - _why:_ The product $mx$ gives the line’s change from its intercept, not the intercept itself. Substitute into $y=mx+c$ and rearrange to obtain $c=y-mx$.
- Divide the value of $y$ by $x$ and subtract the value of $m$ to find $c$.
  - _why:_ Dividing $y$ by $x$ attempts to find a gradient from the origin and ignores the known gradient. Substitute into $y=mx+c$ and solve for $c$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Find the equation of a line parallel to $y=-3x+8$ passing through $(0, 5)$.

**Back:** $y=-3x+5$

**Choices:**

- $y=-3x+5$ ✔
  - _why:_ Parallel lines have the same gradient, so the gradient remains $-3$. Passing through $(0,5)$ means the $y$-intercept is $5$, giving $y=-3x+5$.
- $y=3x+5$
  - _why:_ Changing the sign of the gradient produces a non-parallel line. The gradient must remain $-3$, while the point $(0,5)$ gives an intercept of $5$, so the answer is $y=-3x+5$.
- $y=-3x+8$
  - _why:_ Keeping the original equation ignores the new point: when $x=0$, this line gives $y=8$, not $5$. The required parallel line is $y=-3x+5$.
- $y=-1/3x+5$
  - _why:_ Taking the reciprocal of the gradient is not the rule for parallel lines. Parallel gradients are equal, so the gradient is $-3$ and the equation is $y=-3x+5$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Line A has gradient 3. Line B passes through $(-2, 1)$ and $(0, 7)$. Are they parallel?

**Back:** Yes. Line B gradient is $\frac{7-1}{0-(-2)} = 3$. Gradients are equal.

**Choices:**

- Yes. Line B gradient is $\frac{7-1}{0-(-2)} = 3$. Gradients are equal. ✔
  - _why:_ Line B’s gradient is the change in $y$ divided by the change in $x$: $6/2=3$. This equals Line A’s gradient, so the lines are parallel.
- No. Line B gradient is $\frac{7-1}{0-(-2)} = 6$. Gradients are not equal.
  - _why:_ This divides incorrectly: $\frac{7-1}{0-(-2)}=\frac{6}{2}=3$, not $6$. Both lines therefore have gradient $3$, so they are parallel.
- No. Line B gradient is $\frac{0-(-2)}{7-1} = 1/3$. Gradients are not equal.
  - _why:_ This reverses the gradient formula by calculating change in $x$ over change in $y$. Gradient is change in $y$ over change in $x$, giving $3$, so the lines are parallel.
- No. Line B gradient is $\frac{1-7}{0-(-2)} = -3$. Gradients are not equal.
  - _why:_ The $y$-differences and $x$-differences must be taken in the same direction. Using consistent subtraction gives $\frac{7-1}{0-(-2)}=3$, so the lines are parallel.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you prove two lines are perpendicular using their gradients?

**Back:** Show that the product of the two gradients is $-1$.

**Choices:**

- Show that the product of the two gradients is $-1$. ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular non-vertical lines have gradients that are negative reciprocals. Multiplying a gradient by its negative reciprocal gives $-1$, which proves the lines are perpendicular.
- Show that the product of the two gradients is $1$.
  - _why:_ A product of $1$ means the gradients are reciprocals with the same sign, not negative reciprocals. For perpendicular lines, their gradient product must be $-1$.
- Show that the sum of the two gradients is $-1$.
  - _why:_ Adding the gradients does not test perpendicularity. The gradients must be negative reciprocals, which is shown by their product being $-1$, not their sum.
- Show that the difference of the two gradients is $-1$.
  - _why:_ The difference between gradients has no general perpendicularity rule. Perpendicular gradients are negative reciprocals, so multiplying them must produce $-1$.
