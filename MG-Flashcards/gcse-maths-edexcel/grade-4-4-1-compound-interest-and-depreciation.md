## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In percentage calculations, what is the multiplier for a 6% increase?

**Back:** 1.06

**Choices:**

- 1.06 ✔
  - _why:_ A 6% increase means retaining 100% and adding 6%, giving 106%. Dividing by 100 produces the multiplier 1.06.
- 0.94
  - _why:_ Subtracting 0.06 from 1 gives the multiplier for a 6% decrease. For a 6% increase, add 0.06 to 1 to obtain 1.06.
- 1.6
  - _why:_ Treating 6% as 0.6 misplaces the decimal point. Since 6% is 0.06, the increase multiplier is $1 + 0.06 = 1.06$.
- 1.006
  - _why:_ This treats 6% as 0.006, which represents 0.6%. Converting 6% correctly gives 0.06, so the required multiplier is 1.06.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In percentage calculations, what is the multiplier for a 6% decrease?

**Back:** 0.94

**Choices:**

- 0.94 ✔
  - _why:_ A 6% decrease leaves 94% of the original value. Converting 94% to a decimal gives the multiplier $94 \div 100 = 0.94$.
- 1.06
  - _why:_ Adding 0.06 to 1 gives the multiplier for a 6% increase. A decrease requires subtraction, so $1 - 0.06 = 0.94$.
- 0.6
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats 6% as 0.6 and would leave only 60% of the value. A 6% decrease leaves 94%, giving 0.94.
- 0.994
  - _why:_ This corresponds to a decrease of only 0.6%, caused by placing the decimal incorrectly. A 6% decrease uses $1 - 0.06 = 0.94$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the compound interest formula $A = P(1 + \frac{r}{100})^n$, the power $n$ represents the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In the compound interest formula $A = P(1 + \frac{r}{100})^n$, the power $n$ represents the number of years.

**Choices:**

- number of years ✔
  - _why:_ The multiplier is applied once for every year that interest is added, so the exponent $n$ records the number of years in the investment period.
- annual interest rate
  - _why:_ The annual interest rate is represented by $r$ inside the multiplier, not by the exponent. The power $n$ gives the number of years.
- initial investment
  - _why:_ The initial investment is represented by $P$, because it is the amount multiplied by the growth factor. The power $n$ represents the number of years.
- final investment value
  - _why:_ The final investment value is represented by $A$ on the left-hand side. The exponent $n$ instead shows how many years the multiplier is applied.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the term per annum mean?

**Back:** Each year

**Choices:**

- Each year ✔
  - _why:_ The Latin expression per annum means for each year. An interest rate of 5% per annum is therefore applied at a rate of 5% each year.
- Each month
  - _why:_ Confusing annual and monthly rates changes how often the percentage applies. Per annum means each year, while per month would describe a monthly rate.
- Every two years
  - _why:_ Per annum describes a rate applying once each year, not once every two years. A two-year period would contain two annual applications.
- Each week
  - _why:_ A weekly rate applies each week, whereas per annum refers specifically to a yearly rate. Therefore, the correct meaning is each year.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Explain the core mechanism of compound interest.

**Back:** Interest is added to the total, then the next year's interest is calculated on that new, larger amount.

**Choices:**

- Interest is added to the total, then the next year's interest is calculated on that new, larger amount. ✔
  - _why:_ Compound interest adds each period's interest to the balance. Future interest is then calculated on both the original investment and previously earned interest.
- Interest is calculated from the original total, so every year's interest is based on that same, unchanged amount.
  - _why:_ Keeping the amount used to calculate interest unchanged describes simple interest. Compound interest uses the new, larger balance after each year's interest is added.
- Interest is removed from the total, then the next year's interest is calculated on that new, smaller amount.
  - _why:_ Removing a percentage models depreciation rather than compound interest growth. With compound interest, interest is added and the next calculation uses the new, larger amount.
- Interest is added only at the end, after every year's interest is calculated on the original starting amount.
  - _why:_ Calculating every year's interest from the starting amount produces simple interest, even if it is added later. Compound interest recalculates using each new balance.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it incorrect to simply add the annual interest rates together for a multi-year investment?

**Back:** Each percentage must be calculated from the new value, not the original value.

**Choices:**

- Each percentage must be calculated from the new value, not the original value. ✔
  - _why:_ After each year's percentage change, the base value changes. The next percentage must be applied to that new value, which is why multipliers are compounded.
- Each percentage must be calculated from the original value, not the new value.
  - _why:_ Using the original value every year describes simple interest and makes equal cash amounts each year. Compound interest applies each percentage to the new value.
- Each percentage must be subtracted from the new value, not added to the original value.
  - _why:_ Whether a percentage is added or subtracted depends on growth or depreciation, not on the number of years. The key issue is using the new value each year.
- Each percentage must be converted into the same units, not combined as annual rates.
  - _why:_ Annual rates already use compatible percentage units, so unit conversion is not the problem. Adding them fails because each year's percentage is applied to a new value.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** State the calculation to find the final amount for £3600 invested for 4 years at 2.5% compound interest.

**Back:** $$3600 \times 1.025^4$

**Choices:**

- $$3600 \times 1.025^4$ ✔
  - _why:_ A 2.5% increase has multiplier $1.025$, and compound interest applies it once in each of four years. Therefore, the multiplier is raised to power 4.
- $$3600 \times 1.25^4$
  - _why:_ Using 1.25 treats 2.5% as 25%, making the annual increase ten times too large. The correct multiplier is 1.025, giving $3600 \times 1.025^4$.
- $$3600 \times 1.025 \times 4$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the entire amount by 4 does not represent four years of compound growth. The annual multiplier must be applied four times as $3600 \times 1.025^4$.
- $$3600 \times 0.975^4$
  - _why:_ The multiplier 0.975 represents a 2.5% decrease, so it models depreciation. Compound interest increases the investment, requiring the multiplier 1.025 for four years.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Define depreciation.

**Back:** When a value goes down over time.

**Choices:**

- When a value goes down over time. ✔
  - _why:_ Depreciation is the reduction in an asset’s value as time passes, often modelled by multiplying its current value by a multiplier below 1 each year.
- When a value goes up over time.
  - _why:_ A value going up over time describes appreciation or growth, not depreciation. Depreciation means that the value goes down over time.
- When a value stays constant over time.
  - _why:_ A value remaining constant experiences no growth or depreciation. Depreciation specifically occurs when a value goes down over time.
- When a value goes up and down over time.
  - _why:_ Fluctuation includes both increases and decreases, so it does not specifically define depreciation. Depreciation means that a value goes down over time.

**Back:** 0.80

**Choices:**

- 0.80 ✔
  - _why:_ After a $20\%$ decrease, $80\%$ of the value remains. Converting $80\%$ to a decimal gives the depreciation multiplier $0.80$.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** The formula for the final amount after depreciation is $A = P(1 \text{[...]} \frac{r}{100})^n$.

**Back:** The formula for the final amount after depreciation is $A = P(1 \boxed{-} \frac{r}{100})^n$.

**Choices:**

- $$-$ ✔
  - _why:_ Depreciation removes a proportion of the current value each period, so its multiplier is $1 - \frac{r}{100}$. The missing operation is subtraction.
- $$+$
  - _why:_ Addition produces the compound growth formula and would increase the value each period. Depreciation requires the multiplier $1 - \frac{r}{100}$, so subtraction is needed.
- $$\times$
  - _why:_ Multiplication here would not subtract the depreciated percentage from the whole value. The correct multiplier is $1 - \frac{r}{100}$, requiring a minus sign.
- $$\div$
  - _why:_ Division does not form the proportion of value remaining after depreciation. That proportion is $1 - \frac{r}{100}$, so the required operation is subtraction.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** A value rises by 2% then falls by 4%. State the calculation to find the final value of original amount $X$.

**Back:** $$X \times 1.02 \times 0.96$

**Choices:**

- $$X \times 1.02 \times 0.96$ ✔
  - _why:_ A $2\%$ rise uses multiplier $1.02$, and a subsequent $4\%$ fall uses multiplier $0.96$. Both multipliers must be applied successively to $X$.
- $$X \times 1.02 \times 1.04$
  - _why:_ The multiplier $1.04$ models a $4\%$ increase rather than a decrease. The fall must use $0.96$, giving $X \times 1.02 \times 0.96$.
- $$X \times 1.06$
  - _why:_ The percentages cannot simply be added to form one growth multiplier because the changes apply successively to different values. Use $X \times 1.02 \times 0.96$.
- $$X \times 0.98$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the percentages gives a net $2\%$ fall, but this ignores compounding. Applying both changes correctly requires $X \times 1.02 \times 0.96$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard method for finding the number of years $n$ if you are given the final amount?

**Back:** Try whole-number powers on a calculator until the value matches or passes the target.

**Choices:**

- Try whole-number powers on a calculator until the value matches or passes the target. ✔
  - _why:_ At GCSE, substitute successive whole-number values of $n$ into the compound formula. Continue until the calculated amount matches or first passes the specified target.
- Divide the final percentage change by the annual rate to find the target.
  - _why:_ Using only the percentage change ignores the starting amount and repeated compounding. Instead, test whole-number powers in the full formula until the result reaches the target.
- Add the first year’s interest repeatedly until the value matches the target.
  - _why:_ Repeatedly adding the same interest amount models simple interest, not compound change. Test whole-number powers because each year’s change acts on the updated value.
- Round the annual multiplier to a whole number and divide by the target.
  - _why:_ Rounding the multiplier removes information and cannot determine the elapsed time. Keep the exact multiplier and test whole-number powers until the calculated value reaches the target.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you avoid rounding values during the intermediate steps of a compound interest calculation?

**Back:** Rounding too early can change the final penny (accuracy is lost).

**Choices:**

- Rounding too early can change the final penny (accuracy is lost). ✔
  - _why:_ Small rounding errors can be compounded across several calculation stages. Keeping full calculator values until the end preserves accuracy and prevents the final monetary answer changing by a penny.
- Rounding too early can change the annual interest rate (the rate becomes inaccurate).
  - _why:_ Rounding intermediate values does not alter the stated annual interest rate; it alters the calculated balance. Accumulated rounding error can change the final penny, so round only at the end.
- Rounding too early will always increase the final value (the answer is overestimated).
  - _why:_ Rounding can increase or decrease an intermediate value, so it does not always cause an overestimate. The real risk is accumulated inaccuracy changing the final penny.
- Rounding too early can stop the multiplier being used (the method no longer works).
  - _why:_ A multiplier can still be applied to a rounded number, but the resulting value may be inaccurate. Keeping unrounded intermediate values prevents the final penny from changing.
