## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In the term $a^n$, what are the names for $a$ and $n$?

**Back:** $a$ is the base$n$ is the index (or power/exponent)

**Choices:**

- $a$ is the base$n$ is the index (or power/exponent) ✔
  - _why:_ The base is the quantity repeatedly multiplied, while the index states how many times the base is used as a factor.
- $a$ is the index$n$ is the base (or power/exponent)
  - _why:_ This reverses the two names. In $a^n$, $a$ is the base, while $n$ is the index, also called the power or exponent.
- $a$ is the coefficient$n$ is the index (or power/exponent)
  - _why:_ A coefficient multiplies a term and is not the name for $a$ here. In $a^n$, $a$ is the base and $n$ is the index.
- $a$ is the exponent$n$ is the coefficient (or base number)
  - _why:_ The letter $a$ is not called the exponent in this notation. It is the base, and the raised value $n$ is the index or exponent.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The law for multiplying powers with the same base is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The law for multiplying powers with the same base is $\boxed{a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$ ✔
  - _why:_ When powers with the same base are multiplied, their indices are added because the total number of factors of $a$ is $m+n$.
- $$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{mn}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying powers does not multiply their indices. For a common base, count all factors by adding the indices, giving $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$.
- $$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m-n}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting indices is the rule for division, not multiplication. Multiplying powers with the same base requires adding the indices to obtain $a^{m+n}$.
- $$a^m \cdot a^n = a^m$
  - _why:_ Keeping only one index ignores the factors contributed by the second power. The indices must be added, so the product is $a^{m+n}$.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** The law for dividing powers with the same base is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The law for dividing powers with the same base is $\boxed{\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing equal bases cancels $n$ factors of $a$ from the numerator, leaving a power whose index is the difference $m-n$.
- $$\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m+n}$
  - _why:_ Adding indices applies when powers are multiplied. Division cancels factors, so the denominator’s index is subtracted and the correct result is $a^{m-n}$.
- $$\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{n-m}$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction changes the result to its reciprocal. Since the numerator has index $m$, the correct order is $m-n$, giving $a^{m-n}$.
- $$\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{\frac{m}{n}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the indices is not an index law. For powers with the same base, division subtracts the denominator’s index, producing $a^{m-n}$.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** The law for a power of a power is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The law for a power of a power is $\boxed{(a^m)^n = a^{mn}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Raising $a^m$ to the power $n$ repeats the factor group $n$ times, so the indices multiply to give $a^{mn}$.
- $$(a^m)^n = a^{m+n}$
  - _why:_ Adding indices is associated with multiplying separate powers of the same base. For a power raised to another power, multiply the indices to obtain $a^{mn}$.
- $$(a^m)^n = a^{m-n}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting indices applies to division of powers with a common base. A power of a power requires multiplication of the indices, giving $a^{mn}$.
- $$(a^m)^n = a^{m^n}$
  - _why:_ Applying the outer power only to the index misinterprets exponent notation. Repeating $a^m$ exactly $n$ times multiplies the indices, producing $a^{mn}$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** If $x = 2^p$ and $y = 2^q$, how is $2^{p+q}$ expressed in terms of $x$ and $y$?

**Back:** $$xy$

**Choices:**

- $$xy$ ✔
  - _why:_ Since $2^{p+q}=2^p \cdot 2^q$, substituting $x=2^p$ and $y=2^q$ gives the product $xy$.
- $$x+y$
  - _why:_ Adding $x$ and $y$ does not use the multiplication law for indices. Because adding indices corresponds to multiplying powers, $2^{p+q}=xy$.
- $$\frac{x}{y}$
  - _why:_ The quotient $\frac{x}{y}$ corresponds to $2^{p-q}$ because division subtracts indices. The sum $p+q$ instead gives the product $xy$.
- $$pq$
  - _why:_ The product $pq$ combines the indices themselves rather than the powers they define. Using $2^{p+q}=2^p \cdot 2^q$ gives $xy$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a negative index signify in mathematics?

**Back:** The reciprocal (1 divided by the base raised to the positive power).

**Choices:**

- The reciprocal (1 divided by the base raised to the positive power). ✔
  - _why:_ A negative index instructs you to take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power, so $a^{-n}$ means $1$ divided by $a^n$.
- The negative value (the base raised to the positive power and multiplied by $-1$).
  - _why:_ A negative index does not make the value negative. It produces the reciprocal of the positive power, so $a^{-n}=1/a^n$ for a non-zero base.
- The opposite base (the negative of the base raised to the positive power).
  - _why:_ A negative index does not change the sign of the base. It means take the reciprocal of the positive power, giving $1$ divided by the base raised to that power.
- The root (the base rooted according to the positive value of the index).
  - _why:_ A negative index does not require taking a root; fractional indices perform that role. It signifies the reciprocal of the base raised to the corresponding positive power.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** The general rule for negative indices is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The general rule for negative indices is $\boxed{a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Changing the sign of an index moves the power across the fraction line, so a negative power equals the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power.
- $$a^{-n} = -a^n$
  - _why:_ A negative index does not make the power’s value negative. It forms the reciprocal, so the correct general rule is $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$.
- $$a^{-n} = \frac{1}{an}$
  - _why:_ The reciprocal must include the entire positive power in the denominator. Dividing only by the index confuses an exponent with an ordinary numerical factor.
- $$a^{-n} = (-a)^n$
  - _why:_ Negating the base changes its sign behaviour but does not represent a negative index. The required operation is reciprocation, giving $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Back:** $$\frac{7}{3}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{7}{3}$ ✔
  - _why:_ An index of $-1$ means take the reciprocal. Swapping the numerator and denominator of $\frac{3}{7}$ gives $\frac{7}{3}$.
- $$\frac{3}{7}$
  - _why:_ Leaving the fraction unchanged treats the index as positive $1$. The index $-1$ requires the reciprocal, so the value is $\frac{7}{3}$.
- $$-\frac{7}{3}$
  - _why:_ A negative index does not make the fraction negative. It instructs you to take the reciprocal, so the positive result is $\frac{7}{3}$.
- $$-\frac{3}{7}$
  - _why:_ This adds a negative sign without taking the reciprocal. An index of $-1$ swaps the numerator and denominator, producing the positive fraction $\frac{7}{3}$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Explain why $6^{-1}$ is not $-6$.

**Back:** A negative index represents a reciprocal, not a negative number; $6^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Choices:**

- A negative index represents a reciprocal, not a negative number; $6^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}$. ✔
  - _why:_ A negative index means take the reciprocal of the base, so $a^{-1}=\frac{1}{a}$. It does not change the sign of the number.
- A negative index represents a negative value, not a reciprocal; $6^{-1} = -6$.
  - _why:_ This confuses a negative exponent with a negative number. The exponent $-1$ means reciprocal, so the correct value is $6^{-1}=\frac{1}{6}$.
- A negative index represents a subtraction, not a reciprocal; $6^{-1} = 6 - 1$.
  - _why:_ This treats the index as an instruction to subtract 1 from the base. Indices describe powers, and $6^{-1}$ means the reciprocal $\frac{1}{6}$.
- A negative index represents a zero power, not a reciprocal; $6^{-1} = 1$.
  - _why:_ This mixes up the negative index rule with the zero index rule. $6^0=1$, but $6^{-1}$ means $\frac{1}{6}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $0^{-1}$ undefined?

**Back:** It would require division by zero ($1/0$).

**Choices:**

- It would require division by zero ($1/0$). ✔
  - _why:_ A power of $-1$ means taking the reciprocal, so $0^{-1}$ would be $\frac{1}{0}$. Division by zero is undefined.
- It would require multiplication by zero ($1 \times 0$).
  - _why:_ The tempting slip is treating the reciprocal as multiplication by the base. A power of $-1$ means $\frac{1}{0}$, which is division by zero and is undefined.
- It would require subtracting zero ($1 - 0$).
  - _why:_ This treats the negative index as a subtraction instruction. In fact, $0^{-1}$ means the reciprocal of 0, giving $\frac{1}{0}$, which is undefined.
- It would require raising zero to zero ($0^0$).
  - _why:_ This confuses a negative index with a zero index. The expression $0^{-1}$ means $\frac{1}{0}$, not $0^0$, so it is undefined because division by zero is not allowed.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** When evaluating $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$, why is it usually better to take the root first?

**Back:** It keeps the numbers smaller and easier to calculate before applying the power.

**Choices:**

- It keeps the numbers smaller and easier to calculate before applying the power. ✔
  - _why:_ Taking the root first usually reduces the size of the number before the power is applied. For example, $27^{\frac{2}{3}}$ becomes $3^2$, not $27^2$ first.
- It keeps the numbers larger and easier to calculate before applying the power.
  - _why:_ The point of taking the root first is normally to make the numbers smaller, not larger. Smaller intermediate values reduce the chance of awkward arithmetic.
- It keeps the indices negative and easier to calculate before applying the power.
  - _why:_ This mixes up fractional indices with negative indices. Taking the root first does not make indices negative; it usually makes the base smaller before applying the power.
- It keeps the powers unchanged and easier to calculate before applying the root.
  - _why:_ This reverses the recommended order. For $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$, taking the root first often gives smaller numbers before raising to the power $m$.

**Back:** $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$$3^2 = 9$

**Choices:**

- $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$$3^2 = 9$ ✔
  - _why:_ The denominator 3 means take the cube root first, giving 3. The numerator 2 then means square it, so the result is 9.
- $\sqrt[2]{27} = 3$$3^3 = 27$
  - _why:_ This swaps the numerator and denominator and also treats $\sqrt{27}$ as 3. The denominator is 3, so take $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$, then square to get 9.
- $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$$3^3 = 27$
  - _why:_ The cube root step is right, but the numerator is 2, not 3. After $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$, you must calculate $3^2=9$.
- $\sqrt[3]{27} = 9$$9^2 = 81$
  - _why:_ This confuses the cube root of 27 with another factor. Since $3^3=27$, $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$, and then $3^2=9$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you evaluate a negative index applied to a fraction, such as $\left(\frac{27}{64}\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}}$?

**Back:** Apply the fractional power to both parts, then flip the fraction (take the reciprocal).

**Choices:**

- Apply the fractional power to both parts, then flip the fraction (take the reciprocal). ✔
  - _why:_ A negative index means take the reciprocal, and the fractional power applies to the numerator and denominator. You can evaluate the fractional power, then flip the fraction.
- Apply the fractional power to both parts, then change the sign (make it negative).
  - _why:_ This confuses a negative exponent with a negative value. The negative index means take the reciprocal, so you flip the fraction rather than making the result negative.
- Apply the fractional power to the top only, then flip the fraction (take the reciprocal).
  - _why:_ A power outside brackets applies to both numerator and denominator. For a fraction in brackets, the fractional power must be applied to both parts before taking the reciprocal.
- Apply the fractional power to both parts, then square the fraction (take the power).
  - _why:_ The extra squaring comes from misreading the negative sign as another power. The negative index tells you to take the reciprocal, so the fraction should be flipped.

## Card 19 — `cloze`

**Front:** When a power is outside brackets containing a product, the rule is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** When a power is outside brackets containing a product, the rule is $\boxed{(ab)^n = a^n b^n}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$ ✔
  - _why:_ A power outside brackets containing a product applies to every factor inside. Therefore both $a$ and $b$ are raised to the power $n$.
- $$(ab)^n = a^n + b^n$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly changes a product into a sum. Powers distribute over multiplication here, so the correct rule is $(ab)^n=a^n b^n$.
- $$(ab)^n = ab^n$
  - _why:_ This applies the outside power only to $b$ and forgets $a$. The power applies to every factor inside the brackets, so $(ab)^n=a^n b^n$.
- $$(ab)^n = a^{n}b$
  - _why:_ This applies the outside power only to $a$ and forgets $b$. For a product in brackets, both factors are raised to the power: $(ab)^n=a^n b^n$.

**Front:** Evaluate $(27x^6)^{\frac{2}{3}}$.

**Back:** $\sqrt[3]{27x^6} = 3x^2$$(3x^2)^2 = 9x^4$

  - _why:_ The cube root step is right, but squaring $3x^2$ means squaring both factors. $3^2=9$, not 6, so the result is $9x^4$.

**Back:** $\sqrt[4]{81} = 3$$\sqrt[4]{10^8} = 10^2$Result: $3 \cdot 100 = 300$

  - _why:_ The fourth root can be taken for each factor. Since $3^4=81$ and $(10^2)^4=10^8$, the result is $3 \cdot 100=300$.

  - _why:_ This uses the square root of 81 instead of the fourth root. The fourth root of 81 is 3, so the correct result is $3 \cdot 100=300$.

  - _why:_ This halves the exponent instead of dividing it by 4. The fourth root of $10^8$ is $10^{8/4}=10^2$, giving 300.
- $\sqrt[4]{81} = 3$$\sqrt[4]{10^8} = 10^2$Result: $3 + 100 = 103$

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the general strategy for solving an equation with an unknown index, such as $9^n = 27$?

**Back:** Rewrite both sides using the same base (e.g., base 3).

**Choices:**

- Rewrite both sides using the same base (e.g., base 3). ✔
  - _why:_ Writing both sides with the same base lets you compare the indices directly. For example, $9^n=27$ becomes $(3^2)^n=3^3$.
- Rewrite both sides using the same index (e.g., index 3).
  - _why:_ The useful comparison comes from matching the bases, not forcing the indices to match. Once both sides have base 3, the powers can be equated.
- Rewrite both sides using the same coefficient (e.g., coefficient 3).
  - _why:_ Coefficients are not the key feature in an exponential equation like $9^n=27$. The standard strategy is to rewrite both sides using the same base.
- Rewrite both sides using the same fraction (e.g., fraction $\frac{1}{3}$).
  - _why:_ This confuses index equations with fractional-index calculations. For unknown indices, rewrite both sides with a common base, such as base 3.

## Card 23 — `basic`

**Front:** Write $2\sqrt{8}$ as a single power of 2.

**Back:** $8 = 2^3$, so $\sqrt{8} = 2^{\frac{3}{2}}$$2^1 \cdot 2^{\frac{3}{2}} = 2^{\frac{5}{2}}$

**Choices:**

- $8 = 2^3$, so $\sqrt{8} = 2^{\frac{3}{2}}$$2^1 \cdot 2^{\frac{3}{2}} = 2^{\frac{5}{2}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A square root is equivalent to a power of one half, so $sqrt{2^3}=2^{rac{3}{2}}$. Multiplying powers with the same base adds their indices, giving $2^{1+rac{3}{2}}=2^{rac{5}{2}}$.
- $8 = 2^3$, so $sqrt{8} = 2^3$$2^1 cdot 2^3 = 2^4$
  - _why:_ This fails to apply the square-root power of $rac{1}{2}$, leaving the index unchanged. In fact, $sqrt{2^3}=2^{rac{3}{2}}$, so adding indices gives $2^{rac{5}{2}}$.
- $8 = 2^3$, so $sqrt{8} = 2^{rac{1}{3}}$$2^1 cdot 2^{rac{1}{3}} = 2^{rac{4}{3}}$
  - _why:_ This confuses a square root with a cube root by dividing the index by 3. A square root halves the index, so $sqrt{8}=2^{rac{3}{2}}$ and the product is $2^{rac{5}{2}}$.
- $8 = 2^3$, so $sqrt{8} = 2^{rac{3}{2}}$$2^1 cdot 2^{rac{3}{2}} = 2^{rac{3}{2}}$
  - _why:_ This multiplies the indices when multiplying powers with the same base. The indices must be added instead, so $1+rac{3}{2}=rac{5}{2}$ and the result is $2^{rac{5}{2}}$.
