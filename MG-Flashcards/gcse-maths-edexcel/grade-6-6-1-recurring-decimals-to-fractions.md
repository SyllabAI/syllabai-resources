## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a dot above a single digit in a decimal notation indicate?

**Back:** That digit repeats forever.

**Choices:**

- That digit repeats forever. ✔
  - _why:_ A dot above a single digit marks that digit as the recurring block, so the digit continues repeating indefinitely rather than terminating.
- That digit appears only once.
  - _why:_ A dot is not used to show that a digit occurs once. It marks the recurring digit, meaning that digit repeats forever.
- That digit is rounded up.
  - _why:_ Rounding is usually indicated by an approximation sign or stated degree of accuracy. A dot above the digit instead means that it repeats forever.
- That digit ends the decimal.
  - _why:_ The final digit of a terminating decimal has no dot above it. A dot indicates that the marked digit repeats forever.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In the decimal $0.\dot{4}0\dot{5}$, which digits form the repeating block?

**Back:** 405

**Choices:**

- 405 ✔
  - _why:_ The dots mark the beginning and end of the recurring section. Every digit from 4 through 5 is included, so the block is 405.
- 45
  - _why:_ This omits the 0 between the two dotted digits. The dots mark the endpoints of the entire repeating block, so the correct block is 405.
- 40
  - _why:_ This stops before the second dotted digit, but both dotted digits are included as endpoints. The complete repeating block is therefore 405.
- 05
  - _why:_ This incorrectly begins the block after the first dotted digit. The first dot is above 4, so the full repeating block is 405.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** A recurring decimal is a decimal where a [ ___ ] repeats forever.

**Back:** A recurring decimal is a decimal where a digit or block of digits repeats forever.

**Choices:**

- digit or block of digits ✔
  - _why:_ A recurring decimal contains either one digit or a longer block of digits that repeats indefinitely, such as 7 in $0.777\ldots$ or 405 in $0.405405\ldots$.
- non-repeating block of digits
  - _why:_ A non-repeating block does not satisfy the definition of a recurring decimal. The repeating part must be a digit or block of digits that continues forever.
- decimal point or place-value marker
  - _why:_ The decimal point separates whole-number and fractional places but does not itself recur. A digit or block of digits is what repeats forever.
- whole-number part of the number
  - _why:_ The whole-number part remains fixed rather than repeating through successive decimal places. A recurring decimal has a digit or block of digits that repeats forever.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** How is a fraction like $\frac{5}{11}$ converted into a decimal without a calculator?

**Back:** Divide the numerator (5) by the denominator (11) using long division.

**Choices:**

- Divide the numerator (5) by the denominator (11) using long division. ✔
  - _why:_ A fraction bar represents division, with the numerator as the dividend and the denominator as the divisor. Therefore, calculate $5 \div 11$ using long division.
- Divide the denominator (11) by the numerator (5) using long division.
  - _why:_ This reverses the fraction and calculates $11 \div 5$, which corresponds to $\frac{11}{5}$. For $\frac{5}{11}$, divide 5 by 11.
- Multiply the numerator (5) by the denominator (11) using long multiplication.
  - _why:_ Multiplying 5 by 11 calculates a product, not the value of the fraction. A fraction bar means divide the numerator by the denominator.
- Add the numerator (5) to the denominator (11) using column addition.
  - _why:_ Adding the numerator and denominator gives 16, which does not represent the fraction. Convert it by dividing the numerator, 5, by the denominator, 11.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** When converting a fraction to a decimal using division, how do you identify that the decimal is recurring?

**Back:** A remainder repeats/reappears during the division process.

**Choices:**

- A remainder repeats/reappears during the division process. ✔
  - _why:_ When a remainder reappears, the same subsequent division steps and quotient digits must occur again. This creates a repeating block in the decimal expansion.
- A remainder becomes zero during the division process.
  - _why:_ A remainder of zero means the division has finished exactly, producing a terminating decimal. A recurring decimal is identified when a non-zero remainder reappears.
- The divisor repeats/reappears during the division process.
  - _why:_ The divisor stays fixed in every division step, so its repeated use does not identify recurrence. Recurrence is detected when a remainder repeats or reappears.
- A quotient digit becomes zero during the division process.
  - _why:_ A zero quotient digit can occur within either terminating or recurring decimals, so it is not decisive. A repeated remainder guarantees that the decimal cycle repeats.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the primary goal of multiplying a recurring decimal $x$ by powers of 10 in an algebraic proof?

**Back:** To make the infinite recurring tails match so they cancel out when subtracted.

**Choices:**

- To make the infinite recurring tails match so they cancel out when subtracted. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by a suitable power of 10 aligns identical recurring tails. Subtracting the equations then removes those infinite tails and leaves a finite equation for $x$.
- To turn the recurring decimal into a whole number without any subtraction.
  - _why:_ No finite power of 10 turns a non-zero recurring decimal into an integer. The multiplication instead aligns the recurring tails so subtraction cancels them.
- To make the infinite recurring tails different so they combine when added.
  - _why:_ The recurring tails must be identical and aligned, not different. Once they match, subtracting one equation from the other cancels the infinite decimal parts.
- To remove the recurring digits by rounding them to a terminating decimal.
  - _why:_ Rounding would replace the recurring decimal with an approximation and could not prove an exact fraction. Powers of 10 align the recurring tails for exact cancellation.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the proof for $0.\dot{7}$, if $x = 0.777\ldots$, then $10x = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** In the proof for $0.\dot{7}$, if $x = 0.777\ldots$, then $10x = \boxed{7.777\ldots}$.

**Choices:**

- $$7.777\ldots$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 shifts every digit one place to the left, so $0.777\ldots$ becomes $7.777\ldots$. The recurring tail remains unchanged and aligned.
- $$77.777\ldots$
  - _why:_ This shifts the decimal point two places, which corresponds to multiplying by 100 rather than 10. A one-place shift gives $10x=7.777\ldots$.
- $$0.0777\ldots$
  - _why:_ This shifts the digits one place to the right, which corresponds to dividing by 10. Multiplying by 10 shifts them left, giving $7.777\ldots$.
- $$7.0777\ldots$
  - _why:_ A decimal-point shift moves all digits together and does not insert a zero after the decimal point. Multiplying by 10 gives $7.777\ldots$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is $0.\dot{7}$ expressed as a fraction?

**Back:** $$\frac{7}{9}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{7}{9}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Let $x=0.777\ldots$. Then $10x=7.777\ldots$, so subtraction gives $9x=7$ and therefore $x=\frac{7}{9}$.
- $$\frac{7}{10}$
  - _why:_ This treats the decimal as the terminating value $0.7$ and ignores the infinitely repeated 7s. Algebraic subtraction gives $9x=7$, so $x=\frac{7}{9}$.
- $$\frac{7}{90}$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses 90 as the coefficient after shifting and subtracting. Since $10x-x=9x$, the equation is $9x=7$, giving $\frac{7}{9}$.
- $$\frac{1}{7}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the numerator and denominator. From $9x=7$, division by 9 gives $x=\frac{7}{9}$, not its reciprocal.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** For $0.5\dot{3}$, why is subtracting $x$ from $10x$ ineffective for canceling the decimal?

**Back:** The tails do not match ($5.333\ldots$ vs $0.533\ldots$).

**Choices:**

- The tails do not match ($5.333\ldots$ vs $0.533\ldots$). ✔
  - _why:_ Subtraction cancels a recurring tail only when the digits after the aligned decimal positions are identical. Here, multiplying by 10 does not align the recurring parts of the two numbers.
- The decimal points do not align ($5.333\ldots$ vs $0.533\ldots$).
  - _why:_ The decimal points can be aligned for subtraction, but that alone does not cancel an infinite recurring tail. The method fails because the tails do not match: $5.333\ldots$ and $0.533\ldots$.
- The digits do not recur ($5.333\ldots$ vs $0.533\ldots$).
  - _why:_ Both numbers do have recurring digits, so recurrence is not the problem. Subtraction is ineffective because their tails do not match: one has $5.333\ldots$ while the other has $0.533\ldots$.
- The whole-number parts do not match ($5.333\ldots$ vs $0.533\ldots$).
  - _why:_ The whole-number parts do not need to match when subtracting recurring decimals. The issue is that the recurring tails are misaligned, giving $5.333\ldots$ and $0.533\ldots$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Which two multiples of $x$ should be subtracted to convert $0.5\dot{3}$ to a fraction?

**Back:** $100x$$10x$

**Choices:**

- $100x$$10x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 moves the non-recurring digit before the decimal point, while multiplying by 100 shifts one place further. Both then have the same recurring tail, allowing cancellation.
- $10x$$x$
  - _why:_ Using $10x$ and $x$ leaves the recurring digits in different decimal positions, so their tails cannot cancel. The aligned multiples are $100x$ and $10x$.
- $1000x$$10x$
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 1000 shifts one place too far relative to $10x$, so the recurring tails are not aligned in the required way. Use $100x$ and $10x$.
- $100x$$x$
  - _why:_ Although these multiples differ by a factor of 10, they leave the non-recurring digit in the wrong alignment for the intended subtraction. The required multiples are $100x$ and $10x$.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a decimal has $m$ non-recurring digits and $n$ recurring digits, you use the multiples $\text{[...]}$ and $10^{m+n}x$.

**Back:** If a decimal has $m$ non-recurring digits and $n$ recurring digits, you use the multiples $\boxed{10^m x}$ and $10^{m+n}x$.

**Choices:**

- $$10^m x$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplication by $10^m$ moves all $m$ non-recurring digits before the decimal point. This positions the start of the recurring block for alignment with the larger multiple.
- $$10^n x$
  - _why:_ Using $10^n$ shifts according to the recurring block’s length rather than the number of non-recurring digits. The first required multiple is $10^m x$.
- $$10^{m+1}x$
  - _why:_ Adding one to the exponent shifts the decimal one place too far and misaligns the recurring tails. Exactly $m$ non-recurring digits must be moved, so use $10^m x$.
- $$10^{m-1}x$
  - _why:_ Subtracting one from the exponent leaves one non-recurring digit after the decimal point. All $m$ such digits must be shifted, requiring $10^m x$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a decimal has $m$ non-recurring digits and $n$ recurring digits, you use the multiples $10^m x$ and $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** If a decimal has $m$ non-recurring digits and $n$ recurring digits, you use the multiples $10^m x$ and $\boxed{10^{m+n}x}$.

**Choices:**

- $$10^{m+n}x$ ✔
  - _why:_ The larger multiple must shift past both the $m$ non-recurring digits and one complete block of $n$ recurring digits, requiring a total shift of $m+n$ places.
- $$10^n x$
  - _why:_ Using only $10^n$ ignores the $m$ non-recurring digits before the repeating block. The total shift must cover both sections, so the multiple is $10^{m+n}x$.
- $$10^{mn}x$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the exponents confuses the total number of places with a product. The shifts for the non-recurring and recurring sections are added, giving $10^{m+n}x$.
- $$10^{m-n}x$
  - _why:_ Subtracting $n$ shifts in the wrong direction conceptually and cannot move through both sections of digits. Their lengths must be added, producing $10^{m+n}x$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is $1000x$ used when converting $0.\dot{1}8\dot{9}$ to a fraction?

**Back:** The repeating block contains 3 digits.

**Choices:**

- The repeating block contains 3 digits. ✔
  - _why:_ The dots mark the first and last digits of the repeating block, so the block is $189$. Since it has three digits, multiplying by 1000 shifts one complete repetition.
- The repeating block contains 2 digits.
  - _why:_ Counting only the dotted endpoint digits overlooks the $8$ between them. The complete repeating block is $189$, so it contains three digits and requires multiplication by 1000.
- The repeating block contains 1 digit.
  - _why:_ The notation does not make the first digit non-recurring; the dots enclose the whole block $189$. Therefore, the repeating block contains three digits, not one.
- The repeating block contains 4 digits.
  - _why:_ Repeating the first digit visually can lead to counting an extra place, but one complete block is simply $189$. It contains three digits, which is why $1000x$ is used.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Convert $0.\dot{1}8\dot{9}$ to its simplest fraction form.

**Back:** $$\frac{7}{37}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{7}{37}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Letting $x=0.189189\ldots$ gives $1000x-x=189$, so $999x=189$. Thus $x=\frac{189}{999}=\frac{7}{37}$ after dividing by 27.
- $$\frac{7}{27}$
  - _why:_ A denominator of 27 results from reducing the denominator incorrectly. Since $1000x-x=189$, the fraction is $\frac{189}{999}$, which simplifies to $\frac{7}{37}$.
- $$\frac{7}{36}$
  - _why:_ Subtracting 1 from 1000 gives 999, not a value that reduces to a denominator of 36. Simplifying $\frac{189}{999}$ correctly gives $\frac{7}{37}$.
- $$\frac{189}{1000}$
  - _why:_ This uses 1000 as the denominator without accounting for the recurring subtraction. The correct equation is $999x=189$, giving $\frac{189}{999}=\frac{7}{37}$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** A fraction is in its simplest form when the numerator and denominator have no [ ___ ] greater than 1.

**Back:** A fraction is in its simplest form when the numerator and denominator have no common factors greater than 1.

**Choices:**

- common factors ✔
  - _why:_ A fraction can be reduced only when its numerator and denominator share a factor greater than 1. Having no such common factors therefore means it is in simplest form.
- common multiples
  - _why:_ Every pair of positive integers has common multiples, so this cannot determine whether a fraction is simplified. Simplest form requires the numerator and denominator to have no common factors greater than 1.
- prime factors
  - _why:_ The numerator and denominator may each have prime factors while sharing none of them. The relevant condition is that they have no common factors greater than 1.
- common denominators
  - _why:_ A denominator belongs to a fraction rather than being a shared divisor of its two terms. Simplest form is defined by having no common factors greater than 1.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** For $2.4\dot{3}\dot{6}$, which two multiples of $x$ are needed to align the recurring tails?

**Back:** $1000x$$10x$

**Choices:**

- $1000x$$10x$ ✔
  - _why:_ The single non-recurring digit requires a shift by 10, while the three-digit recurring block requires three further places. Thus $1000x$ and $10x$ have matching recurring tails.
- $100x$$10x$
  - _why:_ Using $100x$ shifts only two places beyond the original decimal and does not span the complete three-digit recurring block. The required multiples are $1000x$ and $10x$.
- $1000x$$x$
  - _why:_ Subtracting $x$ fails to account separately for the one non-recurring digit, leaving the recurring tails misaligned. The correctly aligned multiples are $1000x$ and $10x$.
- $1000x$$100x$
  - _why:_ The difference between these shifts is only one decimal place, but the recurring block has three digits. A three-place difference is needed, using $1000x$ and $10x$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the value of the recurring decimal $0.\dot{9}$?

**Back:** 1

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ The decimal $0.\dot{9}$ approaches 1 exactly; there is no positive gap between them. Algebraically, if $x=0.\dot{9}$, then $10x-x=9$, so $x=1$.
- 0.9
  - _why:_ Treating the recurring decimal as slightly less than 1 is a common misconception. Its infinitely many 9s make it exactly equal to 1, not 0.9.
- 0.99
  - _why:_ This value truncates the decimal after two 9s, but the dot means that 9 repeats infinitely. The recurring decimal $0.\dot{9}$ is exactly equal to 1.
- 1.00
  - _why:_ This rounds the recurring decimal to two decimal places rather than giving its exact value. Since $0.\dot{9}=1$, the exact answer is 1.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** Because $0.\dot{9}=1$, the decimal $1.2\dot{9}$ is exactly equal to [ ___ ].

**Back:** Because $0.\dot{9}=1$, the decimal $1.2\dot{9}$ is exactly equal to 1.3.

**Choices:**

- 1.3 ✔
  - _why:_ The recurring 9 increases the preceding digit: $1.2\dot{9}=1.2999\ldots$, which is exactly 1.3 because $0.0999\ldots=0.1$.
- 1.29
  - _why:_ This truncates the decimal after one recurring digit and ignores the infinitely many following 9s. Since $1.2999\ldots$ reaches the next tenth exactly, the answer is 1.3.
- 1.23
  - _why:_ This incorrectly replaces the recurring 9 with a recurring 3-type value. The sequence $1.2999\ldots$ is exactly equal to 1.3, not 1.23.
- 1.0
  - _why:_ Rounding to the nearest whole number loses the required exact decimal value. Although 1 is a whole-number approximation, $1.2\dot{9}$ is exactly 1.3.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you approach a calculation (like multiplication or division) involving recurring decimals?

**Back:** Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction before calculating.

**Choices:**

- Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction before calculating. ✔
  - _why:_ Fractions preserve the exact values of recurring decimals, allowing multiplication or division without rounding errors. The resulting fraction can then be simplified or converted back to a decimal.
- Round every recurring decimal to a whole number before calculating.
  - _why:_ Rounding first replaces exact recurring values with approximations, so the final result may be inaccurate. Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction before calculating.
- Remove every recurring decimal’s repeated digits before calculating.
  - _why:_ Removing the recurring digits truncates the numbers and changes their values, producing an inexact calculation. Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction before calculating.
- Treat every recurring decimal as a terminating decimal before calculating.
  - _why:_ Treating recurring decimals as terminating ignores that their repeated digits continue indefinitely. Convert every recurring decimal into a fraction before calculating to retain exact values.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** When dividing fractions, what is the 'reciprocal'?

**Back:** The second fraction flipped upside down.

**Choices:**

- The second fraction flipped upside down. ✔
  - _why:_ In fraction division, the reciprocal is formed by interchanging the numerator and denominator of the second fraction. You then multiply the first fraction by this flipped fraction.
- The first fraction flipped upside down.
  - _why:_ A common division error is to flip the first fraction. The reciprocal used when dividing fractions is the second fraction flipped upside down, after which the fractions are multiplied.
- The two fractions swapped around.
  - _why:_ Swapping the fractions’ order is not taking a reciprocal and changes the calculation incorrectly. The reciprocal is the second fraction flipped upside down.
- The second fraction made negative.
  - _why:_ Changing both signs produces negatives rather than reciprocals. For fraction division, keep the signs and flip the numerator and denominator of the second fraction only.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** To show the pattern to an examiner, write out $x$ with [ ___ ] instead of just the dot notation.

**Back:** To show the pattern to an examiner, write out $x$ with a few repeated digits instead of just the dot notation.

**Choices:**

- a few repeated digits ✔
  - _why:_ Writing several repeated digits makes the recurring pattern explicit and shows which digits continue indefinitely. This helps justify the algebra used to convert the decimal into a fraction.
- only non-recurring digits
  - _why:_ Writing only the non-recurring digits hides the repeating pattern needed for the conversion method. Instead, write a few repeated digits so the recurrence is clear to the examiner.
- a rounded decimal value
  - _why:_ Rounding changes the recurring decimal into an approximation and obscures its exact pattern. Write a few repeated digits instead, preserving the value needed for an exact fraction.
- one repeated digit
  - _why:_ Writing a single repeated digit may not clearly display a multi-digit recurring pattern. Write a few repeated digits so the examiner can see exactly what repeats.
