## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What specific type of triangle is required to use Pythagoras' theorem?

**Back:** Right-angled triangles (containing a 90° angle).

**Choices:**

- Right-angled triangles (containing a 90° angle). ✔
  - _why:_ Pythagoras' theorem applies only to right-angled triangles because it relates the squares of the two perpendicular sides to the square of the hypotenuse.
- Acute-angled triangles (containing only angles below 90°).
  - _why:_ Confusing an acute angle with a right angle makes this tempting. Standard Pythagoras' theorem requires a right-angled triangle containing an angle of exactly 90°.
- Obtuse-angled triangles (containing an angle above 90°).
  - _why:_ An obtuse triangle has no right angle, so the standard Pythagorean relationship does not apply. The required triangle is right-angled, with one angle equal to 90°.
- Equilateral triangles (containing three 60° angles).
  - _why:_ Equilateral triangles have three equal 60° angles rather than a right angle. Pythagoras' theorem requires a right-angled triangle containing a 90° angle.

**Back:** The hypotenuse is the longest side and is always opposite the right angle.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ The hypotenuse lies opposite the 90° angle and is always the longest side of a right-angled triangle.

  - _why:_ Mistaking the hypotenuse for one of the perpendicular sides reverses the length relationship. The hypotenuse is the longest side because it lies opposite the largest angle.

  - _why:_ A triangle can be rotated, so the hypotenuse is not identified by its orientation. It is the longest side and lies opposite the right angle.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** In Pythagoras' theorem, the formula is $\text{[...]}$, where $c$ represents the hypotenuse.

**Back:** In Pythagoras' theorem, the formula is $\boxed{a^2 + b^2 = c^2}$, where $c$ represents the hypotenuse.

**Choices:**

- $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ In a right-angled triangle, the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides equals the square of the hypotenuse, represented by $c$.
- $$a + b = c$
  - _why:_ Adding the side lengths directly omits the essential squaring operation. Pythagoras' theorem states that $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ when $c$ is the hypotenuse.
- $$a^2 - b^2 = c^2$
  - _why:_ Subtraction is used only after rearranging to find a shorter side. With $c$ as the hypotenuse, the original formula is $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
- $$a^2 + b^2 = c$
  - _why:_ Failing to square the hypotenuse produces an inconsistent relationship. The squares of the shorter sides add to the square of the hypotenuse: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the hypotenuse calculated if the two shorter sides are known?

**Back:** Square both sidesAdd the squaresSquare root the result

**Choices:**

- Square both sidesAdd the squaresSquare root the result ✔
  - _why:_ Pythagoras gives $c^2=a^2+b^2$. Squaring and adding the two shorter sides finds $c^2$, and taking the square root then gives the hypotenuse $c$.
- Square both sidesSubtract the squaresSquare root the result
  - _why:_ Subtraction is used when the hypotenuse is already known and a shorter side is missing. To find the hypotenuse, square the shorter sides, add, then square root.
- Add both sidesSquare the totalSquare root the result
  - _why:_ Adding the lengths before squaring introduces an unwanted $2ab$ term. Each shorter side must be squared separately, the squares added, and the result square-rooted.
- Square root both sidesAdd the resultsSquare the total
  - _why:_ Reversing the operations does not follow $c^2=a^2+b^2$. Square each known shorter side, add those squares, then take the square root to obtain $c$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** How is a shorter side calculated if the hypotenuse and one other side are known?

**Back:** Square both sidesSubtract the smaller square from the hypotenuse squareSquare root the result

**Choices:**

- Square both sidesSubtract the smaller square from the hypotenuse squareSquare root the result ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging $a^2+b^2=c^2$ gives a missing shorter side squared as $c^2$ minus the known shorter side squared. Square-rooting then gives the side length.
- Square both sidesAdd the smaller square to the hypotenuse squareSquare root the result
  - _why:_ Addition finds a hypotenuse when both shorter sides are known. To find a shorter side, subtract the known shorter-side square from the hypotenuse square, then square root.
- Square both sidesSubtract the hypotenuse square from the smaller squareSquare root the result
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction produces a negative value because the hypotenuse is longest. Subtract the smaller square from the hypotenuse square before taking the square root.
- Subtract both sidesSquare the remaining differenceSquare root the result
  - _why:_ Subtracting the original lengths ignores that Pythagoras relates their squares. Square both known sides first, subtract the smaller square from the hypotenuse square, then square root.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving for a missing side, why would you use subtraction instead of addition?

**Back:** Because the hypotenuse (longest side) is already known.

**Choices:**

- Because the hypotenuse (longest side) is already known. ✔
  - _why:_ When the hypotenuse is known, rearranging $a^2+b^2=c^2$ to find a shorter side requires subtracting the known shorter-side square from the hypotenuse square.
- Because the shorter side (smallest side) is already known.
  - _why:_ Knowing one shorter side does not by itself determine the operation; the key is that the hypotenuse is known. Finding the other shorter side therefore requires subtraction.
- Because the right angle (90° angle) is already known.
  - _why:_ The right angle confirms that Pythagoras can be used, but it does not cause subtraction. Subtraction is used because the hypotenuse is already known.
- Because the triangle area (inside space) is already known.
  - _why:_ The triangle's area is not part of the Pythagorean side-length calculation. Subtraction is required when the hypotenuse is known and a shorter side must be found.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** To square a number like $x$, you [ ___ ] ($x \times x$).

**Back:** To square a number like $x$, you multiply it by itself ($x \times x$).

**Choices:**

- multiply it by itself ✔
  - _why:_ Squaring means multiplying a number by itself once. Therefore, $x$ squared is $x \times x$, written as $x^2$.
- multiply it by two
  - _why:_ Multiplying by two doubles the number rather than squaring it. To square $x$, multiply it by itself, giving $x \times x = x^2$.
- add it to itself
  - _why:_ Adding a number to itself produces $2x$, which is the same as doubling it. Squaring requires multiplication by itself: $x \times x = x^2$.
- divide it by itself
  - _why:_ Dividing a non-zero number by itself gives $1$, not its square. Squaring $x$ means multiplying it by itself to obtain $x^2$.

**Back:** Draw a perpendicular height from the top, which bisects (halves) the base.

**Choices:**

- Draw a perpendicular height from the top, which bisects (halves) the base. ✔
  - _why:_ In an isosceles triangle, the perpendicular from the apex to the base also bisects the base. This produces two congruent right-angled triangles for using Pythagoras.

  - _why:_ A height must be perpendicular to the base, not parallel, and drawing it does not double the base. In an isosceles triangle, it bisects the base.
- Draw a perpendicular height from the top, which bisects (halves) an equal side.
  - _why:_ The apex height in an isosceles triangle bisects the base, not either equal sloping side. This perpendicular creates two right-angled triangles suitable for Pythagoras.
- Draw a diagonal line from the top, which trisects (thirds) the base.
  - _why:_ The required construction is a perpendicular height, and symmetry makes it halve rather than trisect the base. This creates two congruent right-angled triangles.

**Back:** 90° (a right angle).

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ A $45°$ angle may occur in an isosceles right-angled triangle, but not between vertical and horizontal lines. A wall and level ground meet at $90°$.

  - _why:_ A $360°$ angle represents one complete turn rather than the angle between two perpendicular lines. A vertical wall and horizontal ground form a $90°$ angle.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** If a journey goes North then East, what is the geometric relationship between the two paths?

**Back:** They meet at a 90° angle.

**Choices:**

- They meet at a 90° angle. ✔
  - _why:_ North and East are perpendicular compass directions, so the two stages of the journey meet at $90°$. They form the shorter sides of a right-angled triangle.
- They meet at a 45° angle.
  - _why:_ A $45°$ direction would be North-East, halfway between North and East. Travelling North and then East involves a $90°$ turn.
- They meet at a 180° angle.
  - _why:_ A $180°$ angle would mean reversing direction along the same straight line, such as North then South. North and East are perpendicular and meet at $90°$.
- They meet at a 360° angle.
  - _why:_ A $360°$ angle is a complete turn and returns to the original direction. North and East differ by a quarter-turn, which is $90°$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What must you check regarding units before starting a Pythagoras calculation?

**Back:** Ensure all side lengths are in the same units (e.g., all cm or all m).

**Choices:**

- Ensure all side lengths are in the same units (e.g., all cm or all m). ✔
  - _why:_ Pythagoras combines squared side lengths, so every measurement must first use the same unit. Otherwise, the numerical values cannot be substituted into the formula consistently.
- Ensure the two shorter side lengths are in different units (e.g., one cm and one m).
  - _why:_ Using different units makes the squared lengths incompatible. All side lengths must be converted into the same units, such as all centimetres or all metres, before calculating.
- Ensure all side lengths are in squared units (e.g., all cm² or all m²).
  - _why:_ Side lengths use linear units, not squared units; squaring occurs when applying the formula. The original measurements must all be in the same linear unit.
- Ensure only the hypotenuse is in standard units (e.g., cm rather than m).
  - _why:_ Converting only the hypotenuse leaves incompatible measurements in the calculation. Every side length involved must be expressed in the same unit before Pythagoras is applied.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the square root of a number?

**Back:** The positive number which, when multiplied by itself, produces the original number.

**Choices:**

- The positive number which, when multiplied by itself, produces the original number. ✔
  - _why:_ The square root reverses squaring: multiplying the positive square root by itself gives the original number. For example, $csqrt{49}=7$ because $7ctimes7=49$.
- The negative number which, when multiplied by itself, produces the original number.
  - _why:_ Although a negative number can have the same square, the square root symbol denotes the positive value. For example, $csqrt{49}=7$, not $-7$.
- The positive number which, when multiplied by two, produces the original number.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by two finds a relationship involving halves, not square roots. A square root is the positive number that produces the original number when multiplied by itself.
- The positive number which, when added to itself, produces the original number.
  - _why:_ Adding a number to itself finds half of the original number. A square root is found by identifying the positive number whose square equals the original.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the side $EF$ if $EF^2 = 207$?

**Back:** Calculate $\sqrt{207}$.

**Choices:**

- Calculate $\sqrt{207}$. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $EF^2=207$, applying the square root to both sides gives $EF=csqrt{207}$. A length uses the positive square root.
- Calculate $207^2$.
  - _why:_ Squaring again applies the same operation rather than reversing it, producing a much larger value. Take the square root of $207$ to find $EF$.
- Calculate $cfrac{207}{2}$.
  - _why:_ Dividing by two reverses multiplication by two, not squaring. Since $EF$ has been squared, calculate $csqrt{207}$ to undo that operation.
- Calculate $csqrt{207^2}$.
  - _why:_ Squaring $207$ before taking its square root simply returns $207$, not the required side length. Apply the square root directly to $207$.
