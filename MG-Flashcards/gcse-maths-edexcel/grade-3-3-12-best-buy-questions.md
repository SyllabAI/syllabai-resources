# Best Buy Questions Flashcards

_Lane: `gcse-maths-edexcel` · grade 3 · 24 cards (source: MathsGenie flashcards)_

## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In a best-buy question, what must you compare between different pack sizes?

**Back:** Compare the same unit or amount.

**Choices:**

- Compare the same unit or amount. ✔
  - _why:_ Comparing equal units or amounts creates a fair comparison. For example, calculating each price per item or per kilogram reveals which pack genuinely offers better value.
- Compare the total price of each pack.
  - _why:_ Total prices alone are misleading when packs contain different quantities. Compare the same unit or amount, such as price per item, to identify the better buy.
- Compare the largest available pack size.
  - _why:_ A larger pack is not automatically better value because its unit cost may be higher. Compare the same unit or amount for every option.
- Compare the amount saved on each pack.
  - _why:_ A displayed saving does not ensure the lowest unit cost, especially across different pack sizes. Compare the same unit or amount to make a fair judgement.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The best value option has the [ ___ ], not necessarily the lowest shelf price.

**Back:** The best value option has the lowest unit cost, not necessarily the lowest shelf price.

**Choices:**

- lowest unit cost ✔
  - _why:_ The lowest unit cost means paying the least for each equal unit, such as one item or 100 g. This allows a fair comparison between different pack sizes.
- lowest total cost
  - _why:_ Choosing the lowest total cost confuses shelf price with value for money. The correct fill is lowest unit cost, because pack quantities may differ.
- largest pack size
  - _why:_ The largest pack can still have a higher price per item or per gram. The correct fill is lowest unit cost, which compares equal quantities.
- greatest total saving
  - _why:_ A large advertised saving does not necessarily produce the best price for each unit. The correct fill is lowest unit cost, found by comparing equivalent quantities.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** Unit cost = [ ___ ].

**Back:** Unit cost = total price ÷ quantity.

**Choices:**

- total price ÷ quantity ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the total price by the number or quantity of units gives the cost of one unit, allowing packs of different sizes to be compared fairly.
- quantity ÷ total price
  - _why:_ This reverses the division and calculates quantity per pound rather than cost per unit. The correct formula is total price ÷ quantity.
- total price × quantity
  - _why:_ Multiplying combines the price and quantity instead of finding the cost of one unit. Unit cost is calculated using total price ÷ quantity.
- total price ÷ pack size
  - _why:_ Pack size can mean the number of packs rather than the quantity of goods being priced. The general formula is total price ÷ quantity.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ Convert £4.20 to 420p, then divide by 12. Since $420 \div 12 = 35$, each roll costs 35p.

  - _why:_ This treats the 12-roll pack as though it contained 10 rolls. The correct calculation is $420 \div 12 = 35$, so each roll costs 35p.
- 50p per roll.
  - _why:_ This does not divide the 420p total by all 12 rolls. The correct unit cost is $420 \div 12 = 35$, giving 35p per roll.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Choices:**

- The units are different. ✔
  - _why:_ One price describes 100 ml while the other describes only 1 ml, so they are not directly comparable. Both prices must use the same volume.
- The bottle sizes are equal.
  - _why:_ Equal bottle sizes would not make the comparison unfair. The problem is that the units are different: one price is per 100 ml and the other per ml.
- The prices are too similar.
  - _why:_ Similarity between prices does not determine whether a comparison is fair. The comparison is unfair because the units are different and must first be converted.
- The volumes are both metric.
  - _why:_ Using metric measurements does not make unequal quantities directly comparable. The issue is that the units are different: 100 ml must be converted to 1 ml, or vice versa.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** "Extra free" changes the [ ___ ]; "money off" changes the [ ___ ].

**Back:** "Extra free" changes the quantity; "money off" changes the price.

**Choices:**

- "Extra free" changes the quantity; "money off" changes the price. ✔
  - _why:_ An extra-free offer increases the amount received without changing the stated price. A money-off offer reduces the amount paid without changing the stated quantity.
- "Extra free" changes the price; "money off" changes the quantity.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two effects. Extra free increases the quantity supplied, whereas money off reduces the price paid for the stated quantity.
- "Extra free" changes the price; "money off" changes the price.
  - _why:_ Only the second term is correct. Extra free changes the quantity received rather than the price, while money off does change the price.
- "Extra free" changes the quantity; "money off" changes the quantity.
  - _why:_ Only the first term is correct. Extra free increases the quantity, but money off leaves the quantity unchanged and instead reduces the price.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you avoid rounding unit costs too early?

**Back:** Close values may be compared wrongly.

**Choices:**

- Close values may be compared wrongly. ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding can make two slightly different unit costs appear equal or can reverse which seems smaller. Keeping full calculator values until the final comparison avoids this error.
- Close values may be made more accurate.
  - _why:_ Rounding removes digits and therefore reduces accuracy rather than improving it. Full unit costs should be retained until after comparison so close values are ranked correctly.
- Unit costs may be given different units.
  - _why:_ Rounding changes numerical precision, not the units used. The real danger is that close unit costs may be made equal or compared in the wrong order.
- Calculations may become needlessly longer.
  - _why:_ Rounding usually shortens numbers, but that is not why it should be delayed. Early rounding can distort close unit costs and lead to the wrong best-buy choice.
