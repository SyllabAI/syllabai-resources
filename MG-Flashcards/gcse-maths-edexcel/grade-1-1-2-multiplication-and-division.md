## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the mathematical term for the result of a multiplication?

**Back:** Product

**Choices:**

- Product ✔
  - _why:_ The result obtained when two or more numbers are multiplied together is called the product. The numbers being multiplied are called factors.
- Quotient
  - _why:_ A quotient is the result of division, which makes it a tempting confusion with another operation. The result of multiplication is called the product.
- Sum
  - _why:_ A sum is the result obtained by adding numbers together. Multiplication produces a product, while the numbers that are multiplied are called factors.
- Difference
  - _why:_ A difference is the result of subtracting one number from another. The mathematical term for the result of a multiplication is the product.

**Front:** What is the mathematical term for the result of a division?

**Back:** Quotient

**Choices:**

- Quotient ✔
  - _why:_ The result obtained when one number is divided by another is called the quotient. Any amount left over after whole-number division is the remainder.
- Product
  - _why:_ A product is the result of multiplication, not division. The result found by dividing one number by another is called the quotient.
- Difference
  - _why:_ A difference is found through subtraction rather than division. The correct mathematical term for the result of a division is the quotient.
- Remainder
  - _why:_ A remainder is only the amount left after a division does not divide exactly. The overall result of the division is called the quotient.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** In the calculation $A \div B = C$, name the parts $A$ and $B$.

**Back:** $A$: Dividend$B$: Divisor

**Choices:**

- $A$: Dividend$B$: Divisor ✔
  - _why:_ The dividend is the number being divided, while the divisor is the number it is divided by. Therefore, in this calculation, $A$ is the dividend and $B$ is the divisor.
- $A$: Divisor$B$: Dividend
  - _why:_ This reverses the two division terms. The number being divided, $A$, is the dividend, while the number it is divided by, $B$, is the divisor.
- $A$: Numerator$B$: Denominator
  - _why:_ Numerator and denominator name parts of a written fraction. In a division calculation, $A$ is the dividend being divided and $B$ is the divisor.
- $A$: Factor$B$: Product
  - _why:_ Factors and products are multiplication terms, so they do not identify these parts of a division. $A$ is the dividend and $B$ is the divisor.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** A digit is a single symbol in a number (e.g., 0-9), whereas [ ___ ] refers to its value based on its position.

**Back:** A digit is a single symbol in a number (e.g., 0-9), whereas place value refers to its value based on its position.

**Choices:**

- place value ✔
  - _why:_ Place value is the value a digit has because of its position in a number. For example, 4 represents 40 when it occupies the tens column.
- face value
  - _why:_ Face value is the digit’s value considered by itself, regardless of position. The value produced by its position in a number is called place value.
- absolute value
  - _why:_ Absolute value is a number’s distance from zero, so it concerns positive and negative signs rather than digit positions. The positional value of a digit is its place value.
- numerical value
  - _why:_ Numerical value is a broad term for the quantity represented and does not specifically describe positional value. The value determined by a digit’s position is its place value.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you use place value to multiply a 3-digit number by a 1-digit number?

**Back:** Split the 3-digit number into hundreds, tens, and ones before multiplying each part.

**Choices:**

- Split the 3-digit number into hundreds, tens, and ones before multiplying each part. ✔
  - _why:_ Partitioning the number into hundreds, tens, and ones preserves the value of every digit. Each part can then be multiplied separately before the results are added.
- Split the 3-digit number into thousands, hundreds, and tens before multiplying each part.
  - _why:_ This shifts every digit one place to the left, making each part ten times too large. A 3-digit number should be split into hundreds, tens, and ones.
- Split the 3-digit number into hundreds, ones, and tenths before multiplying each part.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly treats the tens digit as ones and the ones digit as tenths. Whole 3-digit numbers must be partitioned into hundreds, tens, and ones.
- Split the 3-digit number into hundreds, tens, and tenths before multiplying each part.
  - _why:_ This places the final digit in the tenths column, reducing its value by a factor of ten. The correct place-value parts are hundreds, tens, and ones.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What happens if you try to divide a number by 0?

**Back:** There is no ordinary answer (it is not possible).

**Choices:**

- There is no ordinary answer (it is not possible). ✔
  - _why:_ Division asks how many groups of the divisor make the dividend. No number multiplied by 0 can produce a non-zero dividend, so ordinary division by 0 is undefined.
- There is an answer of 0 (the result is zero).
  - _why:_ This confuses dividing by 0 with dividing 0 by a non-zero number. Division by 0 has no ordinary answer because multiplication by 0 cannot reverse it.
- There is an answer of 1 (the result is one).
  - _why:_ Dividing a non-zero number by itself gives 1, but 0 cannot be used as a divisor. Division by 0 has no ordinary answer.
- There is an infinite answer (the result is infinity).
  - _why:_ Values can grow without bound as a divisor approaches 0, but infinity is not the result of dividing by 0. The division itself has no ordinary answer.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How should you split 34 when multiplying it by another number?

**Back:** Split it into 30 (tens) and 4 (ones).

**Choices:**

- Split it into 30 (tens) and 4 (ones). ✔
  - _why:_ The digit 3 is in the tens column, so it represents 30, while 4 is in the ones column. Therefore, 34 partitions as 30 and 4.
- Split it into 3 (tens) and 4 (ones).
  - _why:_ This uses the tens digit’s face value instead of its place value. In 34, the 3 represents 30, so the number splits into 30 and 4.
- Split it into 30 (tens) and 40 (ones).
  - _why:_ This incorrectly gives the ones digit a tens value, producing parts that total 70. The 4 represents four ones, so 34 splits into 30 and 4.
- Split it into 34 (tens) and 0 (ones).
  - _why:_ This does not partition the number by place value because 34 still combines both digits. The tens part is 30 and the ones part is 4.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Back:** Multiplying a number by 30 is ten times bigger than multiplying it by 3.

**Choices:**

- ten times ✔
  - _why:_ Since $30 = 10 \times 3$, multiplying by 30 produces a result ten times the result obtained by multiplying the same number by 3.
- twenty-seven times
  - _why:_ This confuses the difference between 30 and 3 with the scale factor. Since $30 \div 3 = 10$, the result is ten times bigger, not 27 times bigger.
- one tenth
  - _why:_ This uses the inverse of the required comparison. Multiplying by 30 gives ten times the result of multiplying by 3, rather than one tenth of it.
- two times
  - _why:_ This treats the zero in 30 as if it doubled the multiplier. In fact, $30 \div 3 = 10$, so the result is ten times bigger.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What determines the number of "decimal places" in a value?

**Back:** The count of digits to the right of the decimal point.

**Choices:**

- The count of digits to the right of the decimal point. ✔
  - _why:_ Decimal places are counted from the decimal point towards the right. For example, 4.275 has three digits to the right, so it has three decimal places.
- The count of digits to the left of the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Digits to the left of the decimal point determine the whole-number part, not the number of decimal places. Decimal places count digits to the right of the point.
- The count of digits on both sides of the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Counting all digits gives the total number of digits, including the whole-number part. Decimal places are determined only by the digits to the right of the decimal point.
- The count of zeros to the right of the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Zeros to the right can be decimal-place digits, but non-zero digits count as well. The correct measure is the total count of digits to the right of the point.

**Choices:**

- Multiply as whole numbers (ignore the point)Restore the same number of decimal places in the answer ✔
  - _why:_ Ignoring the point first allows ordinary whole-number multiplication. The original decimal’s number of decimal places is then restored to give the product its correct place value.
- Divide as whole numbers (ignore the point)Restore the same number of decimal places in the answer
  - _why:_ Dividing the digits applies the wrong operation and cannot produce the required product. Multiply as whole numbers first, then restore the original number of decimal places.
- Multiply as whole numbers (ignore the point)Restore the same number of significant figures in the answer
  - _why:_ Decimal places must be restored, not significant figures. Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit, whereas decimal places count positions to the right of the point.
- Multiply as whole numbers (ignore the point)Move the point one place to the left in the answer
  - _why:_ Moving the point one place is only valid for multiplying or dividing by 10. Multiply as whole numbers, then restore the decimal’s original number of decimal places.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the total decimal places for the product of two decimals (e.g., $4.2 \times 0.31$)?

**Back:** Add the number of decimal places in both numbers together ($1 + 2 = 3$).

**Choices:**

- Add the number of decimal places in both numbers together ($1 + 2 = 3$). ✔
  - _why:_ The product contains the combined decimal places of both factors. Since 4.2 has one and 0.31 has two, the product must have three decimal places.
- Multiply the number of decimal places in both numbers together ($1 \times 2 = 2$).
  - _why:_ Multiplying the counts of decimal places is a common slip. The counts must be added, so one decimal place plus two decimal places gives three.
- Choose the larger number of decimal places from both numbers ($2 > 1 = 2$).
  - _why:_ Using only the larger count ignores the place-value scaling from the other factor. The decimal-place counts must be added, giving $1 + 2 = 3$.
- Subtract the number of decimal places in both numbers ($2 - 1 = 1$).
  - _why:_ Subtracting the decimal-place counts reverses the place-value rule. Multiplying two decimals combines their scaling, so the counts are added to give three decimal places.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** When multiplying decimals, you must [ ___ ] the total decimal places rather than just copying the point's position from the question.

**Back:** When multiplying decimals, you must count the total decimal places rather than just copying the point's position from the question.

**Choices:**

- count ✔
  - _why:_ Each decimal factor contributes its own power of ten to the product. Counting and adding their decimal places locates the product’s decimal point correctly.
- estimate
  - _why:_ Estimating helps check whether the final position is sensible, but it does not determine the exact number of decimal places. You must count the total decimal places.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplying the numbers of decimal places is a common misconception. Their place-value effects combine by addition, so you must count the total decimal places instead.
- subtract
  - _why:_ Subtracting decimal-place counts would apply a division-style place-value idea. In decimal multiplication, you must count all decimal places contributed by both factors.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it helpful to estimate the answer before multiplying decimals?

**Back:** To check if the decimal point in the final answer is in a sensible position.

**Choices:**

- To check if the decimal point in the final answer is in a sensible position. ✔
  - _why:_ An estimate predicts the approximate size of the product. Comparing the exact answer with that estimate helps reveal a decimal point placed too far left or right.
- To ensure the decimal places in the final answer match the first number.
  - _why:_ A product does not generally retain the first factor’s decimal places. The decimal point is positioned using the total decimal places, while estimation checks whether that position is sensible.
- To make the final answer become an exact whole number.
  - _why:_ Estimation gives an approximate value and cannot make an exact product into a whole number. Its purpose is to check whether the final decimal point is sensibly positioned.

  - _why:_ Trailing zeros do not determine whether a multiplication is accurate. Estimation instead checks the product’s approximate size and whether its decimal point is in a sensible position.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step when the divisor in a division problem is a decimal (e.g., $\div 0.4$)?

**Back:** Multiply both the divisor and dividend by the same power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number.

**Choices:**

- Multiply both the divisor and dividend by the same power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number. ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying both numbers by the same power of 10 leaves the quotient unchanged while converting the decimal divisor into a whole number, making the division easier to perform.
- Multiply only the divisor by a power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number.
  - _why:_ Changing only the divisor changes the value of the calculation. Both the divisor and dividend must be multiplied by the same power of 10 so the quotient remains unchanged.
- Round both the divisor and dividend to the nearest whole number before carrying out the division.
  - _why:_ Rounding the divisor alters the division problem and produces an approximate rather than exact result. Instead, scale both numbers equally until the divisor is a whole number.
- Divide both the divisor and dividend by the same power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number.
  - _why:_ Dividing both numbers by 10 moves the decimal point in the wrong direction and does not make a divisor such as 0.4 whole. Multiply both numbers by 10 instead.

  - _why:_ Splitting does not change 936 into a nearby multiple of 8; it remains exactly 936. The purpose is to form convenient parts that each divide exactly by 8.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you ensure you don't miss parts when using the area model or grid method for multiplication?

**Back:** Check that every part of the split numbers (hundreds, tens, and ones) has been multiplied.

**Choices:**

- Check that every part of the split numbers (hundreds, tens, and ones) has been multiplied. ✔
  - _why:_ Every place-value part of one factor must be paired with every part of the other factor. Checking all hundreds, tens and ones prevents omitted partial products.
- Check that matching parts of the split numbers (hundreds, tens, and ones) have been multiplied.
  - _why:_ Multiplying only matching place-value parts omits cross-products such as hundreds by tens. Every part of one split number must be multiplied by every part of the other.
- Check that the largest part of the split numbers (hundreds, tens, and ones) has been multiplied.
  - _why:_ Using only the largest part omits the contributions from the tens and ones, making the product too small. Every part of both split numbers must be included.
- Check that every part of the split numbers (hundreds, tens, and ones) has been added.
  - _why:_ Adding the split parts merely reconstructs the original factors and does not produce all partial products. Every part of one number must be multiplied by every relevant part.
