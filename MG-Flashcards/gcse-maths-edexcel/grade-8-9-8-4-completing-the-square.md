## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In the expansion $(x+r)^2 = x^2 + 2rx + r^2$, how is the number inside the bracket ($r$) related to the $x$ coefficient?

**Back:** It is half the coefficient of $x$.

**Choices:**

- It is half the coefficient of $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ Expanding $(x+r)^2$ gives an $x$ coefficient of $2r$. Therefore, to find $r$, divide the coefficient of $x$ by $2$.
- It is twice the coefficient of $x$.
  - _why:_ This reverses the required operation. Since the $x$ coefficient is $2r$, the number inside the bracket is half the coefficient of $x$, not twice it.
- It is equal to the coefficient of $x$.
  - _why:_ This overlooks the factor of $2$ in the middle term $2rx$. The number inside the bracket is half the coefficient of $x$.
- It is the square of the coefficient of $x$.
  - _why:_ Squaring is used to obtain the constant term $r^2$, not to find $r$. The number inside the bracket is half the coefficient of $x$.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The expansion of a perfect square bracket is $(x+r)^2 = \text{[...]}.

**Back:** The expansion of a perfect square bracket is $(x+r)^2 = \boxed{x^2+2rx+r^2}.

**Choices:**

- $$x^2+2rx+r^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying $(x+r)(x+r)$ gives $x^2+rx+rx+r^2$. Combining the two middle terms produces $x^2+2rx+r^2$.
- $$x^2+rx+r^2$
  - _why:_ This misses one of the two identical cross-products. Both $x\times r$ products contribute $rx$, so the correct expansion is $x^2+2rx+r^2$.
- $$x^2+2rx+2r^2$
  - _why:_ The cross-term occurs twice, but the product $r\times r$ occurs only once. Therefore, the correct expansion is $x^2+2rx+r^2$.
- $$x^2+r^2$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly cancels or omits the two cross-products. They combine to form $2rx$, giving the correct expansion $x^2+2rx+r^2$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** When completing the square for $x^2 + px + q$, what constant value must be subtracted to correct the expression?

**Back:** The square of half the coefficient of $x$, or $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$.

**Choices:**

- The square of half the coefficient of $x$, or $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$. ✔
  - _why:_ The bracket $(x+\frac{p}{2})^2$ introduces the constant $(\frac{p}{2})^2$. This value must be subtracted to keep the original expression unchanged.
- The half of the square of the coefficient of $x$, or $\frac{p^2}{2}$.
  - _why:_ This squares before halving, producing $\frac{p^2}{2}$ instead of $\frac{p^2}{4}$. The required correction is $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$.
- The half of the coefficient of $x$, or $\frac{p}{2}$.
  - _why:_ Half the coefficient belongs inside the bracket, but the introduced constant is its square. The value subtracted must be $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$.
- The square of the coefficient of $x$, or $p^2$.
  - _why:_ This forgets to halve the coefficient before squaring it. Completing the square introduces $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$, so that is the constant that must be subtracted.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Complete the square for the expression $x^2 + 10x$.

**Back:** $$(x+5)^2 - 25$

**Choices:**

- $$(x+5)^2 - 25$ ✔
  - _why:_ Half of $10$ is $5$, so $(x+5)^2=x^2+10x+25$. Subtracting $25$ removes the extra constant and leaves $x^2+10x$.
- $$(x+5)^2 + 25$
  - _why:_ The square already introduces an extra $25$, so adding another $25$ gives $x^2+10x+50$. The correct form subtracts $25$: $(x+5)^2 - 25$.
- $$(x+10)^2 - 100$
  - _why:_ This uses the full $x$ coefficient inside the bracket rather than halving it, producing a middle term of $20x$. The correct form is $(x+5)^2 - 25$.
- $$(x-5)^2 - 25$
  - _why:_ The negative sign produces the middle term $-10x$, not $+10x$. A positive middle term requires the completed form $(x+5)^2 - 25$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** For a quadratic in the form $y = (x+h)^2 + k$, the turning point (vertex) is $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** For a quadratic in the form $y = (x+h)^2 + k$, the turning point (vertex) is $\boxed{(-h, k)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(-h, k)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The squared term is smallest when $x+h=0$, so $x=-h$. At that value, the squared term is zero and $y=k$.
- $$(h, k)$
  - _why:_ This overlooks the sign change needed to solve $x+h=0$. The turning point occurs at $x=-h$ and $y=k$, so it is $(-h, k)$.
- $$(-h, -k)$
  - _why:_ The horizontal coordinate is correctly obtained from $x+h=0$, but the vertical coordinate is not sign-reversed. The turning point is $(-h, k)$.
- $$(h, -k)$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly reverses the vertical shift while keeping the bracket’s sign. Solving $x+h=0$ and using the outside constant gives the turning point $(-h, k)$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If a quadratic is in the form $y = (x-4)^2 - 7$, what are the coordinates of the turning point?

**Back:** $$(4, -7)$

**Choices:**

- $$(4, -7)$ ✔
  - _why:_ The squared term is zero when $x-4=0$, giving $x=4$. Substitution then gives $y=-7$, so the turning point is $(4, -7)$.
- $$(-4, -7)$
  - _why:_ Reading the bracket without reversing its sign causes this error. Since $x-4=0$ at $x=4$, the turning point is $(4, -7)$.
- $$(4, 7)$
  - _why:_ The horizontal coordinate is correct, but the outside constant gives the vertical coordinate directly. Since the constant is $-7$, the turning point is $(4, -7)$.
- $$(-4, 7)$
  - _why:_ Both signs have been reversed incorrectly. Solving $x-4=0$ gives $x=4$, while the outside constant gives $y=-7$, so the point is $(4, -7)$.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** A quadratic graph is a U-shaped curve called a [ ___ ].

**Back:** A quadratic graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola.

**Choices:**

- parabola ✔
  - _why:_ The graph of any quadratic function is called a parabola. It has one turning point and opens upwards or downwards depending on the leading coefficient.
- hyperbola
  - _why:_ A hyperbola usually has two separate branches and is associated with reciprocal-type relationships. A U-shaped quadratic graph is called a parabola.
- ellipse
  - _why:_ An ellipse is a closed oval-shaped curve rather than an open U-shaped graph. The curve formed by a quadratic function is called a parabola.
- circle
  - _why:_ A circle is a closed curve whose points are equally distant from its centre. A U-shaped quadratic graph is instead called a parabola.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you determine if a turning point is a maximum or a minimum from the completed square form $a(x+h)^2 + k$?

**Back:** If $a$ is positive, it is a minimum.If $a$ is negative, it is a maximum.

**Choices:**

- If $a$ is positive, it is a minimum.If $a$ is negative, it is a maximum. ✔
  - _why:_ A positive value of $a$ makes the parabola open upwards, so its turning point is a minimum. A negative value makes it open downwards, producing a maximum.
- If $a$ is positive, it is a maximum.If $a$ is negative, it is a minimum.
  - _why:_ This reverses the direction in which each parabola opens. Positive $a$ gives an upward-opening graph and a minimum, while negative $a$ gives a maximum.
- If $k$ is positive, it is a minimum.If $k$ is negative, it is a maximum.
  - _why:_ The value of $k$ sets the turning point’s vertical position, not whether it is a maximum or minimum. That is determined by the sign of $a$.
- If $h$ is positive, it is a minimum.If $h$ is negative, it is a maximum.
  - _why:_ The value of $h$ controls the turning point’s horizontal position, not the graph’s opening direction. Positive $a$ gives a minimum and negative $a$ gives a maximum.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** When completing the square for $x^2 - 3x$, why is the correction constant $\frac{9}{4}$?

**Back:** It is the square of half the coefficient: $\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}$.

**Choices:**

- It is the square of half the coefficient: $\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}$. ✔
  - _why:_ Half the coefficient of $x$ is $-\frac{3}{2}$. Squaring this gives $\frac{9}{4}$, which is added inside the square and then corrected outside it.
- It is half the coefficient: $-\frac{3}{2}$.
  - _why:_ This stops after halving the coefficient and forgets to square it. The correction constant is $\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}$.
- It is the square of the coefficient: $(-3)^2 = 9$.
  - _why:_ This squares the full coefficient instead of halving it first. Completing the square requires $\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2$, so the correction constant is $\frac{9}{4}$.
- It is the square of a quarter of the coefficient: $\left(-\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{16}$.
  - _why:_ This divides the coefficient by $4$ rather than by $2$. The correct procedure halves $-3$ and squares the result, giving $\frac{9}{4}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step when completing the square for an expression where the $x^2$ coefficient is not 1 (e.g., $2x^2 + 16x$)?

**Back:** Factor out the leading coefficient from the $x^2$ and $x$ terms.

**Choices:**

- Factor out the leading coefficient from the $x^2$ and $x$ terms. ✔
  - _why:_ Factoring out the leading coefficient makes the coefficient of $x^2$ inside the bracket equal to $1$, allowing the usual completing-the-square method to be applied.
- Add the leading coefficient to the $x^2$ and $x$ terms.
  - _why:_ Adding the leading coefficient changes the expression rather than rewriting it equivalently. The coefficient must be factored out from both the $x^2$ and $x$ terms first.
- Divide the $x$ coefficient by the leading coefficient without factoring.
  - _why:_ Dividing only the $x$ coefficient changes the expression because the adjustment is not applied consistently. Factor the leading coefficient out of both variable terms instead.
- Factor out the leading coefficient from the $x^2$ term only.
  - _why:_ Factoring from only the $x^2$ term does not produce a useful common bracket. The leading coefficient must be factored from both the $x^2$ and $x$ terms.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Choices:**

- $$32$ ✔

- $$16$
  - _why:_ This uses only the correction inside the bracket and ignores the outside multiplier. Multiplying the internal $16$ by $2$ gives a total correction of $32$.
- $$18$
  - _why:_ This adds the outside multiplier to the internal correction instead of multiplying them. The bracket is multiplied by $2$, so the total correction is $2 \times 16 = 32$.
- $$64$
  - _why:_ This effectively multiplies the correction by $4$ rather than by the outside factor $2$. The total constant subtracted is $2 \times 16 = 32$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** When completing the square for $2(x^2 + 8x)$, the internal correction is $-16$, but the total constant subtracted is $32$ because of the [ ___ ].

**Back:** When completing the square for $2(x^2 + 8x)$, the internal correction is $-16$, but the total constant subtracted is $32$ because of the outside multiplier.

**Choices:**

- outside multiplier ✔
  - _why:_ Every term inside $2(x^2+8x)$ is multiplied by $2$. Consequently, the internal correction of $16$ contributes a total correction of $2 \times 16 = 32$.
- internal correction
  - _why:_ The internal correction explains the value $16$, not why it becomes $32$. The doubling occurs because the entire bracket is affected by the outside multiplier $2$.
- bracket coefficient
  - _why:_ The coefficient of $x$ inside the bracket determines the internal correction of $16$. Its conversion to $32$ is specifically caused by the outside multiplier $2$.
- squared term
  - _why:_ The squared term is part of the completed-square structure, but it does not explain the doubling. The total correction is $32$ because of the outside multiplier $2$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Outline the 4 steps to solve a quadratic equation by completing the square.

**Back:** Complete the square.Isolate the squared bracket.Square root both sides (using $\pm$).Solve for $x$.

**Choices:**

- Complete the square.Isolate the squared bracket.Square root both sides (using $\pm$).Solve for $x$. ✔
  - _why:_ Completing and isolating the square produces an equation of the form $(x+h)^2=k$. Taking both square roots with $\pm$ and rearranging then gives every solution.
- Complete the square.Isolate the squared bracket.Square root both sides (positive only).Solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ Taking only the positive square root usually loses one solution. After isolating the squared bracket, use $\pm$ when square-rooting before solving for $x$.
- Factorise the quadratic.Isolate each linear factor.Set both factors equal to zero.Solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ These steps describe solving by factorisation, not completing the square. The required method forms and isolates a squared bracket, then square-roots both sides using $\pm$.
- Complete the square.Expand the squared bracket.Square both sides.Solve for $x$.
  - _why:_ Expanding reverses the completed-square form, while squaring both sides does not remove the square. Isolate the squared bracket and square-root both sides using $\pm$ instead.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is surd form?

**Back:** Leaving a result as an exact root (e.g., $\sqrt{6}$) instead of a rounded decimal.

**Choices:**

- Leaving a result as an exact root (e.g., $\sqrt{6}$) instead of a rounded decimal. ✔
  - _why:_ Surd form preserves an irrational root exactly rather than replacing it with a decimal approximation. For example, $\sqrt{6}$ is exact, while $2.45$ is rounded.
- Leaving a result as an exact fraction (e.g., $\frac{5}{2}$) instead of a rounded decimal.
  - _why:_ An exact fraction is rational and is not normally called a surd. Surd form retains an irrational root exactly, such as $\sqrt{6}$, rather than rounding it.
- Writing a result as a rounded decimal (e.g., $2.45$) instead of an exact root.
  - _why:_ This reverses the definition because a rounded decimal is only an approximation. Surd form leaves the result as an exact root, such as $\sqrt{6}$.
- Removing a result’s square root (e.g., $\sqrt{6}=6$) instead of using a decimal.
  - _why:_ A square-root symbol cannot simply be removed, since $\sqrt{6}$ is not equal to $6$. Surd form keeps the exact root unchanged rather than evaluating it approximately.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** When solving $(x+h)^2 = k$ by taking a square root, you must include a $\text{[...]}$ sign to ensure you find both roots.

**Back:** When solving $(x+h)^2 = k$ by taking a square root, you must include a $\boxed{\pm}$ sign to ensure you find both roots.

**Choices:**

- $$\pm$ ✔
  - _why:_ A positive number has both a positive and a negative square root. Including $\pm$ therefore captures both values of $x+h$ and ensures both roots are found.
- $$+$
  - _why:_ Using only the positive sign discards the negative square root and can lose one solution. The correct sign is $\pm$, representing both possible square roots of $k$.
- $$-$
  - _why:_ Using only the negative sign discards the positive square root and can lose one solution. The correct sign is $\pm$, so both square-root possibilities are considered.
- $$\times$
  - _why:_ A multiplication sign does not represent the two possible square roots of a positive number. The $\pm$ sign is required to include both the positive and negative roots.

**Front:** Rewrite $x^2 - 8x + 9$ in completed square form.

**Back:** $$(x-4)^2 - 7$

**Choices:**

- $$(x-4)^2 - 7$ ✔
  - _why:_ Half of $-8$ is $-4$, so $(x-4)^2=x^2-8x+16$. Subtracting $7$ changes the constant from $16$ to $9$, giving the original expression.
- $$(x-4)^2 + 7$
  - _why:_ The sign of the outside constant has been reversed. Since $(x-4)^2$ contributes $16$, subtracting $7$ is required to obtain the original constant term of $9$.
- $$(x-4)^2 - 9$
  - _why:_ This subtracts the original constant rather than correcting the added $16$. The required adjustment is $9-16=-7$, so the completed form is $(x-4)^2-7$.
- $$(x-8)^2 - 55$
  - _why:_ Using $-8$ inside the bracket fails to halve the coefficient of $x$, producing a linear term of $-16x$. Half of $-8$ is $-4$, giving $(x-4)^2-7$.
