## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is capture recapture used to estimate?

**Back:** A large population size when individuals cannot be counted directly.

**Choices:**

- A large population size when individuals cannot be counted directly. ✔
  - _why:_ Capture recapture uses the proportion of marked individuals found in a later sample to estimate the size of a population that cannot feasibly be counted directly.
- An exact population size when individuals cannot be counted directly.
  - _why:_ Capture recapture produces an estimate rather than an exact count because its samples may not perfectly represent the population. The correct result is an estimated large population size.
- A large population mean when individual values cannot be measured directly.
  - _why:_ A population mean is estimated using representative measurements, not recaptures. Capture recapture instead estimates the size of a large population whose individuals cannot be counted directly.
- A large population growth rate when changes cannot be observed directly.
  - _why:_ Population growth requires measurements from different times and is not found from one recapture calculation. Capture recapture estimates a large population's size when direct counting is impractical.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Back:** Capture recapture relies on the assumption that the fraction of marked items in the second sample is the same as the fraction in the whole population.

**Choices:**

- fraction ✔
  - _why:_ The method assumes that marked individuals are mixed randomly, so the fraction marked in the second sample represents the fraction marked throughout the whole population.
- number
  - _why:_ The raw number marked depends on sample size and is not expected to match a population count. It is the fraction marked that should be representative.
- difference
  - _why:_ Capture recapture does not compare a subtraction between marked and unmarked groups. It assumes the fraction of marked items in the sample matches that in the population.
- product
  - _why:_ Multiplying quantities of marked items does not express their prevalence. The representative quantity is the fraction marked, calculated relative to the total number sampled.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** State the capture recapture formula for estimated population size.

**Back:** $$$\frac{M \times S}{R}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{M \times S}{R}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The proportion assumption gives $\frac{R}{S}=\frac{M}{N}$. Rearranging for the estimated population $N$ produces $N=\frac{M\times S}{R}$.
- $$$\frac{M + S}{R}$$
  - _why:_ The two sample sizes must be multiplied, not added, when rearranging the proportion $\frac{R}{S}=\frac{M}{N}$. The correct formula is $\frac{M\times S}{R}$.
- $$$\frac{M \times R}{S}$$
  - _why:_ This swaps the second-sample size and the number recaptured. Recaptures must be the denominator, so the estimated population is $\frac{M\times S}{R}$.
- $$$\frac{S \times R}{M}$$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly divides by the number initially marked and multiplies by recaptures. The number recaptured belongs in the denominator, giving $\frac{M\times S}{R}$.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** In the formula $$\frac{M \times S}{R}$$, what do the variables M and S represent?

**Back:** M: Number marked in the first sample.S: Total size of the second sample.

**Choices:**

- M: Number marked in the first sample.S: Total size of the second sample. ✔
  - _why:_ $$M$ records how many individuals were marked initially, while $S$ is the full number examined in the later sample. These are multiplied in the numerator.
- M: Total size of the second sample.S: Number marked in the first sample.
  - _why:_ This reverses the roles of $M$ and $S$. $M$ is the number marked in the first sample, while $S$ is the total size of the second sample.
- M: Number recaptured in the second sample.S: Total size of the first sample.
  - _why:_ Recaptures are represented by $R$, not $M$, and $S$ concerns the second sample rather than the first. $M$ is initially marked and $S$ is sampled second.
- M: Total size of the whole population.S: Number marked in the second sample.
  - _why:_ The entire population is the unknown being estimated, and marked individuals in the second sample are counted by $R$. $M$ is initially marked and $S$ is the second-sample total.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** In the formula $$\frac{M \times S}{R}$$, what does R represent?

**Back:** Number of marked individuals found in the second sample (recaptures).

**Choices:**

- Number of marked individuals found in the second sample (recaptures). ✔
  - _why:_ $$R$ counts the recaptures: individuals marked during the first capture that are encountered again within the second sample. It forms the denominator of the formula.
- Number of unmarked individuals found in the second sample (new captures).
  - _why:_ Unmarked individuals in the second sample are new captures, not recaptures. $R$ specifically counts marked individuals found again in the second sample.
- Total number of all individuals found in the second sample (sample size).
  - _why:_ The total size of the second sample is $S$, not $R$. $R$ counts only those individuals in that sample that carry the original mark.
- Number of marked individuals found in the first sample (initial captures).
  - _why:_ The number marked in the first sample is $M$, not $R$. $R$ is the subset of marked individuals encountered again in the second sample.

  - _why:_ The recaptured marked individuals provide the overlap between the two samples. Their number is $R$, which divides the product $M\times S$ in the formula.

  - _why:_ The initially marked individuals are represented by $M$ and belong in the numerator. The denominator is the number of recaptured marked individuals, represented by $R$.
- unmarked sampled
  - _why:_ Unmarked sampled individuals are new captures and are not used as the denominator. The denominator counts recaptured marked individuals because they link the two samples.
- newly captured
  - _why:_ Newly captured individuals have no original mark and therefore do not measure the overlap between samples. The denominator must be the recaptured marked individuals.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If 1/5 of the second sample is marked, and 60 were originally marked, how is the population estimated?

**Back:** Multiply the original number by 5 ($$60 \times 5 = 300$$).

**Choices:**

- Multiply the original number by 5 ($$60 \times 5 = 300$$). ✔
  - _why:_ If marked individuals make up one fifth of the second sample, the original 60 marked individuals represent one fifth of the population. Therefore, $60 \times 5 = 300$.
- Multiply the original number by 4 ($$60 \times 4 = 240$$).
  - _why:_ Using 4 counts only the remaining fifths and omits the fifth represented by the marked animals. There are five fifths altogether, so the estimate is $60 \times 5 = 300$.
- Multiply the original number by 6 ($$60 \times 6 = 360$$).
  - _why:_ Using 6 is an off-by-one error that adds an extra fifth. Since 60 represents one of five equal parts, the population estimate is $60 \times 5 = 300$.
- Divide the original number by 5 ($$60 \div 5 = 12$$).
  - _why:_ Dividing reverses the required calculation and finds one fifth of 60. Since 60 marked individuals represent one fifth of the population, multiply by 5 to obtain 300.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** Give two assumptions regarding the movement and size of the population.

**Back:** The marked animals mix evenly back into the population.The population size is constant (no births, deaths, or migration).

**Choices:**

- The marked animals mix evenly back into the population.The population size is constant (no births, deaths, or migration). ✔
  - _why:_ Even mixing gives every animal a comparable chance of entering the second sample, while a constant population prevents births, deaths, immigration or emigration from changing the proportions.
- The marked animals stay together within the population.The population size increases (due to births or immigration).
  - _why:_ Marked animals staying together would make them overrepresented or underrepresented in the second sample, while population growth changes the proportions. They must mix evenly in a constant population.
- The marked animals leave immediately after release.The population size decreases (due to deaths or migration).
  - _why:_ Leaving the population prevents marked animals from being recaptured, and a shrinking population alters the marked fraction. The marked animals must mix evenly while population size remains constant.
- The marked animals avoid unmarked members of the population.The population size may vary (with births, deaths, or migration).
  - _why:_ Separating marked animals violates the equal-mixing assumption, and allowing unrestricted population change invalidates the comparison. Marked animals must mix evenly and population size must remain constant.

**Back:** A key assumption for a fair estimate is that the second sample taken is random.

**Choices:**

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** 70 birds are marked. Later, 50 birds are caught and 10 are marked. Calculate the population estimate.

**Back:** $$$\frac{70 \times 50}{10} = 350$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{70 \times 50}{10} = 350$$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiply the number initially marked by the size of the second sample, then divide by the 10 marked birds recaptured: $70 \times 50 \div 10=350$.
- $$$\frac{70 \times 10}{50} = 14$$
  - _why:_ This swaps the second-sample size and the recaptured number in the formula. The correct calculation is $70 \times 50 \div 10=350$ birds.
- $$$70 \times 50 = 3500$$
  - _why:_ This omits division by the 10 marked birds recaptured, making the estimate ten times too large. The full calculation gives $70 \times 50 \div 10=350$.
- $$$70 + 50 - 10 = 110$$
  - _why:_ Adding the samples and subtracting recaptures treats capture-recapture like a simple total. The proportional formula is $70 \times 50 \div 10=350$ birds.
