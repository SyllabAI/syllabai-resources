## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In transformations, what is the name for the starting shape?

**Back:** The object.

**Choices:**

- The object. ✔
  - _why:_ The object is the original shape before any transformation is applied; the resulting transformed shape is called the image.
- The image.
  - _why:_ The image is the shape produced by a transformation, not the starting shape. The original shape before transformation is called the object.
- The vector.
  - _why:_ A vector describes the direction and distance of a translation; it does not name a shape. The starting shape is called the object.
- The centre.
  - _why:_ A centre may identify the fixed point of a rotation or enlargement, rather than the starting shape. The starting shape is called the object.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In transformations, what is the name for the shape after it has been moved or resized?

**Back:** The image.

**Choices:**

- The image. ✔
  - _why:_ The image is the resulting shape after the object has been translated, reflected, rotated or enlarged by a transformation.
- The object.
  - _why:_ The object is the original shape before the transformation takes place. The shape produced after it has been transformed is called the image.
- The vector.
  - _why:_ A vector specifies the movement in a translation, rather than naming the transformed shape. The resulting shape is called the image.
- The centre.
  - _why:_ A centre is a point used to define a rotation or enlargement, not the transformed shape. The shape produced is called the image.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** In a translation vector, what movement does a negative top number indicate?

**Back:** Movement to the left.

**Choices:**

- Movement to the left. ✔
  - _why:_ The top number of a translation vector controls horizontal movement, and a negative value means movement to the left.
- Movement to the right.
  - _why:_ A positive top number represents movement to the right. A negative top number reverses the horizontal direction, so it indicates movement to the left.
- Movement to the up.
  - _why:_ Vertical movement is controlled by the bottom number, not the top number. A negative top number represents horizontal movement to the left.
- Movement to the down.
  - _why:_ A negative bottom number would indicate movement down. The top number controls horizontal movement, so a negative top number means left.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** In a translation vector, what movement does a positive bottom number indicate?

**Back:** Movement up.

**Choices:**

- Movement up. ✔
  - _why:_ The bottom number of a translation vector controls vertical movement, and a positive value means movement upwards.
- Movement down.
  - _why:_ A negative bottom number represents downward movement. Since the bottom number is positive, the translation instead moves vertically upwards.
- Movement right.
  - _why:_ Horizontal movement is determined by the top number of the vector. A positive bottom number controls vertical movement and therefore means up.
- Movement left.
  - _why:_ Movement to the left is represented by a negative top number. A positive bottom number instead indicates vertical movement upwards.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What detail is required to describe a translation fully?

**Back:** A vector $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$.

**Choices:**

- A vector $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$. ✔
  - _why:_ A translation is fully described by a column vector, where $x$ gives horizontal movement and $y$ gives vertical movement.
- A coordinate $(x, y)$.
  - _why:_ A coordinate locates one point and does not describe how every point moves. A translation requires a vector giving horizontal and vertical displacement.
- A scale factor $k$.
  - _why:_ A scale factor is required for an enlargement, not a translation. A translation is fully specified by a vector showing horizontal and vertical movement.
- A line $y=mx+c$.
  - _why:_ A line is needed to describe the mirror line of a reflection, not a translation. A translation must be described using a vector.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When reflecting a point in the $x$-axis, how do the coordinates change?

**Back:** The $y$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1).

**Choices:**

- The $y$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1). ✔
  - _why:_ Reflection in the $x$-axis reverses a point’s vertical position, so the $y$-coordinate changes sign while the $x$-coordinate remains unchanged.
- The $x$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1).
  - _why:_ Changing the $x$-coordinate reflects a point in the $y$-axis. Reflection in the $x$-axis instead changes the sign of the $y$-coordinate.
- The $x$- and $y$-coordinates change sign (multiplied by -1).
  - _why:_ Changing both signs gives a half-turn about the origin, not a reflection in the $x$-axis. Only the $y$-coordinate changes sign.
- The $y$-coordinate stays unchanged (multiplied by 1).
  - _why:_ Leaving the $y$-coordinate unchanged would not reverse the point’s vertical position. Reflection in the $x$-axis changes the sign of the $y$-coordinate.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** When reflecting a point in the $y$-axis, how do the coordinates change?

**Back:** The $x$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1).

**Choices:**

- The $x$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1). ✔
  - _why:_ Reflection in the $y$-axis reverses a point’s horizontal position, so the $x$-coordinate changes sign while the $y$-coordinate remains unchanged.
- The $y$-coordinate changes sign (multiplied by -1).
  - _why:_ Changing the $y$-coordinate reflects a point in the $x$-axis. Reflection in the $y$-axis instead changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate.
- The $x$- and $y$-coordinates change sign (multiplied by -1).
  - _why:_ Changing both coordinate signs produces a rotation of $180°$ about the origin. Reflection in the $y$-axis changes only the $x$-coordinate’s sign.
- The $x$-coordinate stays unchanged (multiplied by 1).
  - _why:_ Leaving the $x$-coordinate unchanged would not reverse the point’s horizontal position. Reflection in the $y$-axis changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coordinate rule for a reflection in the line $y=x$?

**Back:** $$(x, y) \to (y, x)$ (swap the coordinates).

**Choices:**

- $$(x, y) \to (y, x)$ (swap the coordinates). ✔
  - _why:_ The line $y=x$ treats the horizontal and vertical directions symmetrically, so reflection in this line swaps the $x$- and $y$-coordinates.
- $$(x, y) \to (-x, y)$ (change the sign of $x$).
  - _why:_ Changing only the sign of $x$ is the rule for reflection in the $y$-axis. Reflection in $y=x$ swaps the two coordinates.
- $$(x, y) \to (x, -y)$ (change the sign of $y$).
  - _why:_ Changing only the sign of $y$ is the rule for reflection in the $x$-axis. Reflection in $y=x$ instead swaps the coordinates.
- $$(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$ (swap and change the signs).
  - _why:_ Swapping both coordinates and changing their signs reflects a point in $y=-x$. Reflection in $y=x$ swaps the coordinates without changing their signs.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a reflection, corresponding vertices are the [ ___ ] from the mirror line, on opposite sides.

**Back:** In a reflection, corresponding vertices are the same distance from the mirror line, on opposite sides.

**Choices:**

- same distance ✔
  - _why:_ A reflection places each image vertex the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line as its corresponding object vertex, preserving symmetry across the line.
- double distance
  - _why:_ Doubling the distance describes a change in size rather than a reflection. Corresponding vertices must be the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line.
- half distance
  - _why:_ Halving the distance would move the image closer to the mirror line and break reflection symmetry. The two corresponding vertices must be the same distance away.
- different distances
  - _why:_ Unequal distances from the mirror line do not produce corresponding reflected points. Each object vertex and its image must be the same perpendicular distance from the line.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a reflection, corresponding vertices are the same distance from the mirror line, on [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a reflection, corresponding vertices are the same distance from the mirror line, on opposite sides.

**Choices:**

- opposite sides ✔
  - _why:_ The mirror line lies between each object vertex and its corresponding image vertex, so the two points are equally distant from it on opposite sides.
- the same side
  - _why:_ Placing both vertices on the same side does not create a reflection across the line. Corresponding vertices must lie on opposite sides of the mirror line.
- one side only
  - _why:_ A reflection crosses the mirror line rather than keeping both corresponding vertices on one side. They must be equally distant on opposite sides of the line.
- either side at random
  - _why:_ The side is not chosen randomly in a reflection. Each image vertex must be positioned on the opposite side of the mirror line from its object vertex.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What three details are required to describe a rotation fully?

**Back:** Angle (e.g. $90^{\circ}$)Direction (clockwise or anticlockwise)Centre of rotation

**Choices:**

- Angle (e.g. $90^{\circ}$)Direction (clockwise or anticlockwise)Centre of rotation ✔
  - _why:_ A rotation is uniquely determined by how far it turns, which way it turns, and the fixed point about which the turn is made.
- Angle (e.g. $90^{\circ}$)Direction (clockwise or anticlockwise)Mirror line
  - _why:_ A mirror line belongs to a reflection, not a rotation. A rotation requires an angle, a clockwise or anticlockwise direction, and a centre of rotation.
- Angle (e.g. $90^{\circ}$)Scale factorCentre of rotation
  - _why:_ Scale factor describes an enlargement and does not specify which way a shape turns. The missing detail is the clockwise or anticlockwise direction.
- Direction (clockwise or anticlockwise)Centre of rotationTranslation vector
  - _why:_ A translation vector describes movement without turning and cannot replace the rotation angle. The required details are angle, direction, and centre of rotation.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coordinate rule for a $180^{\circ}$ rotation about the origin?

**Back:** $$(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$.

**Choices:**

- $$(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$. ✔
  - _why:_ A $180^{\circ}$ rotation sends each point to the position directly opposite through the origin, so both the $x$-coordinate and $y$-coordinate change sign.
- $$(x, y) \to (-y, x)$.
  - _why:_ Swapping the coordinates and negating the original $y$-coordinate gives a $90^{\circ}$ anticlockwise rotation. For $180^{\circ}$, both coordinates simply change sign.
- $$(x, y) \to (y, -x)$.
  - _why:_ This rule represents a $90^{\circ}$ clockwise rotation about the origin. A $180^{\circ}$ rotation instead maps the point to $(-x,-y)$.
- $$(x, y) \to (-x, y)$.
  - _why:_ Changing only the sign of $x$ reflects a point in the $y$-axis. A $180^{\circ}$ rotation changes the signs of both coordinates.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the coordinate rule for a $90^{\circ}$ anticlockwise rotation about the origin?

**Back:** $$(x, y) \to (-y, x)$.

**Choices:**

- $$(x, y) \to (-y, x)$. ✔
  - _why:_ For a $90^{\circ}$ anticlockwise rotation about the origin, the coordinates swap positions and the original $y$-coordinate becomes negative.
- $$(x, y) \to (y, -x)$.
  - _why:_ This coordinate rule turns points $90^{\circ}$ clockwise, reversing the required direction. Anticlockwise rotation uses $(-y,x)$ instead.
- $$(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$.
  - _why:_ Negating both coordinates sends a point to the opposite side of the origin, which is a $180^{\circ}$ rotation rather than a $90^{\circ}$ anticlockwise rotation.
- $$(x, y) \to (-x, y)$.
  - _why:_ Negating only the $x$-coordinate reflects the point in the $y$-axis. A $90^{\circ}$ anticlockwise rotation swaps the coordinates and gives $(-y,x)$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What two details are required to describe an enlargement fully?

**Back:** Scale factorCentre of enlargement

**Choices:**

- Scale factorCentre of enlargement ✔
  - _why:_ The scale factor determines the change in size, while the centre of enlargement determines the point from which corresponding vertices are projected.
- Scale factorDirection
  - _why:_ Clockwise or anticlockwise direction is needed for a rotation, not an enlargement. An enlargement is fully described by its scale factor and centre of enlargement.
- Scale factorAngle
  - _why:_ An angle describes the amount of turning in a rotation and does not locate an enlargement. The second required detail is the centre of enlargement.
- Centre of enlargementTranslation vector
  - _why:_ A translation vector describes a fixed movement and gives no change in size. An enlargement requires its centre of enlargement and its scale factor.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** For an enlargement with scale factor $k$ and centre $O$, the coordinates of the image are found by [ ___ ] the object coordinates by $k$.

**Back:** For an enlargement with scale factor $k$ and centre $O$, the coordinates of the image are found by multiplying the object coordinates by $k$.

**Choices:**

- multiplying ✔
  - _why:_ With the centre at the origin $O$, multiplying each coordinate by $k$ scales its distance and direction from the origin by the required scale factor.
- dividing
  - _why:_ Dividing by $k$ applies the reciprocal scale factor $\frac{1}{k}$ rather than $k$. The object coordinates must be multiplied by $k$.
- adding
  - _why:_ Adding $k$ to coordinates produces a translation-like shift and does not scale distances proportionally. An enlargement about the origin is found by multiplying coordinates by $k$.
- subtracting
  - _why:_ Subtracting $k$ changes coordinates by a fixed amount instead of scaling their distances from the origin. The coordinates must be multiplied by $k$.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** For an enlargement with scale factor $k$ and centre $O$, the coordinates of the image are found by multiplying the object coordinates by $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** For an enlargement with scale factor $k$ and centre $O$, the coordinates of the image are found by multiplying the object coordinates by $\boxed{k}$.

**Choices:**

- $$k$ ✔
  - _why:_ The scale factor $k$ multiplies each coordinate, so every point’s distance from the centre of enlargement is scaled by the same factor.
- $$k^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring the scale factor confuses length scale factor with area scale factor. Coordinates represent lengths, so they are multiplied by $k$, not $k^2$.
- $$\frac{1}{k}$
  - _why:_ Using the reciprocal reverses the intended enlargement and applies scale factor $\frac{1}{k}$. To perform an enlargement with scale factor $k$, multiply by $k$.
- $$-k$
  - _why:_ The negative sign would place image points on the opposite side of the centre as well as scaling them. The stated scale factor is $k$, so coordinates use $k$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Which transformation changes the size of a shape while keeping it the same proportions?

**Back:** An enlargement.

**Choices:**

- An enlargement. ✔
  - _why:_ An enlargement changes a shape’s dimensions by a scale factor while preserving its proportions, so the image remains mathematically similar to the original shape.
- A translation.
  - _why:_ A translation moves every point by the same vector without changing the shape’s size. An enlargement changes size while preserving the shape’s proportions.
- A reflection.
  - _why:_ A reflection flips a shape across a mirror line but leaves its dimensions unchanged. An enlargement is the transformation that changes size while preserving proportions.
- A rotation.
  - _why:_ A rotation turns a shape about a centre without altering its dimensions. An enlargement changes the dimensions by a scale factor while maintaining the same proportions.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the order of combined transformations important?

**Back:** Performing transformations in a different order can result in a different final image.

**Choices:**

- Performing transformations in a different order can result in a different final image. ✔
  - _why:_ Many transformations are not commutative, meaning that reversing their order can move points to different positions and therefore produce a different final image.
- Performing transformations in a different order always results in the same final image.
  - _why:_ It is tempting to assume transformations commute, but many do not. Changing their order can move points to different positions and produce a different final image.
- Performing transformations in a different order only changes the names of the vertices.
  - _why:_ The order can affect the actual positions of the transformed vertices, not merely their labels. A different order can therefore produce a different final image.
- Performing transformations in a different order only changes the description of the method.
  - _why:_ Reordering transformations does more than alter how the method is described. Because many transformations do not commute, it can produce a different final image.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the equation for the mirror line if it is the $y$-axis?

**Back:** $$x = 0$.

**Choices:**

- $$x = 0$. ✔
  - _why:_ Every point on the $y$-axis has an $x$-coordinate of zero, so the equation of this vertical mirror line is $x = 0$.
- $$y = 0$.
  - _why:_ This is the equation of the $x$-axis, a common axis-label reversal. Every point on the $y$-axis instead satisfies $x = 0$.
- $$x = 1$.
  - _why:_ This describes a vertical line one unit to the right of the $y$-axis. The $y$-axis passes through points whose $x$-coordinate is zero, so its equation is $x = 0$.
- $$y = x$.
  - _why:_ This describes a diagonal line through the origin, not the vertical $y$-axis. The equation of the $y$-axis is $x = 0$.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** The point where the $x$-axis and $y$-axis intersect is called the [ ___ ] and has coordinates $(0,0)$.

**Back:** The point where the $x$-axis and $y$-axis intersect is called the origin and has coordinates $(0,0)$.

**Choices:**

- origin ✔
  - _why:_ The origin is the unique point where the two coordinate axes intersect. Its $x$-coordinate and $y$-coordinate are both zero, giving $(0,0)$.
- midpoint
  - _why:_ A midpoint is halfway between two specified points, so it does not necessarily lie where the axes meet. Their intersection at $(0,0)$ is called the origin.
- intercept
  - _why:_ An intercept is where a graph crosses an axis, rather than the name of the axes’ intersection. The $x$-axis and $y$-axis meet at the origin.
- centre
  - _why:_ Although the axes may appear centred on a coordinate grid, their intersection has the specific mathematical name origin and has coordinates $(0,0)$.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** The point where the $x$-axis and $y$-axis intersect is called the origin and has coordinates $\text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The point where the $x$-axis and $y$-axis intersect is called the origin and has coordinates $\boxed{(0,0)}$.

**Choices:**

- $$(0,0)$ ✔
  - _why:_ At the origin, the point is neither horizontally nor vertically displaced from the axes’ intersection. Therefore both coordinates are zero, giving $(0,0)$.
- $$(0,1)$
  - _why:_ This point lies on the $y$-axis one unit above the origin. The axes themselves intersect where both coordinates are zero, at $(0,0)$.
- $$(1,0)$
  - _why:_ This point lies on the $x$-axis one unit to the right of the origin. The intersection of both axes has coordinates $(0,0)$.
- $$(1,1)$
  - _why:_ This point is one unit right and one unit above the axes’ intersection. At the origin, both coordinates are zero, so the coordinates are $(0,0)$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** If a vertex moves from $(-2, -1)$ to $(4, 2)$, what is the translation vector?

**Back:** $$\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$. ✔
  - _why:_ Subtract the starting coordinates from the final coordinates: $4-(-2)=6$ horizontally and $2-(-1)=3$ vertically, giving the translation vector $\begin{pmatrix}6\\3\end{pmatrix}$.
- $$\begin{pmatrix} -6 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$.
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction and gives the vector for moving from the final point back to the start. Final minus initial gives $\begin{pmatrix}6\\3\end{pmatrix}$.
- $$\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
  - _why:_ These are the destination coordinates, not the change in position. Subtracting the initial coordinates from them gives $4-(-2)=6$ and $2-(-1)=3$.
- $$\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
  - _why:_ This comes from adding the negative starting coordinates rather than subtracting them. Subtracting a negative gives $4-(-2)=6$ and $2-(-1)=3$.
