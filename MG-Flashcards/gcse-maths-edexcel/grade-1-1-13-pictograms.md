## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a tally chart, tally marks are usually grouped in [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a tally chart, tally marks are usually grouped in fives.

**Choices:**

- fives ✔
  - _why:_ Tally marks are grouped in fives: four vertical marks are crossed by a fifth mark. This makes larger frequencies quicker and easier to count.
- fours
  - _why:_ Groups of four may be confused with the four vertical strokes, but the diagonal fifth stroke completes each standard tally group. Tally marks are grouped in fives.
- tens
  - _why:_ Ten is often used when counting, but it is not the standard tally grouping. Tally marks are grouped in fives, with each fifth mark crossing the previous four.
- pairs
  - _why:_ Pairs would allow counting in twos, but standard tally notation uses groups of five. Four separate marks are crossed by a fifth mark to complete each group.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** If the frequency is 13, what should the tally marks count to?

**Back:** 13.

**Choices:**

- 13. ✔
  - _why:_ Frequency gives the total number of observations, so the tally marks must count to exactly 13. This is usually two complete groups of five followed by three marks.
- 12.
  - _why:_ Stopping at 12 creates an off-by-one error. The number of tally marks must equal the frequency, so a frequency of 13 requires exactly 13 tally marks.
- 15.
  - _why:_ Completing three full groups of five would give 15, which exceeds the stated frequency. The tally marks must stop at 13, usually shown as two groups of five and three more.
- 14.
  - _why:_ Counting one extra tally produces 14 rather than the stated frequency. A frequency of 13 must be represented by exactly 13 tally marks.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** A table is called a tally chart, but its tally column is empty. What is missing?

**Back:** Tally marks.

**Choices:**

- Tally marks. ✔
  - _why:_ A tally chart must contain tally marks in its tally column. These marks record each observation and can then be counted to calculate the frequency.
- Frequency numbers.
  - _why:_ Frequency numbers belong in the frequency column and show totals after counting. An empty tally column is specifically missing tally marks, which record observations as they occur.
- Pictogram symbols.
  - _why:_ Pictogram symbols are used in pictograms rather than tally charts. The empty tally column should contain tally marks, normally arranged in groups of five.
- Category headings.
  - _why:_ Category headings label the rows or categories, but they do not fill the tally column. That column is missing tally marks used to record each observation.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a pictogram, the key tells you what each symbol [ ___ ].

**Back:** In a pictogram, the key tells you what each symbol represents.

**Choices:**

- represents ✔
  - _why:_ The key states the number or quantity represented by each symbol. This information is needed to convert the symbols into the actual frequency.
- costs
  - _why:_ A pictogram key does not normally give the price of each symbol. It states what quantity each symbol represents, allowing the displayed data to be interpreted correctly.
- contains
  - _why:_ A symbol does not physically contain the data items shown by a pictogram. The key explains what number or quantity each symbol represents.
- weighs
  - _why:_ The key is not describing a symbol’s physical weight. It tells the reader what quantity each symbol represents so the pictogram can be interpreted.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** Why check the key before counting symbols?

**Back:** A symbol may represent more than one item.

**Choices:**

- A symbol may represent more than one item. ✔
  - _why:_ The key gives the value of each symbol, which may be two, five or another number of items. Counting symbols alone could therefore give the wrong frequency.
- A symbol always represents exactly one item.
  - _why:_ Assuming one symbol equals one item ignores the scale and can produce an incorrect total. The key must be checked because a symbol may represent more than one item.
- A symbol must always be counted in pairs.
  - _why:_ Symbols are not automatically counted in pairs; their value depends on the stated key. The key is checked because one symbol may represent more than one item.
- A symbol has a different value in every row.
  - _why:_ A pictogram normally uses the same key throughout, rather than changing the symbol’s value by row. The key matters because each symbol may represent several items.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Key: 1 circle = 2 badges. A row has 3 circles. How many badges?

**Back:** 6 badges.

**Choices:**

- 6 badges. ✔
  - _why:_ Each of the three circles represents two badges. Multiplying the number of circles by the key value gives $3 \times 2 = 6$ badges.
- 3 badges.
  - _why:_ This counts the circles but ignores the key’s value of two badges per circle. Multiplying three circles by two gives the correct total of 6 badges.
- 12 badges.
  - _why:_ This doubles the result a second time after already applying the key. Three circles multiplied by two badges per circle gives 6 badges, not 12.
- 5 badges.
  - _why:_ Adding the three circles to the key value gives five, but pictogram values must be multiplied. Three circles at two badges each represent 6 badges.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** If one full symbol represents 8 items, half a symbol represents [ ___ ] items.

**Back:** If one full symbol represents 8 items, half a symbol represents 4 items.

**Choices:**

- 4 ✔
  - _why:_ A half-symbol has half the value of a full symbol. Therefore, $8 \div 2 = 4$, so half a symbol represents 4 items.
- 2
  - _why:_ Two is one quarter of eight, so this comes from dividing by four instead of two. Half of eight is $8 \div 2 = 4$ items.
- 8
  - _why:_ Eight is the value of a full symbol, so it ignores that only half is shown. Dividing the full value by two gives 4 items.
- 16
  - _why:_ Multiplying by two gives the value of two full symbols, not half a symbol. A half-symbol requires dividing eight by two, giving 4 items.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** If one full symbol represents 8 items, what does a quarter-symbol represent?

**Back:** 2 items.

**Choices:**

- 2 items. ✔
  - _why:_ A quarter-symbol represents one quarter of the full symbol’s value. Dividing eight by four gives $8 \div 4 = 2$ items.
- 4 items.
  - _why:_ Four is half of eight, so this treats the quarter-symbol as a half-symbol. A quarter of eight is $8 \div 4 = 2$ items.
- 8 items.
  - _why:_ Eight is the value of a complete symbol, so this ignores that only one quarter is shown. Dividing eight by four gives 2 items.
- 32 items.
  - _why:_ Multiplying eight by four gives the value of four full symbols, not a quarter-symbol. A quarter requires division by four, producing 2 items.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If one full symbol represents 8 items, what do three-quarters of a symbol represent?

**Back:** 6 items.

**Choices:**

- 6 items. ✔
  - _why:_ Three-quarters of 8 is found by calculating $8 \times \frac{3}{4}=6$, so three-quarters of the pictogram symbol represents 6 items.
- 4 items.
  - _why:_ This treats three-quarters of a symbol as one-half. Since a full symbol represents 8 items, three-quarters represents $8 \times \frac{3}{4}=6$ items.
- 8 items.
  - _why:_ This uses the value of a full symbol and ignores that only three-quarters is shown. The correct calculation is $8 \times \frac{3}{4}=6$ items.
- 12 items.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly adds half of 8 to the full value. Three-quarters means multiplying 8 by $\frac{3}{4}$, giving 6 items.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Key: 1 circle = 8 pizzas. A row has 1 full circle and half a circle. How many pizzas?

**Back:** 12 pizzas.

**Choices:**

- 12 pizzas. ✔
  - _why:_ The full circle represents 8 pizzas and the half-circle represents 4 pizzas. Adding these gives $8+4=12$ pizzas altogether.
- 8 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This counts only the full circle and ignores the half-circle. Half of 8 is 4, so the row represents $8+4=12$ pizzas.
- 16 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This treats the half-circle as another full circle. It represents only 4 pizzas, so the total is $8+4=12$ pizzas.
- 10 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly interprets the half-circle as 2 pizzas. A half-circle represents half of 8, which is 4, making the total 12 pizzas.

## Card 11 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a row shows 2½ symbols, multiply by the [ ___ ] rather than reading it as 2½ items.

**Back:** If a row shows 2½ symbols, multiply by the key rather than reading it as 2½ items.

**Choices:**

- key ✔
  - _why:_ The key states the number of items represented by each symbol. Therefore, the number of symbols must be multiplied by the key’s value.
- frequency
  - _why:_ The frequency is the result represented by the pictogram row, not the multiplier. Multiply 2½ symbols by the key to calculate the frequency.
- category
  - _why:_ A category labels the group being counted and has no numerical multiplier. The number of symbols must instead be multiplied by the key.
- total
  - _why:_ The total is obtained after interpreting the symbols, so multiplying by it would be circular. The correct multiplier is the value given in the key.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Key: 1 circle = 2 ice creams. How should 6 ice creams be drawn?

**Back:** 3 full circles.

**Choices:**

- 3 full circles. ✔
  - _why:_ Each full circle represents 2 ice creams. Dividing 6 by 2 gives 3, so 6 ice creams should be shown using 3 full circles.
- 6 full circles.
  - _why:_ This draws one circle per ice cream and ignores the key. Since each circle represents 2 ice creams, only $6 \div 2=3$ full circles are needed.
- 2 full circles.
  - _why:_ Two full circles would represent only $2 \times 2=4$ ice creams. Six ice creams require $6 \div 2=3$ full circles.
- 3 half-circles.
  - _why:_ Each half-circle represents 1 ice cream, so three half-circles represent only 3 ice creams. Six ice creams should be drawn as 3 full circles.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** When one symbol represents 2, an odd frequency usually needs a [ ___ ].

**Back:** When one symbol represents 2, an odd frequency usually needs a half-symbol.

**Choices:**

- half-symbol ✔
  - _why:_ If a full symbol represents 2, half a symbol represents 1. An odd frequency can therefore be shown using full symbols plus one half-symbol.
- double-symbol
  - _why:_ A double-symbol would represent 4, so it cannot supply the extra single item needed for an odd frequency. A half-symbol represents 1 and is required.
- quarter-symbol
  - _why:_ A quarter-symbol would represent $\frac{1}{2}$ when a full symbol represents 2, not one whole item. An odd frequency usually requires a half-symbol.
- full symbol
  - _why:_ Full symbols each contribute 2, so any collection of them gives an even frequency. A half-symbol contributes 1, allowing an odd frequency to be represented.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Key: 1 circle = 2 ice creams. How should 9 ice creams be drawn?

**Back:** 4 full circles and half a circle.

**Choices:**

- 4 full circles and half a circle. ✔
  - _why:_ Four full circles represent $4 \times 2=8$ ice creams, and half a circle represents 1 more. Together they correctly represent 9 ice creams.
- 4 full circles and a full circle.
  - _why:_ Five full circles represent $5 \times 2=10$ ice creams, which is one too many. Nine requires 4 full circles and half a circle.
- 4 full circles and quarter of a circle.
  - _why:_ A quarter-circle represents only $\frac{1}{2}$ of an ice cream because a full circle represents 2. The final single ice cream requires half a circle.
- 3 full circles and half a circle.
  - _why:_ Three full circles represent 6 ice creams and the half-circle represents 1, giving only 7. Nine requires 4 full circles and half a circle.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** Use the same [ ___ ] of symbol throughout a pictogram.

**Back:** Use the same size and style of symbol throughout a pictogram.

**Choices:**

- size and style ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping symbols the same size and style ensures equal fractions of symbols represent equal fractions of the key’s value, making the pictogram accurate and unambiguous.
- colour and position
  - _why:_ Colour and position may help presentation, but they do not ensure fractional symbols have consistent values. The same size and style must be used throughout.
- number and frequency
  - _why:_ The number of symbols changes to represent different frequencies, so it should not remain the same. It is the symbols’ size and style that must stay consistent.
- label and category
  - _why:_ Labels and categories normally differ between rows because they identify different groups. The symbols themselves must retain the same size and style throughout the pictogram.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** The key uses a vertical half-circle, but one row uses a horizontal half-circle. What is wrong?

**Back:** The part-symbol is inconsistent with the key.

**Choices:**

- The part-symbol is inconsistent with the key. ✔
  - _why:_ A partial symbol must be divided in the same way as the example in the key. Changing its orientation makes the pictogram’s notation inconsistent and potentially ambiguous.
- The full symbol represents the wrong frequency.
  - _why:_ The problem concerns how the partial circle is drawn, not the value assigned to a full symbol. The part-symbol is inconsistent with the key’s vertical division.
- The row contains too many full symbols.
  - _why:_ No frequency or symbol count is given, so the number of full symbols cannot be judged. The identifiable error is the part-symbol’s inconsistency with the key.
- The key should use a horizontal half-circle.
  - _why:_ Either orientation could be chosen when creating a key, but rows must then follow that choice. Here, the row’s part-symbol should match the key’s vertical half-circle.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Rows show 2¼, 1¼ and 1½ symbols. How many full symbols is this altogether?

**Back:** 5 full symbols.

**Choices:**

- 5 full symbols. ✔
  - _why:_ Adding the symbol amounts gives 2¼ + 1¼ + 1½ = 5, so the rows contain exactly 5 full symbols altogether.
- 4 full symbols.
  - _why:_ This comes from overlooking some of the fractional symbols. The fractions total one whole symbol, and the whole-number parts total four, giving 5 full symbols.
- 4½ full symbols.
  - _why:_ This results from combining the fractional symbols incorrectly. The fractions ¼ + ¼ + ½ make one whole, so the correct total is 5 full symbols.
- 5½ full symbols.
  - _why:_ This overcounts the fractional symbols by half a symbol. Since ¼ + ¼ + ½ equals exactly one, the correct total is 5 full symbols.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** If 5 full symbols represent 40 stickers, what is one full symbol worth?

**Back:** 8 stickers.

**Choices:**

- 8 stickers. ✔
  - _why:_ Five equal symbols represent 40 stickers, so dividing 40 by 5 gives 8. Therefore, each full symbol is worth 8 stickers.
- 5 stickers.
  - _why:_ This confuses the number of symbols with the value of each symbol. Dividing 40 stickers equally between 5 symbols gives 8 stickers per symbol.
- 10 stickers.
  - _why:_ This value would make the five symbols represent 50 stickers, not 40. The correct calculation is 40 ÷ 5 = 8 stickers.
- 35 stickers.
  - _why:_ Subtracting 5 from 40 does not find the value of one equal symbol. Division is needed: 40 ÷ 5 = 8 stickers.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Key: 1 full symbol = 8 stickers. How should 18 stickers be drawn?

**Back:** 2 full symbols and a quarter-symbol.

**Choices:**

- 2 full symbols and a quarter-symbol. ✔
  - _why:_ Two full symbols represent 16 stickers, leaving 2 stickers. Since 2 is one quarter of 8, the remainder is shown by a quarter-symbol.
- 2 full symbols and a half-symbol.
  - _why:_ A half-symbol represents 4 stickers, so this drawing would show 20 stickers. The remaining 2 stickers require a quarter-symbol, giving 18 altogether.
- 1 full symbol and a quarter-symbol.
  - _why:_ One full symbol and a quarter-symbol represent only 8 + 2 = 10 stickers. Eighteen stickers require 2 full symbols and a quarter-symbol.
- 2 full symbols and a three-quarter-symbol.
  - _why:_ A three-quarter-symbol represents 6 stickers, so this would show 16 + 6 = 22 stickers. The correct remainder is 2 stickers, requiring a quarter-symbol.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Wednesday and Thursday total 24 pizzas. Thursday is 3 times Wednesday. How many equal parts are there?

**Back:** 4 parts.

**Choices:**

- 4 parts. ✔
  - _why:_ Wednesday represents one equal part and Thursday represents three equal parts. Together they make 1 + 3 = 4 equal parts.
- 3 parts.
  - _why:_ Three parts represent Thursday alone, not the combined total. Including Wednesday’s one part gives 1 + 3 = 4 equal parts altogether.
- 6 parts.
  - _why:_ This confuses the value of each part with the number of parts. The ratio is 1:3, so there are 4 equal parts in total.
- 8 parts.
  - _why:_ This doubles the total number of ratio parts without justification. Wednesday is one part and Thursday is three parts, making 4 equal parts altogether.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** Wednesday and Thursday total 24 pizzas. Thursday is 3 times Wednesday. How many pizzas are on Wednesday?

**Back:** 6 pizzas.

**Choices:**

- 6 pizzas. ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio 1:3 contains four equal parts altogether. Dividing 24 pizzas by 4 gives 6 pizzas for Wednesday’s single part.
- 8 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This comes from dividing 24 by 3 and ignoring Wednesday’s additional ratio part. There are four parts altogether, so Wednesday has 24 ÷ 4 = 6 pizzas.
- 12 pizzas.
  - _why:_ Splitting the total equally ignores that Thursday has three times Wednesday’s amount. The 1:3 ratio gives Wednesday one quarter of 24, which is 6 pizzas.
- 18 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This is Thursday’s amount, representing three of the four equal parts. Wednesday has only one part, so its amount is 24 ÷ 4 = 6 pizzas.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Wednesday and Thursday total 24 pizzas. Thursday is 3 times Wednesday. How many pizzas are on Thursday?

**Back:** 18 pizzas.

**Choices:**

- 18 pizzas. ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio 1:3 has four equal parts, so each part is 24 ÷ 4 = 6 pizzas. Thursday has three parts, giving 3 × 6 = 18 pizzas.
- 6 pizzas.
  - _why:_ This is Wednesday’s one-part amount, not Thursday’s three-part amount. Thursday has 3 × 6 = 18 pizzas because it has three times as many.
- 8 pizzas.
  - _why:_ Dividing 24 by 3 treats Thursday’s multiplier as the total number of parts. The ratio has four parts, and Thursday receives three, giving 18 pizzas.
- 72 pizzas.
  - _why:_ Multiplying the total of 24 by 3 incorrectly treats all 24 pizzas as Wednesday’s amount. Wednesday has 6 pizzas, so Thursday has 3 × 6 = 18 pizzas.
