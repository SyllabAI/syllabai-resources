## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the definition of cumulative frequency?

**Back:** The running total of the frequencies.

**Choices:**

- The running total of the frequencies. ✔
  - _why:_ Cumulative frequency is found by successively adding each frequency to the total from the preceding classes, producing a running total through the data set.
- The difference between consecutive frequencies.
  - _why:_ Subtracting consecutive frequencies compares individual classes rather than accumulating them. Cumulative frequency is the running total obtained by adding frequencies successively.
- The average of all the frequencies.
  - _why:_ Calculating a mean would give an average class frequency, not an accumulated count. Cumulative frequency is the running total of the frequencies.
- The highest of the individual frequencies.
  - _why:_ Selecting the largest frequency identifies the modal class rather than the accumulated total. Cumulative frequency is the running total of all frequencies reached so far.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The final value in a cumulative frequency table must equal the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The final value in a cumulative frequency table must equal the total frequency.

**Choices:**

- total frequency ✔
  - _why:_ The last cumulative frequency includes every observation in every class, so it must equal the total frequency of the complete data set.
- class width
  - _why:_ Class width is the difference between a class’s boundaries and is unrelated to the final accumulated count. The final cumulative frequency equals the total frequency.
- upper boundary
  - _why:_ The upper boundary is an endpoint on the horizontal axis, not a number of observations. The final cumulative frequency must equal the total frequency.
- frequency density
  - _why:_ Frequency density is calculated by dividing frequency by class width, so it is not the final accumulated count. The last cumulative frequency equals the total frequency.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** When plotting a cumulative frequency graph, which value from the class interval is used for the horizontal coordinate?

**Back:** The upper boundary (upper bound).

**Choices:**

- The upper boundary (upper bound). ✔
  - _why:_ Each cumulative total counts observations up to the end of its class interval, so it is plotted against that class’s upper boundary.
- The lower boundary (lower bound).
  - _why:_ The cumulative total for a class includes values throughout that class, not merely values below its lower boundary. Each total is plotted at the upper boundary.
- The midpoint (class centre).
  - _why:_ Midpoints are commonly used for frequency polygons, which makes this a tempting confusion. Cumulative frequencies are plotted against the upper class boundaries.
- The class width (interval size).
  - _why:_ Class width measures the size of an interval rather than locating its endpoint. A cumulative frequency point uses the class’s upper boundary as its horizontal coordinate.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the starting coordinate for a cumulative frequency graph?

**Back:** The lower boundary of the first class at cumulative frequency 0.

**Choices:**

- The lower boundary of the first class at cumulative frequency 0. ✔
  - _why:_ Before the first class begins, no observations have yet been accumulated. The graph therefore starts at the first lower boundary with cumulative frequency zero.
- The upper boundary of the first class at cumulative frequency 0.
  - _why:_ By the first upper boundary, all observations in the first class have been counted, so the cumulative frequency is not zero. Start at the first lower boundary.
- The lower boundary of the first class at cumulative frequency 1.
  - _why:_ This off-by-one error assumes counting starts with one observation. Before the first class begins, none have been counted, so the starting cumulative frequency is zero.
- The midpoint of the first class at cumulative frequency 0.
  - _why:_ The midpoint lies inside the first class, where values may already have accumulated. The graph starts at the first class’s lower boundary with cumulative frequency zero.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Back:** A common mistake is plotting the midpoint instead of the upper boundary.

**Choices:**

- midpoint ✔
  - _why:_ Midpoints are used when plotting frequency polygons, but cumulative frequency points must use upper class boundaries. Confusing the two graph types is a common error.
- lower boundary
  - _why:_ Although lower boundaries are relevant to class intervals, the commonly highlighted plotting error is using the midpoint. Cumulative totals should be plotted at upper boundaries.
- class width
  - _why:_ Class width describes the size of an interval and is not normally used as a plotted horizontal coordinate. The common error is plotting the midpoint instead of the upper boundary.
- frequency density
  - _why:_ Frequency density is a vertical-axis quantity used for histograms, not a horizontal class value. The common mistake is plotting the midpoint rather than the upper boundary.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** A common mistake is plotting the midpoint instead of the [ ___ ].

**Back:** A common mistake is plotting the midpoint instead of the upper boundary.

**Choices:**

- upper boundary ✔
  - _why:_ A cumulative total represents the number of observations up to the end of a class, so each point belongs at that class’s upper boundary.
- lower boundary
  - _why:_ Using the lower boundary places the cumulative total at the beginning of the class, before that class has been counted. It belongs at the upper boundary.
- class width
  - _why:_ Class width measures the span of an interval rather than its horizontal endpoint. Cumulative frequency points are plotted using each class’s upper boundary.
- lower limit
  - _why:_ A lower limit marks the beginning of a stated class and does not show where its cumulative total is reached. The required value is the upper boundary.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** Individual points on a cumulative frequency graph should be joined with a [ ___ ].

**Back:** Individual points on a cumulative frequency graph should be joined with a smooth increasing curve.

**Choices:**

- smooth increasing curve ✔
  - _why:_ Cumulative frequency cannot decrease as more observations are included. A smooth increasing curve represents the growing running total and allows estimates of quartiles and percentiles.
- straight-line graph
  - _why:_ Joining every point with rigid straight sections treats the estimated distribution as changing abruptly. At GCSE, cumulative frequency points should be joined by a smooth increasing curve.
- smooth decreasing curve
  - _why:_ A decreasing curve would imply that observations are removed as the measured value increases. Cumulative totals cannot fall, so the curve must increase smoothly.
- frequency polygon
  - _why:_ A frequency polygon joins frequencies plotted at class midpoints and is a different graph type. Cumulative frequency points are joined with a smooth increasing curve.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the vertical position of the lower quartile ($Q_1$) calculated?

**Back:** $$\frac{N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency). ✔
  - _why:_ The lower quartile marks the point below which one quarter of the observations lie, so its vertical position is calculated as $N \div 4$.
- $$\frac{N}{2}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Dividing the total frequency by two locates the median, not the lower quartile. The lower quartile is one quarter of the data, at $\frac{N}{4}$.
- $$\frac{3N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Using three quarters of the total locates the upper quartile, $Q_3$. The lower quartile, $Q_1$, is read at cumulative frequency $\frac{N}{4}$.
- $$\frac{N}{10}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Dividing by ten locates the first decile rather than the lower quartile. Since a quartile divides the data into four parts, use $\frac{N}{4}$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the vertical position of the median calculated?

**Back:** $$\frac{N}{2}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{N}{2}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency). ✔
  - _why:_ The median divides the ordered data into two equal halves, so it is located at cumulative frequency $\frac{N}{2}$, where $N$ is the total frequency.
- $$\frac{N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Using $\frac{N}{4}$ locates the lower quartile, not the median. The median is halfway through the data, so its vertical position is $\frac{N}{2}$.
- $$\frac{3N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Using $\frac{3N}{4}$ locates the upper quartile, not the median. The median splits the data into two equal halves, giving position $\frac{N}{2}$.
- $$\frac{N}{3}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Dividing the total frequency by three would locate a third, not the median. The median is halfway through the ordered data, so its position is $\frac{N}{2}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the vertical position of the upper quartile ($Q_3$) calculated?

**Back:** $$\frac{3N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{3N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency). ✔
  - _why:_ The upper quartile has 75% of the data at or below it. Therefore, its vertical position is $\frac{3N}{4}$, where $N$ is the total frequency.
- $$\frac{N}{4}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Using $\frac{N}{4}$ gives the lower quartile, where 25% of the data lies at or below it. The upper quartile is at $\frac{3N}{4}$.
- $$\frac{N}{2}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Using $\frac{N}{2}$ gives the median, which is halfway through the data. The upper quartile is three-quarters through, at $\frac{3N}{4}$.
- $$\frac{4N}{3}$ (where $N$ is the total frequency).
  - _why:_ Reversing the fraction produces a position greater than the total frequency, so it cannot be a quartile. The upper quartile is located at $\frac{3N}{4}$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the steps to estimate a quartile from a cumulative frequency graph?

**Back:** Find the position on the vertical axisRead across to the curveRead down to the horizontal axis

**Choices:**

- Find the position on the vertical axisRead across to the curveRead down to the horizontal axis ✔
  - _why:_ A quartile position is first found using cumulative frequency on the vertical axis. Moving across to the curve and then down gives the corresponding data value.
- Find the position on the horizontal axisRead up to the curveRead across to the vertical axis
  - _why:_ This procedure starts with a data value and finds its cumulative frequency, reversing the required process. Begin at the quartile position vertically, then read across and down.
- Find the position on the vertical axisRead down to the curveRead across to the horizontal axis
  - _why:_ Reading down from the vertical axis does not lead correctly to the curve. From the quartile position, read horizontally across to the curve and then vertically down.
- Find the position on the horizontal axisRead across to the curveRead up to the vertical axis
  - _why:_ Quartile positions are cumulative frequencies, so they must be located on the vertical axis rather than the horizontal axis. Then read across to the curve and down.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the interquartile range (IQR) calculated?

**Back:** $$Q_3 - Q_1$

**Choices:**

- $$Q_3 - Q_1$ ✔
  - _why:_ The interquartile range is the width of the middle 50% of the data. It is calculated by subtracting the lower quartile from the upper quartile.
- $$Q_3 + Q_1$
  - _why:_ Adding the quartiles does not measure the distance between them. The interquartile range is the upper quartile minus the lower quartile, so it is $Q_3 - Q_1$.
- $$Q_1 - Q_3$
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction usually produces a negative value, which cannot represent a spread. Subtract the lower quartile from the upper quartile: $Q_3 - Q_1$.
- $$Q_3 - Q_2$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the median $Q_2$ from the upper quartile measures only one section of the box. The full interquartile range is $Q_3 - Q_1$.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** The interquartile range measures the spread of the [ ___ ] of the data.

**Back:** The interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

**Choices:**

- middle 50% ✔
  - _why:_ The interval from the lower quartile to the upper quartile contains the central half of the ordered data. Therefore, the interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50%.
- lowest 50%
  - _why:_ The lowest 50% runs from the minimum to the median, not between the quartiles. The interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data.
- middle 75%
  - _why:_ The quartiles divide the data into four equal 25% sections, and the interval from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ covers two sections. It therefore contains the middle 50%.
- highest 25%
  - _why:_ The highest 25% lies above the upper quartile and is not measured by the interquartile range. The IQR covers the middle 50%, from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** Why are values read from a cumulative frequency graph called estimates?

**Back:** The original data is groupedThe curve is drawn by hand

**Choices:**

- The original data is groupedThe curve is drawn by hand ✔
  - _why:_ Grouped data does not give every exact individual value, and a hand-drawn curve introduces graphical uncertainty. Consequently, values read from the curve can only be estimates.
- The original data is continuousThe curve is perfectly accurate
  - _why:_ Continuous data can be recorded exactly, and a perfectly accurate curve would not itself cause uncertainty. Estimates arise because the data is grouped and the curve is drawn by hand.
- The original data is unorderedThe curve is drawn by computer
  - _why:_ Cumulative frequency already organises observations by value, so unordered data is not the issue. Uncertainty comes from grouped intervals and reading from a hand-drawn curve.
- The original data is discreteThe curve is made of straight lines
  - _why:_ Discrete data is not automatically estimated, and cumulative frequency curves are not necessarily made of straight lines. The estimates result from grouping and hand-drawing the curve.

**Front:** Which five values are used to construct a box plot?

**Back:** Lowest valueLower quartile ($Q_1$)MedianUpper quartile ($Q_3$)Highest value

**Choices:**

- Lowest valueLower quartile ($Q_1$)MedianUpper quartile ($Q_3$)Highest value ✔
  - _why:_ A box plot uses the five-number summary: the minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile and maximum. These values determine the whisker ends, box edges and central line.
- Lowest valueLower quartile ($Q_1$)MeanUpper quartile ($Q_3$)Highest value
  - _why:_ The central line of a standard box plot represents the median, not the mean. The other four values are the minimum, lower quartile, upper quartile and maximum.
- Lowest valueLower quartile ($Q_1$)MedianInterquartile rangeHighest value
  - _why:_ The interquartile range determines the box’s width but is not itself one of the five plotted positions. The upper quartile $Q_3$ must be included instead.
- RangeLower quartile ($Q_1$)MedianUpper quartile ($Q_3$)Highest value
  - _why:_ The range is a difference, not a plotted endpoint. A box plot needs the actual lowest value alongside the lower quartile, median, upper quartile and highest value.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a box plot, the [ ___ ] line does not have to be in the center of the box.

**Back:** In a box plot, the median line does not have to be in the center of the box.

**Choices:**

- median ✔
  - _why:_ The line inside the box marks the median. It is centred only when the distances from the median to the lower and upper quartiles are equal.
- mean
  - _why:_ A standard box plot does not usually display the mean as its internal line. The line marks the median, which need not be centred between the two quartiles.
- range
  - _why:_ The range is the numerical difference between the highest and lowest values, not a line inside the box. The internal line represents the median.
- frequency
  - _why:_ Frequency counts how often values occur and is not represented by the line inside a standard box plot. That line shows the median of the data.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** When comparing two data sets, what does a higher median indicate?

**Back:** The typical value is higher.

**Choices:**

- The typical value is higher. ✔
  - _why:_ The median is the middle value when the data is ordered, so a higher median shows that the typical or central value of the data set is higher.
- The typical value is lower.
  - _why:_ This reverses the comparison between the medians. A higher median means the middle, or typical, value is higher rather than lower.
- The spread of values is greater.
  - _why:_ The median measures central tendency, not spread. A higher median indicates a higher typical value; the IQR or range must be compared to assess spread.
- The data consistency is higher.
  - _why:_ Consistency is determined by measures of spread such as the IQR, not by the median. A higher median only indicates that the typical value is higher.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** When comparing two data sets, what does a smaller IQR indicate?

**Back:** The data is more consistent (less spread out).

**Choices:**

- The data is more consistent (less spread out). ✔
  - _why:_ The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data. A smaller IQR means these values are closer together, indicating greater consistency.
- The data is less consistent (more spread out).
  - _why:_ This reverses the meaning of the IQR comparison. A smaller IQR means the middle 50% is less spread out, so the data is more consistent.
- The data has a higher median (larger typical value).
  - _why:_ The IQR measures spread and does not determine the median. A smaller IQR indicates more consistent data, but the typical value could be higher or lower.
- The data is more symmetrical (evenly spread out).
  - _why:_ The size of the IQR alone does not reveal the distribution’s symmetry. It only shows that the middle 50% is less spread out and therefore more consistent.

**Back:** 20

**Choices:**

- 20 ✔
  - _why:_ The inequality includes values up to and including 20, so 20 is the upper boundary of the class interval.
- 10
  - _why:_ The value 10 is the lower boundary, even though it is excluded by the strict inequality. The upper boundary is 20.
- 19
  - _why:_ Choosing 19 incorrectly treats the data as restricted to whole numbers. The stated class interval extends continuously to 20, which is its upper boundary.

  - _why:_ This adds one to the included endpoint, a common whole-number interval mistake. The class ends at 20, so its upper boundary is 20.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** If the total frequency ($N$) is 80, what is the vertical position for the lower quartile?

**Back:** 20 (since $\frac{80}{4} = 20$).

**Choices:**

- 20 (since $\frac{80}{4} = 20$). ✔
  - _why:_ The lower quartile is located at one quarter of the total cumulative frequency. With 80 values, its vertical position is $\frac{80}{4}=20$.
- 40 (since $\frac{80}{2} = 40$).
  - _why:_ Dividing the total frequency by 2 finds the median position, not the lower quartile. The lower quartile is at $\frac{80}{4}=20$.
- 60 (since $\frac{3 \times 80}{4} = 60$).
  - _why:_ Using three quarters of the total gives the upper quartile position. The lower quartile uses one quarter, so its vertical position is $\frac{80}{4}=20$.
- 16 (since $\frac{80}{5} = 16$).
  - _why:_ Dividing by 5 incorrectly splits the data into fifths. Quartiles divide ordered data into four parts, so the lower quartile position is $\frac{80}{4}=20$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the range calculated from a box plot?

**Back:** Highest value minus the lowest value.

**Choices:**

- Highest value minus the lowest value. ✔
  - _why:_ The range measures the full spread of the data, from the minimum to the maximum. It is therefore calculated by subtracting the lowest value from the highest value.
- Highest value plus the lowest value.
  - _why:_ Adding the two endpoints does not measure the distance between them. The range is found by subtracting the lowest value from the highest value.
- Upper quartile minus the lower quartile.
  - _why:_ Subtracting the lower quartile from the upper quartile calculates the IQR, which measures the middle 50%. The range uses the highest and lowest values instead.
- Lowest value minus the highest value.
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction and usually produces a negative result. The range is non-negative and is calculated as the highest value minus the lowest value.
