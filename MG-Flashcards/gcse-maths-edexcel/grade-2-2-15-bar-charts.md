## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** A bar chart uses separate bars to compare amounts in different [ ___ ].

**Back:** A bar chart uses separate bars to compare amounts in different categories.

**Choices:**

- categories ✔
  - _why:_ Bar charts compare discrete groups, called categories, using separate bars. Each bar’s height represents the amount associated with one category.
- intervals
  - _why:_ Intervals are continuous ranges and are more commonly associated with grouped data or histograms. Separate bars on a bar chart represent different categories.
- measurements
  - _why:_ Measurements are the numerical amounts represented by bar heights, not the groups being compared. Those separate groups are called categories.
- frequencies
  - _why:_ Frequencies can determine the heights of the bars, but they are not necessarily the groups along the horizontal axis. Bar charts compare different categories.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** If one bar is taller than another, what does that show?

**Back:** The taller bar has the bigger value.

**Choices:**

- The taller bar has the bigger value. ✔
  - _why:_ Bar height represents the value for a category, so a taller bar reaches a larger number on the vertical scale and therefore has the bigger value.
- The taller bar has the smaller value.
  - _why:_ This reverses the relationship between height and value. A taller bar reaches higher on the vertical scale, so it has the bigger value.
- The two bars have the same value.
  - _why:_ Equal values would normally produce bars of equal height. If one bar is taller, it represents a bigger value than the other bar.
- The wider bar has the bigger value.
  - _why:_ Bar width does not usually represent the value in a standard bar chart. The taller bar, rather than the wider bar, has the bigger value.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows [ ___ ] and the vertical axis shows [ ___ ].

**Back:** On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows categories and the vertical axis shows numbers.

**Choices:**

- On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows categories and the vertical axis shows numbers. ✔
  - _why:_ A usual bar chart places the groups being compared along the horizontal axis. Numerical values are shown on the vertical axis and determine the bars’ heights.
- On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows numbers and the vertical axis shows categories.
  - _why:_ This swaps the usual roles of the axes. Categories normally appear along the horizontal axis, while numbers are displayed on the vertical axis.
- On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows categories and the vertical axis shows bars.
  - _why:_ Bars are drawn within the chart rather than used as vertical-axis labels. The horizontal axis shows categories and the vertical axis shows numbers.
- On a usual bar chart, the horizontal axis shows bar heights and the vertical axis shows numbers.
  - _why:_ Bar heights are read using the vertical scale, not listed on the horizontal axis. The horizontal axis shows categories and the vertical axis shows numbers.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you check the vertical scale before reading a bar?

**Back:** It may increase by more than 1.

**Choices:**

- It may increase by more than 1. ✔
  - _why:_ Consecutive marks on the vertical axis do not always differ by one. Checking the scale prevents a bar’s height from being read as the wrong value.
- It always increases by exactly 1.
  - _why:_ Assuming every scale rises in ones is a common reading error. The vertical scale may increase by more than 1 between consecutive marks.
- It shows which bar is widest.
  - _why:_ The vertical scale is used to read bar values, not widths. It must be checked because consecutive marks may increase by more than 1.
- It determines the order of the categories.
  - _why:_ Category order is normally shown along the horizontal axis, not determined by the vertical scale. The scale needs checking because it may rise by more than 1.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** A vertical scale is labelled 0, 2, 4, 6, 8. What is each labelled step worth?

**Back:** 2.

**Choices:**

- 2. ✔
  - _why:_ The difference between consecutive labels is $2$, as shown by $2-0=2$ and $4-2=2$. Therefore, each labelled step is worth 2.
- 1.
  - _why:_ Counting each labelled jump as one ignores the numbers printed on the scale. Consecutive labels differ by $2$, so each labelled step is worth 2.
- 4.
  - _why:_ Using $4$ confuses two labelled steps with one: moving from 0 to 4 crosses two steps. A single labelled step is worth 2.
- 8.
  - _why:_ The value 8 is the highest label shown, not the value of one step. Consecutive labels differ by 2, so each step is worth 2.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** With a scale of 1°C per unit, what height should an October bar for 15°C reach?

**Back:** 15°C.

**Choices:**

- 15°C. ✔
  - _why:_ At 1°C per unit, a temperature of 15°C corresponds directly to a height of 15 units. The bar should therefore reach 15°C on the scale.
- 14°C.
  - _why:_ This is one unit too low, usually from stopping before the fifteenth scale interval. At 1°C per unit, 15°C requires a height of 15 units.
- 16°C.
  - _why:_ This is one unit too high, usually from counting both zero and the first interval. At 1°C per unit, the bar should reach 15°C.
- 150°C.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 10 incorrectly treats each unit as 10°C. Since the scale is 1°C per unit, a 15°C bar should reach 15°C.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** The range is the [ ___ ] minus the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The range is the biggest value minus the smallest value.

**Choices:**

- The range is the biggest value minus the smallest value. ✔
  - _why:_ The range measures the full spread of a data set. It is calculated by subtracting the smallest observed value from the biggest observed value.
- The range is the smallest value minus the biggest value.
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction and would produce a negative result when the values differ. The range is the biggest value minus the smallest value.
- The range is the biggest value minus the second-smallest value.
  - _why:_ Using the second-smallest value ignores part of the data’s spread. The range uses both extremes: the biggest value minus the smallest value.
- The range is the total value minus the smallest value.
  - _why:_ The total is used when finding a mean, not a range. To measure the full spread, subtract the smallest value from the biggest value.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Temperatures are 6°C, 8°C, 12°C, 17°C and 20°C. What is the range?

**Back:** 14°C.

**Choices:**

- 14°C. ✔
  - _why:_ The highest temperature is 20°C and the lowest is 6°C. Subtracting the lowest from the highest gives a range of $20-6=14$°C.
- 12°C.
  - _why:_ This uses 8°C rather than the actual minimum of 6°C. The range must use both extremes, giving $20-6=14$°C.
- 26°C.
  - _why:_ Adding the highest and lowest temperatures does not calculate the range. Subtract the minimum from the maximum instead: $20-6=14$°C.
- 8°C.
  - _why:_ This subtracts the middle value, 12°C, from 20°C and ignores the minimum. The range uses the smallest value: $20-6=14$°C.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is using the last bar minus the first bar unreliable for the range?

**Back:** Range uses the biggest and smallest values.

**Choices:**

- Range uses the biggest and smallest values. ✔
  - _why:_ The range measures the full spread of the data, so it is calculated by subtracting the smallest value from the biggest value, regardless of bar order.
- Range uses the first and last values.
  - _why:_ This assumes the bars are arranged in numerical order, which they may not be. The range uses the biggest and smallest values in the entire data set.
- Range uses the tallest and shortest bars.
  - _why:_ Bar heights may represent frequencies rather than the data values themselves. The range is found from the biggest and smallest values, not automatically from bar heights.
- Range uses the most common and least common values.
  - _why:_ This confuses range with frequency. How often values occur does not determine the range, which is the difference between the biggest and smallest values.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** A grouped bar chart has [ ___ ] next to each other for each category.

**Back:** A grouped bar chart has two or more bars next to each other for each category.

**Choices:**

- two or more bars ✔
  - _why:_ Grouped bar charts place two or more bars beside one another within each category, allowing the values for different groups to be compared directly.
- one continuous bar
  - _why:_ A single continuous bar does not display separate groups side by side. A grouped bar chart has two or more bars next to each other for each category.
- two or more sectors
  - _why:_ Sectors are used in pie charts, not grouped bar charts. Each category in a grouped bar chart contains two or more separate bars.
- several stacked sections
  - _why:_ Stacked sections describe a stacked bar chart, where values share one bar. A grouped bar chart instead places two or more separate bars next to each other.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** In a grouped bar chart, why is a key needed?

**Back:** It shows which colour, shading or pattern belongs to each group.

**Choices:**

- It shows which colour, shading or pattern belongs to each group. ✔
  - _why:_ Each group is represented using a particular visual style. The key links every colour, shading or pattern to the group it represents.
- It shows which bar height or scale belongs to each group.
  - _why:_ Bar heights are read using the vertical scale rather than the key. The key identifies which colour, shading or pattern represents each group.
- It shows which category label or number belongs to each group.
  - _why:_ Categories and numbers are normally displayed on the chart’s axes. The key explains which colour, shading or pattern has been assigned to each group.
- It shows which frequency, range or mode belongs to each group.
  - _why:_ A key does not calculate statistical measures or state frequencies. It matches each group with its colour, shading or pattern so the bars can be identified.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Aisha ran 4 miles and Ben ran 7 miles. How much further did Ben run?

**Back:** 3 miles.

**Choices:**

- 3 miles. ✔
  - _why:_ The difference is found by subtracting Aisha’s distance from Ben’s distance: $7-4=3$. Therefore, Ben ran 3 miles further.
- 11 miles.
  - _why:_ Adding $4+7$ gives the total distance both people ran, not how much further Ben ran. Subtracting gives $7-4=3$ miles.
- 4 miles.
  - _why:_ This repeats Aisha’s distance instead of finding the difference. Ben’s extra distance is calculated using $7-4=3$, so he ran 3 miles further.
- 28 miles.
  - _why:_ Multiplying the two distances does not measure how much further Ben ran. The required comparison is subtraction: $7-4=3$ miles.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** Maya has run 22 miles and Liam has run 27 miles. Liam will not run again. How many miles must Maya run to match him?

**Back:** 5 miles.

**Choices:**

- 5 miles. ✔
  - _why:_ Maya must close the difference between 22 miles and 27 miles. Subtracting gives $27-22=5$, so she must run another 5 miles.
- 49 miles.
  - _why:_ Adding $22+27$ finds the combined distance, not the extra distance Maya needs. She must cover the difference, which is $27-22=5$ miles.
- 27 miles.
  - _why:_ This uses Liam’s total as the additional distance Maya must run. Maya already has 22 miles, so she needs only $27-22=5$ more miles.
- 6 miles.
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one subtraction error. Counting from 22 up to 27 gives five steps, so Maya must run exactly 5 more miles.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** Frequency means how many [ ___ ] something happens.

**Back:** Frequency means how many times something happens.

**Choices:**

- times ✔
  - _why:_ Frequency is the number of times an event or value occurs. For example, a frequency of 6 means that it happened six times.
- categories
  - _why:_ Categories are the groups into which data is organised, not the number of occurrences. Frequency means how many times something happens.
- groups
  - _why:_ Groups can be used to organise or compare data, but they do not define frequency. Frequency is the number of times something occurs.
- days
  - _why:_ Days could be a unit in a particular data set, but frequency is not limited to time. It means the number of times something happens.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** On a frequency bar chart, how do you identify the mode?

**Back:** Choose the category with the tallest bar.

**Choices:**

- Choose the category with the tallest bar. ✔
  - _why:_ The mode is the category occurring most frequently. On a frequency bar chart, the greatest frequency is represented by the tallest bar.
- Choose the category with the shortest bar.
  - _why:_ The shortest bar represents the lowest frequency, not the mode. The modal category has the greatest frequency and therefore the tallest bar.
- Choose the category with the first bar.
  - _why:_ A bar’s position does not determine whether its category is the mode. The mode is identified by finding the category with the tallest bar.
- Choose the category with the last bar.
  - _why:_ The final category is not necessarily the most frequent one. The modal category is shown by whichever bar is tallest on the chart.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** A goals frequency chart shows 0 goals: 5, 1 goal: 9, 2 goals: 7, 3 goals: 4. What is the modal number of goals?

**Back:** 1 goal.

**Choices:**

- 1 goal. ✔
  - _why:_ The mode is the result with the highest frequency. One goal occurred 9 times, which is more frequent than 0, 2 or 3 goals.
- 0 goals.
  - _why:_ Zero goals has a frequency of 5, but this is not the greatest frequency. One goal has the highest frequency of 9, so it is the mode.
- 2 goals.
  - _why:_ Two goals occurred 7 times, which is less than the frequency of 9 for one goal. Therefore, the modal number is 1 goal.
- 3 goals.
  - _why:_ Three goals has the lowest frequency, occurring only 4 times. The highest frequency is 9 for one goal, making 1 goal the mode.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** If a team scored 3 goals in 4 matches, how many goals is that in total?

**Back:** 12 goals.

**Choices:**

- 12 goals. ✔
  - _why:_ The team scored 3 goals in each of 4 matches, so multiplying $3 \times 4$ gives a total of 12 goals.
- 7 goals.
  - _why:_ Adding 3 goals and 4 matches mixes different quantities and gives 7. The total is found by multiplying $3 \times 4$, giving 12 goals.
- 9 goals.
  - _why:_ This accounts for only three of the four matches. Multiplying 3 goals per match by all 4 matches gives $3 \times 4 = 12$ goals.
- 16 goals.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses 4 goals for each of the 4 matches. The stated rate is 3 goals per match, so the total is 12 goals.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each [ ___ ] by its [ ___ ], then add.

**Back:** To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each value by its frequency, then add.

**Choices:**

- To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each value by its frequency, then add. ✔
  - _why:_ Each value contributes to the total once for every time it occurs. Therefore, multiplying each value by its frequency and adding the products gives the overall total.
- To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each frequency by its frequency, then add.
  - _why:_ Squaring each frequency ignores the values represented by the bars. The correct method is to multiply each value by its frequency, then add all the products.
- To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each value by its cumulative frequency, then add.
  - _why:_ Cumulative frequencies are running totals, so using them repeatedly overcounts the data. Each value must instead be multiplied by its ordinary frequency before the products are added.
- To find a total from a frequency bar chart, multiply each class width by its frequency density, then add.
  - _why:_ Class width and frequency density are used to recover frequencies from histograms, not totals from frequency bar charts. Here, multiply each value by its frequency, then add.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** A team scored 0 goals in 3 matches, 1 in 5 matches, 2 in 4 matches, and 3 in 2 matches. What was the total number of goals?

**Back:** 19 goals.

**Choices:**

- 19 goals. ✔
  - _why:_ Weighting each score by its frequency gives $(0 \times 3) + (1 \times 5) + (2 \times 4) + (3 \times 2) = 19$ goals.
- 14 goals.
  - _why:_ Adding $3+5+4+2$ counts the number of matches, not the goals scored. Weighting each score by its frequency gives a total of 19 goals.
- 20 goals.
  - _why:_ This is an arithmetic slip when adding the weighted scores. The products are 0, 5, 8 and 6, which sum to 19 goals.
- 22 goals.
  - _why:_ This overcounts the goals by failing to match each score with its correct frequency. The correct calculation is $0+5+8+6=19$ goals.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is adding only the frequencies wrong when finding total goals?

**Back:** Frequencies count matches, not total goals.

**Choices:**

- Frequencies count matches, not total goals. ✔
  - _why:_ A frequency records how many matches produced each score. Adding the frequencies therefore gives the number of matches, while total goals require each score to be multiplied by its frequency.
- Frequencies count total goals, not matches.
  - _why:_ This reverses the meaning of frequency. Frequencies count how many matches had each score, so adding them gives matches; total goals require multiplying scores by frequencies.
- Each frequency already includes the goals scored.
  - _why:_ A frequency contains only the number of occurrences, not the score attached to them. Each score must be multiplied by its frequency to find its contribution to total goals.
- Adding frequencies counts each match twice.
  - _why:_ Adding frequencies counts each match once, not twice, but it still gives only the number of matches. Total goals come from multiplying each score by its frequency.

## Card 21 — `cloze`

**Front:** Tallies are usually grouped in [ ___ ].

**Back:** Tallies are usually grouped in fives.

**Choices:**

- fives ✔
  - _why:_ Tallies are grouped in fives because the fifth stroke crosses the previous four, making larger frequencies quicker and easier to count accurately.
- tens
  - _why:_ Groups of ten would be harder to recognise and count quickly. Standard tally notation groups marks in fives, with the fifth mark crossing the first four.
- threes
  - _why:_ Groups of three are not the standard tally convention and make larger totals less efficient to read. Tallies are normally arranged in recognisable groups of five.
- pairs
  - _why:_ Pairing tally marks would create many small groups and slow down counting. Tallies are conventionally grouped in fives so totals can be identified quickly.

## Card 23 — `cloze`

**Front:** Bars in a bar chart should have [ ___ ] and [ ___ ] between them.

**Back:** Bars in a bar chart should have equal widths and clear gaps between them.

**Choices:**

- Bars in a bar chart should have equal widths and clear gaps between them. ✔
  - _why:_ Equal widths prevent visual distortion, while clear gaps show that the categories are separate. These features make a bar chart accurate and easy to interpret.
- Bars in a bar chart should have equal widths and no gaps between them.
  - _why:_ Bars without gaps suggest continuous data, as in a histogram. A bar chart represents separate categories, so its equal-width bars should have clear gaps between them.
- Bars in a bar chart should have different widths and clear gaps between them.
  - _why:_ Different widths can exaggerate some categories and make comparisons misleading. Bar-chart bars should have equal widths as well as clear gaps between the separate categories.
- Bars in a bar chart should have different widths and no gaps between them.
  - _why:_ This confuses a bar chart with features associated with histograms and also distorts comparisons. Bar-chart bars should have equal widths and clear gaps between categories.

## Card 24 — `basic`

**Front:** What is wrong with a vertical scale labelled 0, 5, 12, 20, 30?

**Back:** The scale is uneven.

**Choices:**

- The scale is uneven. ✔
  - _why:_ The intervals are 5, 7, 8 and 10 rather than a constant amount. A numerical axis must increase by equal values at equally spaced points.
- The scale starts at zero.
  - _why:_ Starting at zero is not the problem and is often appropriate for a bar chart. The error is that the intervals between labels are unequal.
- The scale has too many labels.
  - _why:_ Five labels are not excessive for an axis. The actual problem is that the increases are 5, 7, 8 and 10, so the scale is uneven.
- The scale is increasing.
  - _why:_ A vertical scale should normally increase upwards, so increasing values are appropriate. The fault is that the values do not rise in equal intervals.
