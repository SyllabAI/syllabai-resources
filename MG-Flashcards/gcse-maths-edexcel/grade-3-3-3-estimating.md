## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** The first significant figure in a number is the first [ ___ ] digit.

**Back:** The first significant figure in a number is the first non-zero digit.

**Choices:**

- non-zero ✔
  - _why:_ Leading zeros only locate the decimal point, so the first significant figure is the first digit with a value from 1 to 9.
- zero
  - _why:_ Leading zeros are not significant because they only indicate place value. The first significant figure is therefore the first non-zero digit.
- decimal
  - _why:_ The first significant figure is not necessarily after the decimal point, as whole-number digits may be significant. It is the first non-zero digit.
- even
  - _why:_ Whether a digit is odd or even does not determine its significance. The first significant figure is the first non-zero digit in the number.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** To how many significant figures should you usually round numbers for a GCSE estimation problem?

**Back:** 1 significant figure

**Choices:**

- 1 significant figure ✔
  - _why:_ Rounding each number to 1 significant figure usually makes the arithmetic simple enough to perform mentally while retaining a useful estimate.
- 2 significant figures
  - _why:_ Using 2 significant figures often leaves unnecessarily difficult arithmetic. GCSE estimation problems usually require numbers to be rounded to 1 significant figure.
- 3 significant figures
  - _why:_ Using 3 significant figures keeps too much detail for a quick estimate. Numbers should usually be rounded to 1 significant figure before calculating.
- 1 decimal place
  - _why:_ Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, whereas estimation normally uses significant figures. Numbers should usually be rounded to 1 significant figure.

**Back:** 0.5

**Choices:**

- 0.5 ✔
  - _why:_ The first significant digit is 5, and the next digit is 2. Since 2 is below 5, the 5 remains unchanged.
- 0.6
  - _why:_ Rounding the 5 upward mistakes the following digit as 5 or more. The next digit is 2, so the correct result is 0.5.

  - _why:_ Leaving both significant digits does not perform the requested rounding. Keeping only the first significant digit gives 0.5 because the next digit is 2.
- 0.05
  - _why:_ This place-value slip moves the significant digit one position to the right. Rounding changes precision, not magnitude, so the correct result is 0.5.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** When should you round numbers during an estimation calculation?

**Back:** Round before performing any calculations.

**Choices:**

- Round before performing any calculations. ✔
  - _why:_ Estimation replaces the original values with simple rounded numbers first. The calculation is then performed using those rounded values to obtain an approximate answer.
- Round after performing all calculations.
  - _why:_ Rounding only the final answer finds an approximation to the exact calculation rather than carrying out an estimate. Round the input numbers before calculating.
- Round during each stage of the calculation.
  - _why:_ Repeated rounding can introduce extra errors and does not simplify the calculation from the start. For estimation, round the given numbers before performing any operations.
- Round halfway through the calculation.
  - _why:_ Waiting until halfway misses the purpose of simplifying the arithmetic at the outset. The given numbers should be rounded before any calculations are performed.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a quick way to divide a number by 0.5?

**Back:** Multiply the number by 2.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the number by 2. ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing by 0.5 asks how many halves fit into the number. There are twice as many halves as wholes, so multiplication by 2 is equivalent.
- Divide the number by 2.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 2 is equivalent to multiplying by 0.5, the reverse of the required operation. Dividing by 0.5 means multiplying by 2.
- Multiply the number by 0.5.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 0.5 halves a number rather than dividing it by one-half. The reciprocal of 0.5 is 2, so multiply by 2.
- Add the number to 2.
  - _why:_ Addition cannot replace division by a fixed fraction because it does not scale the number proportionally. Dividing by 0.5 is equivalent to multiplying by 2.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a quick way to divide a number by 0.2?

**Back:** Multiply the number by 5.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the number by 5. ✔
  - _why:_ Since $0.2 = \frac{1}{5}$, dividing by $0.2$ is equivalent to dividing by one-fifth, which multiplies the original number by $5$.
- Divide the number by 5.
  - _why:_ This treats $0.2$ as though it were $5$. Since $0.2 = \frac{1}{5}$, dividing by $0.2$ means multiplying the number by $5$.
- Multiply the number by 2.
  - _why:_ This ignores the place value in $0.2$ and uses its digit alone. The reciprocal of $0.2$ is $5$, so the number must be multiplied by $5$.
- Divide the number by 2.
  - _why:_ This confuses dividing by $0.2$ with dividing by $2$. Dividing by a number smaller than $1$ increases the value, and here it multiplies it by $5$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** When estimating square roots, what should you round the number inside the root to?

**Back:** The nearest perfect square (e.g., 25, 49, 100).

**Choices:**

- The nearest perfect square (e.g., 25, 49, 100). ✔
  - _why:_ Perfect squares have integer square roots, making them easy to calculate mentally. Replacing the number with the nearest perfect square therefore gives a quick and sensible estimate.
- The nearest multiple of ten (e.g., 20, 50, 100).
  - _why:_ Multiples of ten do not usually have simple square roots, so this rounding may not help. The number should be rounded to the nearest perfect square, such as $25$ or $100$.
- The nearest prime number (e.g., 23, 47, 101).
  - _why:_ Prime numbers generally do not have integer square roots, so they are not useful reference values. Round to the nearest perfect square, whose square root is easy to find.
- The nearest whole number (e.g., 25, 49, 101).
  - _why:_ Rounding to a whole number may still leave a difficult square root. Rounding to the nearest perfect square produces a value with a known integer square root.

**Back:** An underestimate is an answer that is too small, while an overestimate is too large.

**Back:** An underestimate

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ For positive multiplication, rounding every factor down makes each factor no greater than its original value. Their product is therefore smaller than the exact product, giving an underestimate.
- An overestimate
  - _why:_ This reverses the effect of rounding down. With positive factors, using smaller values produces a smaller product than the exact one, so the result is an underestimate.

  - _why:_ Rounded factors are generally different from the original factors, so their product is not exact. Rounding all positive factors down produces an underestimate.

  - _why:_ The direction can be predicted because every positive factor has been reduced. Multiplying these reduced values gives a product below the exact value, so it is an underestimate.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How does rounding down the denominator (bottom) of a fraction affect the final estimate?

**Back:** It makes the final answer larger (overestimate).

**Choices:**

- It makes the final answer larger (overestimate). ✔
  - _why:_ A smaller positive denominator means the numerator is divided by a smaller number, producing a larger quotient. The estimated fraction is therefore greater than it would otherwise be.
- It makes the final answer smaller (underestimate).
  - _why:_ This reverses the denominator relationship. Dividing by a smaller positive denominator gives a larger quotient, so rounding the denominator down produces an overestimate.
- It has no effect on the final estimate.
  - _why:_ This overlooks the denominator’s role in division. Reducing a positive denominator increases the value of the fraction, so the final estimate becomes larger.
- It makes the final answer exact rather than estimated.
  - _why:_ Rounding cannot make a calculation exact because information has been removed. Rounding a positive denominator down makes the quotient larger, producing an overestimate.
