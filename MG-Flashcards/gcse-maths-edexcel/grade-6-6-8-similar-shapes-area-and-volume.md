## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean if two shapes are mathematically similar?

**Back:** They have the same shape but different sizes (one is an enlargement of the other).

**Choices:**

- They have the same shape but different sizes (one is an enlargement of the other). ✔
  - _why:_ Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding lengths, so their shape is unchanged even though one may be a larger or smaller enlargement.
- They have the same size but different shapes (one has the same area as the other).
  - _why:_ Having the same area does not make shapes similar, because their corresponding angles and side proportions may differ. Similar shapes have the same shape but may have different sizes.
- They have the same perimeter but different areas (one has longer sides than the other).
  - _why:_ Equal perimeters do not guarantee equal corresponding angles or proportional sides. Similar shapes have the same shape, with every corresponding length changed by one consistent scale factor.
- They have the same area but different perimeters (one is a rotation of the other).
  - _why:_ Equal areas alone do not establish similarity, and a rotation does not create a different-sized enlargement. Similar shapes have matching angles and proportional corresponding side lengths.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for the linear scale factor ($k$) when going from Shape A to Shape B?

**Back:** $$$k = \frac{\text{length on B}}{\text{matching length on A}}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$k = \frac{\text{length on B}}{\text{matching length on A}}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ When transforming Shape A into Shape B, divide a length on the resulting shape B by its matching length on the original shape A.
- $$$k = \frac{\text{length on A}}{\text{matching length on B}}$$
  - _why:_ This reverses the ratio and gives the scale factor for going from B to A. From A to B, divide the matching length on B by the length on A.
- $$$k = \frac{\text{area of B}}{\text{matching area of A}}$$
  - _why:_ Dividing corresponding areas gives the area scale factor, which equals $k^2$, rather than the linear scale factor. Linear scale factor uses matching lengths on B and A.
- $$$k = \frac{\text{length on B}}{\text{area of A}}$$
  - _why:_ A length cannot be divided by an area to obtain a dimensionless linear scale factor. The calculation must compare a length on B with its matching length on A.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the area scale factor from the linear scale factor ($k$)?

**Back:** Square the linear scale factor: $k^2$.

**Choices:**

- Square the linear scale factor: $k^2$. ✔
  - _why:_ Area involves two dimensions, so both corresponding lengths are multiplied by $k$. Their combined effect is $k \times k = k^2$.
- Multiply by the linear scale factor: $k$.
  - _why:_ Using $k$ only accounts for one dimension, which applies to lengths rather than areas. Area changes in two dimensions, so the correct scale factor is $k^2$.
- Cube the linear scale factor: $k^3$.
  - _why:_ Cubing accounts for three dimensions and therefore gives the volume scale factor. Area has two dimensions, so its scale factor is the square, $k^2$.
- Square root the linear scale factor: $\sqrt{k}$.
  - _why:_ Taking a square root is used when recovering a linear scale factor from an area scale factor. Starting with $k$, the area scale factor is $k^2$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the volume scale factor from the linear scale factor ($k$)?

**Back:** Cube the linear scale factor: $k^3$.

**Choices:**

- Cube the linear scale factor: $k^3$. ✔
  - _why:_ Volume involves three dimensions, each multiplied by $k$. Multiplying the three changes gives $k \times k \times k = k^3$.
- Square the linear scale factor: $k^2$.
  - _why:_ Squaring accounts for only two dimensions and gives the area scale factor. Volume changes in three dimensions, so the correct scale factor is $k^3$.
- Multiply by the linear scale factor: $k$.
  - _why:_ Using $k$ accounts for the change in just one length. A volume has three dimensions, so all three scale changes combine to give $k^3$.
- Cube root the linear scale factor: $\sqrt[3]{k}$.
  - _why:_ Taking a cube root recovers a linear scale factor from a known volume scale factor. Starting with linear factor $k$, the volume factor is $k^3$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** If the length of a shape doubles, what happens to its surface area?

**Back:** It is multiplied by 4 (because $2^2 = 4$).

**Choices:**

- It is multiplied by 4 (because $2^2 = 4$). ✔
  - _why:_ Surface area changes with the square of the linear scale factor. Doubling every length gives an area scale factor of $2^2 = 4$.
- It is multiplied by 2 (because $2 \times 1 = 2$).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 2 treats surface area like a one-dimensional length. Surface area changes in two dimensions, so doubling lengths multiplies it by $2^2 = 4$.
- It is multiplied by 8 (because $2^3 = 8$).
  - _why:_ Cubing the scale factor gives the change in volume, not surface area. Surface area uses the square, so doubling lengths multiplies it by $2^2 = 4$.
- It is multiplied by 16 (because $4^2 = 16$).
  - _why:_ This incorrectly squares the already calculated area factor of 4. The linear factor 2 should be squared once, giving a surface-area multiplier of $2^2 = 4$.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** If the length of a shape triples, what happens to its volume?

**Back:** It is multiplied by 27 (because $3^3 = 27$).

**Choices:**

- It is multiplied by 27 (because $3^3 = 27$). ✔
  - _why:_ Volume changes with the cube of the linear scale factor. Tripling every dimension gives a volume scale factor of $3^3 = 27$.
- It is multiplied by 9 (because $3^2 = 9$).
  - _why:_ Squaring the linear factor gives the area scale factor, not the volume scale factor. Volume changes in three dimensions, so tripling lengths gives $3^3 = 27$.
- It is multiplied by 3 (because $3 \times 1 = 3$).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 3 accounts for only one dimension and describes the change in length. Volume uses three dimensions, so the multiplier is $3^3 = 27$.
- It is multiplied by 81 (because $3^4 = 81$).
  - _why:_ Using a fourth power adds an extra dimension that volume does not have. Volume depends on three dimensions, so tripling lengths multiplies it by $3^3 = 27$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** What value will the linear scale factor ($k$) have if the shape is being shrunk?

**Back:** $$k$ will be less than 1.

**Choices:**

- $$k$ will be less than 1. ✔
  - _why:_ A shrink makes every corresponding length smaller, so the new-to-original length ratio, and therefore the linear scale factor $k$, must lie between $0$ and $1$.
- $$k$ will be greater than 1.
  - _why:_ A scale factor greater than $1$ produces an enlargement rather than a reduction. For a shape being shrunk, $k$ must be less than $1$.
- $$k$ will be equal to 1.
  - _why:_ A scale factor of exactly $1$ leaves every length unchanged, so the shape is neither enlarged nor shrunk. Shrinking requires $k$ to be less than $1$.
- $$k$ will be less than 0.
  - _why:_ Scale factors for ordinary lengths are positive because lengths cannot be negative. A shrinking transformation has a positive value of $k$ that is less than $1$.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the linear scale factor from an area scale factor, you must [ ___ ] the area scale factor.

**Back:** To find the linear scale factor from an area scale factor, you must square root the area scale factor.

**Choices:**

- square root ✔
  - _why:_ For similar shapes, the area scale factor equals $k^2$, where $k$ is the linear scale factor. Taking the square root therefore recovers $k$.
- square
  - _why:_ Squaring applies the area relationship in the wrong direction and produces $k^4$. The linear scale factor is found by taking the square root of the area scale factor.
- cube root
  - _why:_ A cube root converts a volume scale factor into a linear scale factor. Since the given factor compares areas, its square root gives the linear scale factor.
- halve
  - _why:_ Halving is not the inverse of squaring and only happens to work in isolated cases. The area scale factor must be square rooted to obtain $k$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Two similar solids have surface areas in the ratio $25:49$. What is their length ratio?

**Back:** $$5:7$ (the square root of the area ratio).

**Choices:**

- $$5:7$ (the square root of the area ratio). ✔
  - _why:_ Surface-area ratios are the squares of length ratios. Taking the square root of each part of $25:49$ gives $sqrt{25}:sqrt{49}=5:7$.
- $$25:49$ (the same as the area ratio).
  - _why:_ This incorrectly uses the surface-area ratio as the length ratio. Since area scales with the square of length, square rooting $25:49$ gives $5:7$.
- $$7:5$ (the reverse of the square root ratio).
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the two solids after square rooting. Keeping the original order, $sqrt{25}:sqrt{49}$ gives the length ratio $5:7$.
- $$5:49$ (the square root of the first area only).
  - _why:_ This square roots only the first part of the area ratio. Both values must be square rooted, so $25:49$ becomes $5:7$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a frustum?

**Back:** The solid remaining after the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to its base.

**Choices:**

- The solid remaining after the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to its base. ✔
  - _why:_ A frustum is formed by slicing a cone or pyramid along a plane parallel to its base and removing the smaller, similar solid at the top.
- The solid remaining after the top of a cone or pyramid is cut off perpendicular to its base.
  - _why:_ A perpendicular cut does not produce the two parallel faces characteristic of a frustum. The top must be removed using a cut parallel to the base.
- The solid remaining after the base of a cone or pyramid is cut off parallel to its top.
  - _why:_ Removing the bottom would leave the small cone or pyramid rather than the frustum normally required. A frustum is the remainder after the top is removed parallel to the base.
- The solid remaining after the side of a cone or pyramid is cut off through its apex.
  - _why:_ A cut through the apex does not remove a smaller similar cone and does not create a frustum. The cut must be parallel to the base and below the apex.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** How is the volume of a frustum calculated?

**Back:** Total volume of the large cone minus the volume of the small cone removed from the top.

**Choices:**

- Total volume of the large cone minus the volume of the small cone removed from the top. ✔
  - _why:_ The original large cone can be divided into the frustum and the small cone removed. Subtracting the small cone’s volume therefore leaves the frustum’s volume.
- Total volume of the large cone plus the volume of the small cone removed from the top.
  - _why:_ Adding counts the removed cone as extra material rather than taking it away. The frustum’s volume is the large cone’s total volume minus the small cone’s volume.
- Total volume of the small cone minus the volume of the large cone remaining at the bottom.
  - _why:_ This subtraction is reversed, which would produce a negative value because the small cone has less volume. Subtract the small cone from the large cone.
- Total surface area of the large cone minus the surface area of the small cone removed from the top.
  - _why:_ Surface area and volume measure different quantities, so their values cannot be subtracted. Both measurements must be volumes: large cone volume minus small cone volume.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** The formula for the volume of a cone is $V = $ $$\text{[...]}$$.

**Back:** The formula for the volume of a cone is $V = $ $$\boxed{\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h}$$.

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$$ ✔
  - _why:_ A cone has one third of the volume of a cylinder with the same circular base and perpendicular height, so its volume is $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$$\pi r^2h$$
  - _why:_ This omits the factor of $\frac{1}{3}$ and therefore calculates the volume of the corresponding cylinder. A cone occupies one third of that volume, giving $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$$\frac{1}{3}\pi rh$$
  - _why:_ This fails to square the radius when finding the circular base area. Since the base area is $\pi r^2$, the cone’s volume is $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.
- $$$\frac{1}{3}\pi rh^2$$
  - _why:_ This incorrectly squares the height rather than the radius. The circular base contributes $\pi r^2$, which is multiplied by $h$ and divided by $3$, giving $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** When using the cone volume formula, what must you do if the question provides the diameter?

**Back:** Halve the diameter to find the radius ($r$) first.

**Choices:**

- Halve the diameter to find the radius ($r$) first. ✔
  - _why:_ The radius is the distance from the centre to the circle’s edge, while the diameter spans the full width. Therefore, $r$ is half the given diameter.
- Double the diameter to find the radius ($r$) first.
  - _why:_ Doubling converts a radius into a diameter, not the other way around. When the diameter is given, halve it to obtain the radius used in the formula.
- Use the diameter as the radius ($r$) directly.
  - _why:_ Using the diameter directly as $r$ makes the radius twice its correct value and the $r^2$ term four times too large. The diameter must be halved.
- Square the diameter to find the radius ($r$) first.
  - _why:_ Squaring the diameter does not convert it into a radius; squaring occurs later in the formula. First halve the diameter to find $r$.

**Choices:**

- The area scale factor is already squared; you must square root it to find $k$ before cubing. ✔

- The area scale factor is already cubed; you must cube root it to find $k$ before squaring.

  - _why:_ Dividing by 2 does not reverse a square; this confuses an exponent with multiplication. Take the square root of the area scale factor to recover $k$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Two similar cylinders have lengths 5 cm and 8 cm. What is the volume scale factor from small to large?

**Back:** $$$(\frac{8}{5})^3 = \frac{512}{125}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$(\frac{8}{5})^3 = \frac{512}{125}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ The length scale factor from small to large is $\frac{8}{5}$. Volumes scale with the cube of the length factor, giving $\left(\frac{8}{5}\right)^3=\frac{512}{125}$.
- $$$(\frac{8}{5})^2 = \frac{64}{25}$$
  - _why:_ Squaring the length scale factor finds the area scale factor, not the volume scale factor. For volume, cube $\frac{8}{5}$ to obtain $\frac{512}{125}$.
- $$$(\frac{5}{8})^3 = \frac{125}{512}$$
  - _why:_ This reverses the direction of the scale factor and gives large to small. From small to large, cube $\frac{8}{5}$, producing $\frac{512}{125}$.
- $$$(\frac{8}{5}) \times 3 = \frac{24}{5}$$
  - _why:_ Cubing means multiplying the length scale factor by itself three times, not multiplying it by 3. The correct calculation gives $\left(\frac{8}{5}\right)^3=\frac{512}{125}$.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** What measurements count as corresponding lengths in similar 3D shapes?

**Back:** Matching measurements in the same position (e.g., radius with radius, or height with height).

**Choices:**

- Matching measurements in the same position (e.g., radius with radius, or height with height). ✔
  - _why:_ Corresponding lengths occupy equivalent positions in similar shapes. For example, one solid’s radius matches the other solid’s radius, and its height matches the other height.
- Measurements with the same numerical value (e.g., a 5 cm radius with a 5 cm height).
  - _why:_ Equal numerical values do not make lengths corresponding, because similar shapes can have different sizes. Corresponding lengths occupy the same relative position, such as radius with radius.
- Any measurements in the same solid (e.g., radius with height, or diameter with slant height).
  - _why:_ Lengths from different positions in one solid are not automatically corresponding. The correct pairs match equivalent positions across the two solids, such as height with height.
- Matching measurements in opposite positions (e.g., radius with height, or length with width).
  - _why:_ Pairing different types of dimensions gives an invalid scale comparison. Corresponding lengths must occupy the same position in each shape, such as radius with radius.
