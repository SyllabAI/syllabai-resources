## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the sample space in probability?

**Back:** The complete list of all possible outcomes.

**Choices:**

- The complete list of all possible outcomes. ✔
  - _why:_ A sample space includes every outcome that could occur in an experiment, allowing probabilities to be calculated without accidentally omitting any possibilities.
- The complete list of all favourable outcomes.
  - _why:_ This confuses the sample space with the favourable outcomes for an event. The sample space is the complete list of all possible outcomes, whether favourable or not.
- The probability of every possible outcome.
  - _why:_ Probabilities may be assigned after the outcomes are identified, but they are not the sample space itself. The sample space is the complete list of all possible outcomes.
- The total number of all possible outcomes.
  - _why:_ This gives the size of the sample space rather than the sample space itself. The sample space is the complete list of the actual possible outcomes.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the main rule for systematic listing?

**Back:** Keep one choice fixed, list everything that goes with it, then move to the next choice.

**Choices:**

- Keep one choice fixed, list everything that goes with it, then move to the next choice. ✔
  - _why:_ Fixing one choice while listing every possible partner creates an organised pattern. Repeating this for each choice ensures that no combinations are missed or repeated.
- Keep one choice fixed, list one thing that goes with it, then move to the next choice.
  - _why:_ Listing only one partner for each fixed choice omits valid combinations. The correct method lists everything that goes with one fixed choice before moving on.
- Keep changing every choice, list each result from memory, then check them at the end.
  - _why:_ Changing all choices at once makes omissions and repetitions more likely. Systematic listing keeps one choice fixed and exhausts its combinations before moving to the next.
- Keep the choices unordered, list the easiest combinations first, then add any that were missed.
  - _why:_ Listing easy combinations first does not guarantee completeness and often causes duplicates. The reliable rule is to fix one choice and list everything paired with it.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** An [ ___ ] is one possible result, such as getting heads when you toss a coin.

**Back:** An outcome is one possible result, such as getting heads when you toss a coin.

**Choices:**

- outcome ✔
  - _why:_ An outcome is one individual result of a probability experiment, such as heads from one coin toss or a six from rolling a die.
- event
  - _why:_ An event is a set of one or more outcomes, not necessarily one individual result. The term for one possible result is outcome.
- sample space
  - _why:_ A sample space contains every possible outcome of an experiment, rather than describing one result. One possible result is called an outcome.
- probability
  - _why:_ Probability measures how likely an outcome or event is; it is not the result itself. One possible result of an experiment is an outcome.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** A cafe offers 2 sandwiches and 3 drinks. How many lunch combinations are possible?

**Back:** 6 (calculated as $2 \times 3$)

**Choices:**

- 6 (calculated as $2 \times 3$) ✔
  - _why:_ Each of the 2 sandwiches can be paired with any of the 3 drinks, so the multiplication principle gives $2 \times 3 = 6$ combinations.
- 5 (calculated as $2 + 3$)
  - _why:_ Adding counts the five individual menu items, not the sandwich-and-drink pairings. Each sandwich pairs with three drinks, so $2 \times 3 = 6$ combinations.
- 3 (calculated as $2 + 3 - 2$)
  - _why:_ This subtraction incorrectly removes possible pairings and effectively considers only one sandwich. Both sandwiches pair with all three drinks, giving $2 \times 3 = 6$ combinations.
- 9 (calculated as $3 \times 3$)
  - _why:_ This mistakenly uses three sandwich choices as well as three drinks. There are only two sandwiches, so the correct calculation is $2 \times 3 = 6$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** To keep a list neat for a coin toss, you can use the short labels [ ___ ] and T.

**Back:** To keep a list neat for a coin toss, you can use the short labels H and T.

**Choices:**

- H ✔
  - _why:_ H is the standard short label for heads. Together, H and T allow coin-toss sequences to be recorded clearly and compactly.
- C
  - _why:_ C might be chosen to represent coin, but it does not identify which side appears. The standard label for heads is H, paired with T for tails.
- A
  - _why:_ A is not a standard label for either result of a coin toss. Heads is abbreviated to H, while tails is abbreviated to T.
- X
  - _why:_ X could mark an unspecified result, but it does not communicate that heads occurred. The conventional short label for heads is H.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Why are HHT and HTH considered different outcomes when tossing a coin three times?

**Back:** Because order matters: the tails happens on a different toss.

**Choices:**

- Because order matters: the tails happens on a different toss. ✔
  - _why:_ HHT has tails on the third toss, whereas HTH has tails on the second toss. Since the position of each result matters, the sequences are distinct outcomes.
- Because the totals differ: one sequence contains more heads than the other.
  - _why:_ Both sequences contain two heads and one tail, so their totals are identical. They are different because order matters and the tail occurs on a different toss.
- Because their probabilities differ: HHT is more likely to occur than HTH.
  - _why:_ For a fair coin, both specified sequences have probability $(\frac{1}{2})^3 = \frac{1}{8}$. They differ only because their heads and tails occur in different orders.
- Because tails becomes less likely: its probability changes on each successive toss.
  - _why:_ Independent coin tosses do not become more or less likely based on their position. HHT and HTH are distinct because the tail occurs on different tosses.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** How many total outcomes are in the sample space for tossing a coin three times?

**Back:** 8

**Choices:**

- 8 ✔
  - _why:_ Each of the three tosses has 2 possible results, H or T. The multiplication principle therefore gives $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ outcomes.
- 6
  - _why:_ Multiplying the 2 possible results by the 3 tosses gives 6, but choices across successive tosses multiply. The correct calculation is $2^3 = 8$.
- 9
  - _why:_ Squaring the number of tosses gives $3^2 = 9$, but there are two choices on each of three tosses. Therefore, the total is $2^3 = 8$.
- 4
  - _why:_ This counts only two tosses, since $2^2 = 4$. Including the third independent toss doubles the number of sequences, giving $2^3 = 8$.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Back:** When making numbers from cards, each card can usually be used once unless the question says otherwise.

**Choices:**

- once ✔
  - _why:_ Each physical card represents one available digit, so it can normally be selected only once when constructing a number unless repetition is explicitly permitted.
- twice
  - _why:_ Using every card twice incorrectly treats each card as reusable. The correct term is “once” because each physical card can normally be used only one time.
- repeatedly
  - _why:_ Unlimited repetition ignores the fixed supply of number cards. The blank should be filled by “once”, as a card is unavailable after it has been used.
- more than once
  - _why:_ This incorrectly allows a card to be selected again. Each card can usually be used “once”, although the available cards may be arranged in any order.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** When listing matches between teams, [ ___ ] because Lions v Tigers is the same match as Tigers v Lions.

**Back:** When listing matches between teams, order does not matter because Lions v Tigers is the same match as Tigers v Lions.

**Choices:**

- order does not matter ✔
  - _why:_ A match is determined by the pair of teams involved, not by which team is named first. Reversing the names therefore does not create another match.
- order matters
  - _why:_ Treating order as important would count Lions v Tigers and Tigers v Lions separately. The correct fill is “order does not matter” because both name the same pairing.
- teams can play twice
  - _why:_ The statement concerns whether reversed team names represent a new pairing, not how often teams play. The correct fill is “order does not matter”.
- each ordering is counted separately
  - _why:_ Counting each ordering separately would double-count every match. The correct fill is “order does not matter” because reversing the two teams leaves the pairing unchanged.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** List all unique matches if four teams (A, B, C, D) play each other once.

**Back:** A v B, A v C, A v DB v C, B v DC v D

**Choices:**

- A v B, A v C, A v DB v C, B v DC v D ✔
  - _why:_ Each unordered pair appears exactly once, producing six matches altogether. Reversing a pairing would name the same match and must not be counted again.
- A v B, A v C, A v DB v A, B v CC v D
  - _why:_ B v A duplicates A v B, while B v D is missing. Reversed team order does not create a new match, so the correct list contains each of the six pairs once.
- A v B, A v C, A v DB v C, B v DA v A
  - _why:_ A v A is not a match between two different teams, and C v D has been omitted. Four teams produce six distinct pairings with no self-matches.
- A v B, A v C, A v DB v C, B v DC v B
  - _why:_ C v B repeats B v C and leaves out C v D. Each pair must appear once regardless of ordering, giving six unique matches in total.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What does it mean for outcomes to be equally likely?

**Back:** Each outcome has the same chance of happening.

**Choices:**

- Each outcome has the same chance of happening. ✔
  - _why:_ Equally likely outcomes have identical probabilities, even though a short sequence of trials may not produce each outcome the same number of times.
- Each outcome happens the same number of times in every experiment.
  - _why:_ Equal frequencies in every experiment are not guaranteed because random variation occurs. Equally likely means that each outcome has the same chance before the experiment.
- Each outcome has a different chance of happening.
  - _why:_ Different probabilities are the opposite of being equally likely. The correct definition is that every outcome has the same chance of happening.
- At least one outcome has a 50% chance of happening.
  - _why:_ A 50% outcome does not make all outcomes equally likely, especially when there are more than two possibilities. Every outcome must instead have the same chance.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the formula for probability using a sample space of equally likely outcomes?

**Back:** $$$\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$

**Choices:**

- $$$\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$ ✔
  - _why:_ For equally likely outcomes, probability is the proportion of all possible outcomes that satisfy the event, so favourable outcomes form the numerator and total outcomes the denominator.
- $$$\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{number of unfavourable outcomes}}$$
  - _why:_ Dividing by unfavourable outcomes calculates favourable-to-unfavourable odds, not probability. Probability divides the number of favourable outcomes by the total number of outcomes.
- $$$\frac{\text{total number of outcomes}}{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}$$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required fraction and can produce values greater than 1. The favourable outcomes must be divided by the total number of outcomes.
- $$$\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes} - \text{number of favourable outcomes}}$$
  - _why:_ Subtracting favourable outcomes leaves only the unfavourable outcomes in the denominator, producing odds rather than probability. The denominator should be the total number of outcomes.

**Back:** A grid (with one dice across the top and one down the side).

**Choices:**

- A grid (with one dice across the top and one down the side). ✔
  - _why:_ A grid pairs every result on the first die with every result on the second die, displaying all 36 ordered outcomes systematically without omissions.
- A tree diagram (with one dice on each separate branch).
  - _why:_ A tree diagram can represent repeated events, but it is less neat for the 36 pairs from two dice. A grid displays every pairing directly.
- A tally chart (with each dice total listed in one column).
  - _why:_ A tally chart records observed frequencies rather than systematically listing the theoretical sample space. A grid with one die on each axis shows all possible pairs.
- A Venn diagram (with one dice represented by each circle).
  - _why:_ A Venn diagram represents relationships between sets, not every ordered pair of dice results. A grid is the clearest way to organise the 36 outcomes.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** Box A has cards 1, 4, 7. Box B has 3, 5, 6. If one is picked from each, when is the product odd?

**Back:** When both cards picked are odd numbers.

**Choices:**

- When both cards picked are odd numbers. ✔
  - _why:_ A product is odd only when every factor is odd. Therefore, the card selected from Box A and the card selected from Box B must both be odd.
- When both cards picked are even numbers.
  - _why:_ Multiplying two even numbers produces an even product, not an odd one. The product is odd only when both selected cards are odd numbers.
- When at least one card picked is an odd number.
  - _why:_ Having one odd card is insufficient because an odd number multiplied by an even number is even. Both selected cards must be odd for the product to be odd.
- When one card picked is odd and the other is even.
  - _why:_ An odd number multiplied by an even number always gives an even product. To obtain an odd product, both cards picked must be odd numbers.
