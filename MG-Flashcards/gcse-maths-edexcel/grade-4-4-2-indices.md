## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In the expression $x^5$, what is the mathematical name for $x$ and $5$?

**Back:** $x$ is the base$5$ is the index

**Choices:**

- $x$ is the base$5$ is the index ✔
  - _why:_ The repeated factor is the base, while the index states how many times that factor is multiplied by itself. Therefore, $x$ is the base and $5$ is the index.
- $x$ is the index$5$ is the base
  - _why:_ The labels have been reversed. In $x^5$, $x$ is the quantity repeatedly multiplied, making it the base, while $5$ is the index.
- $x$ is the coefficient$5$ is the index
  - _why:_ A coefficient is a numerical multiplier written before a variable, so $x$ is not the coefficient here. It is the base, and $5$ is the index.
- $x$ is the base$5$ is the coefficient
  - _why:_ The number $5$ is not multiplying the power, so it is not a coefficient. It is the index, while $x$ is the base being raised to that power.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** When multiplying powers with the same base, $a^m \times a^n = a^{\text{[...]}}$.

**Back:** When multiplying powers with the same base, $a^m \times a^n = a^{\boxed{m+n}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$m+n$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying powers with the same base combines all repeated factors, so their indices are added. Therefore, $a^m \times a^n=a^{m+n}$.
- $$m-n$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the indices is the rule for dividing powers with the same base. Multiplication requires addition, giving the exponent $m+n$.
- $$mn$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices is used when raising an existing power to another power, as in $(a^m)^n$. Here the powers are multiplied, so the exponent is $m+n$.
- $$\frac{m}{n}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the indices is not an index law for multiplying equal bases. The repeated factors combine, so the correct resulting exponent is $m+n$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $x^6 \times x^5$.

**Back:** $$x^{11}$

**Choices:**

- $$x^{11}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The powers have the same base, so their indices are added when multiplying. Since $6+5=11$, the product simplifies to $x^{11}$.
- $$x^{30}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices confuses this rule with neither valid multiplication nor the power-of-a-power law. Equal bases are multiplied by adding indices, giving $x^{11}$.
- $$x^1$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the indices is appropriate for division of equal bases, not multiplication. Adding them gives $6+5=11$, so the result is $x^{11}$.
- $$2x^{11}$
  - _why:_ The implicit coefficients are both $1$, whose product remains $1$, rather than $2$. The indices add to $11$, so the product is $x^{11}$.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $\frac{w^9}{w^4}$.

**Back:** $$w^5$

**Choices:**

- $$w^5$ ✔
  - _why:_ When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the denominator’s index from the numerator’s index. Since $9-4=5$, the expression simplifies to $w^5$.
- $$w^{13}$
  - _why:_ Adding indices is the rule for multiplying equal bases, not dividing them. Division requires subtraction, so $9-4=5$ and the result is $w^5$.
- $$w^{36}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices is not the division rule for powers. The denominator’s index must be subtracted from the numerator’s, producing $w^{9-4}=w^5$.
- $$w^{\frac{9}{4}}$
  - _why:_ The indices themselves are not divided when powers share a base. Instead, subtract the denominator’s index from the numerator’s, giving $w^{9-4}=w^5$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** A letter on its own, such as $x$, has an invisible index of [ ___ ].

**Back:** A letter on its own, such as $x$, has an invisible index of 1.

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ A variable written without an explicit index represents one copy of itself. Thus $x$ means $x^1$, so its invisible index is 1.
- 0
  - _why:_ An index of 0 would give $x^0=1$, not $x$. A lone variable represents $x^1$, so its invisible index is 1.
- 2
  - _why:_ An index of 2 would mean $x^2=x\times x$, which contains two factors of $x$. A lone $x$ is $x^1$, so the index is 1.
- -1
  - _why:_ An index of -1 represents a reciprocal, since $x^{-1}=\frac{1}{x}$. A letter on its own is $x^1$, so its invisible index is 1.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $4m^3 \times 6m$.

**Back:** $$24m^4$

**Choices:**

- $$24m^4$ ✔
  - _why:_ Multiply the coefficients to get $4\times6=24$, then add the indices of $m$, treating $m$ as $m^1$. This gives $24m^{3+1}=24m^4$.
- $$24m^3$
  - _why:_ Keeping the index at 3 overlooks the invisible index of 1 on $m$. The coefficients multiply to 24 and the indices add, giving $24m^4$.
- $$10m^4$
  - _why:_ The coefficients must be multiplied, not added: $4\times6=24$. The indices also add as $3+1=4$, so the correct product is $24m^4$.
- $$24m^6$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices incorrectly treats the lone $m$ as though its index altered the 3 multiplicatively. Indices add here, so the result is $24m^4$.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $\frac{18p^5q^4}{6p^2q}$.

**Back:** $$3p^3q^3$

**Choices:**

- $$3p^3q^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Divide the coefficients to get $18\div6=3$, then subtract corresponding indices: $5-2=3$ for $p$ and $4-1=3$ for $q$.
- $$3p^3q^4$
  - _why:_ Leaving the index of $q$ unchanged overlooks the denominator’s invisible index of 1. Subtracting it gives $q^{4-1}=q^3$, so the answer is $3p^3q^3$.
- $$12p^3q^3$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the coefficients is not how numerical factors are divided. Since $18\div6=3$, and both variable indices reduce to 3, the answer is $3p^3q^3$.
- $$3p^7q^5$
  - _why:_ Adding indices applies when multiplying powers, but this expression is a quotient. Subtracting denominator indices gives $p^{5-2}q^{4-1}$, so the result is $3p^3q^3$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** When raising a power to another power, $(a^m)^n = a^{\text{[...]}}$.

**Back:** When raising a power to another power, $(a^m)^n = a^{\boxed{mn}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$mn$ ✔
  - _why:_ Raising a power to another power repeats the original power $n$ times, so the indices multiply. Therefore, $(a^m)^n=a^{mn}$.
- $$m+n$
  - _why:_ Adding indices applies when multiplying separate powers with the same base. For a power raised to another power, the indices multiply, giving $mn$.
- $$m^n$
  - _why:_ Raising one index to the other is not the power-of-a-power rule. The outer power repeats the inner power, so the resulting exponent is the product $mn$.
- $$\frac{m}{n}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the indices does not describe repeatedly raising a power. A power raised to another power requires the indices to be multiplied, producing $mn$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Back:** $$t^{12}$

**Choices:**

- $$t^{12}$ ✔
  - _why:_ When a power is raised to another power, multiply the indices. Multiplying $4$ by $3$ gives $12$, so the simplified expression is $t^{12}$.
- $$t^7$
  - _why:_ Adding $4$ and $3$ confuses the power-of-a-power rule with multiplying powers of the same base. Here the indices must be multiplied, giving $t^{12}$.
- $$t^{64}$
  - _why:_ Calculating $4^3$ incorrectly treats the index itself as the base. The power-of-a-power rule multiplies the indices, so $4 \times 3=12$ and the answer is $t^{12}$.
- $$t^4$
  - _why:_ Leaving the index unchanged ignores the outer power of $3$. A power raised to a power has its indices multiplied, so the correct result is $t^{12}$.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $(4x)^2$.

**Back:** $$16x^2$

**Choices:**

- $$16x^2$ ✔
  - _why:_ The power of $2$ applies to both factors inside the brackets: $4^2=16$ and $x^2=x^2$. Therefore, $(4x)^2=16x^2$.
- $$8x^2$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $4$ by $2$ instead of squaring $4$ gives the incorrect coefficient $8$. The coefficient should be $4^2=16$, so the answer is $16x^2$.
- $$16x$
  - _why:_ Squaring only the coefficient ignores that the power applies to every factor inside the brackets. Both $4$ and $x$ are squared, giving $16x^2$.
- $$4x^2$
  - _why:_ Squaring only the variable leaves the coefficient unchanged, but the outer power applies to the entire bracket. Since $4^2=16$, the correct answer is $16x^2$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** Simplify $(3a^2b)^3$.

**Back:** $$27a^6b^3$

**Choices:**

- $$27a^6b^3$ ✔
  - _why:_ Cube every factor inside the brackets: $3^3=27$, $(a^2)^3=a^6$, and $b^3=b^3$. Combining these gives $27a^6b^3$.
- $$9a^6b^3$
  - _why:_ Using $3^2=9$ applies the exponent on $a$ to the coefficient instead of cubing it. The coefficient is $3^3=27$, giving $27a^6b^3$.
- $$27a^5b^3$
  - _why:_ Adding the indices $2$ and $3$ gives $a^5$, but a power raised to a power requires multiplication. Since $2 \times 3=6$, the answer is $27a^6b^3$.
- $$27a^6b$
  - _why:_ Leaving $b$ unchanged ignores that the outer power applies to every factor in the bracket. Cubing $b$ gives $b^3$, so the answer is $27a^6b^3$.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** Any non-zero base to the power of 0 equals [ ___ ].

**Back:** Any non-zero base to the power of 0 equals 1.

**Choices:**

- 1 ✔
  - _why:_ For every non-zero number $a$, dividing $a^m$ by itself gives $a^{m-m}=a^0$ and also equals $1$. Therefore, $a^0=1$.
- 0
  - _why:_ A zero index does not make the value zero; it makes the exponent difference zero when equal powers are divided. Any non-zero base to power $0$ equals $1$.
- -1
  - _why:_ A zero index does not introduce a negative sign. Using the index law $a^m \div a^m=a^0$, the quotient is $1$, so any non-zero base gives $1$.
- undefined
  - _why:_ The zero-index rule is defined for every non-zero base. Since a non-zero quantity divided by itself is $1$, any non-zero base raised to power $0$ equals $1$.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the reciprocal of $5$?

**Back:** $$\frac{1}{5}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{5}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The reciprocal of a non-zero number is found by writing $1$ divided by that number. Therefore, the reciprocal of $5$ is $\frac{1}{5}$.
- $$-\frac{1}{5}$
  - _why:_ Taking a reciprocal does not change a positive number into a negative number. The reciprocal must multiply by $5$ to make $1$, so it is $\frac{1}{5}$.
- $$\frac{5}{1}$
  - _why:_ Writing $5$ as $\frac{5}{1}$ does not invert it. A reciprocal swaps the numerator and denominator, so the reciprocal is $\frac{1}{5}$.
- $$\frac{1}{25}$
  - _why:_ Using $25$ in the denominator squares $5$ instead of taking its reciprocal. The number that multiplies by $5$ to give $1$ is $\frac{1}{5}$.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** A negative index means you must take the [ ___ ].

**Back:** A negative index means you must take the reciprocal.

**Choices:**

- reciprocal ✔
  - _why:_ A negative index reverses the corresponding positive power: $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$ for $a \ne 0$. This means taking the reciprocal.
- opposite
  - _why:_ Taking the opposite changes the sign of a value, whereas a negative index changes its position in a fraction. The required operation is taking the reciprocal.
- square root
  - _why:_ A square root corresponds to an index of $\frac{1}{2}$, not a negative index. A negative index instead means taking the reciprocal of the positive-index power.
- absolute value
  - _why:_ Absolute value removes a negative sign from a number but does not describe negative indices. A negative index means taking the reciprocal, as in $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** $$a^{-n} = \text{[...]}$

**Back:** $$a^{-n} = \boxed{\frac{1}{a^n}}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{a^n}$ ✔
  - _why:_ A negative index means taking the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. Therefore, for $a \ne 0$, $a^{-n}$ equals $\frac{1}{a^n}$.
- $$-a^n$
  - _why:_ A negative index does not place a negative sign before the expression. It indicates a reciprocal, so $a^{-n}$ equals $\frac{1}{a^n}$ for $a \ne 0$.
- $$\frac{1}{a^{-n}}$
  - _why:_ This takes the reciprocal of an already negative power, which gives $a^n$ rather than $a^{-n}$. The correct positive-index form is $\frac{1}{a^n}$.
- $$-\frac{1}{a^n}$
  - _why:_ Taking the reciprocal is correct, but adding a negative sign is not. The negative index changes the power’s position, not its sign, so the result is $\frac{1}{a^n}$.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** Calculate the value of $2^{-4}$.

**Back:** $$\frac{1}{16}$

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{16}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The negative index means take the reciprocal: $2^{-4}=\frac{1}{2^4}$. Since $2^4=16$, the value is $\frac{1}{16}$.
- $$-16$
  - _why:_ A negative index does not make the final value negative. It means take the reciprocal of $2^4=16$, so $2^{-4}=\frac{1}{16}$.
- $$\frac{1}{8}$
  - _why:_ The denominator $8$ comes from calculating $2^3$, so the exponent has effectively been reduced by one. Since $2^4=16$, the correct value is $\frac{1}{16}$.
- $$16$
  - _why:_ Calculating $2^4=16$ ignores the negative sign on the index. A negative index requires the reciprocal, making the correct value $\frac{1}{16}$.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** If $y^3 \times y^a = y^8$, what is the value of $a$?

**Back:** $$5$

**Choices:**

- $$5$ ✔
  - _why:_ When multiplying powers with the same base, add the indices. Therefore $3+a=8$, and subtracting $3$ from both sides gives $a=5$.
- $$11$
  - _why:_ Adding $3$ to $8$ reverses the required calculation. The index law gives $3+a=8$, so subtracting $3$ shows that the correct value is $a=5$.
- $$24$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices is not the rule for multiplying powers with the same base. The indices must be added, giving $3+a=8$ and hence $a=5$.
- $$6$
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one subtraction error. Since multiplication of equal bases adds indices, $3+a=8$, and calculating $8-3$ correctly gives $a=5$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** If $(x^5)^b = x^{20}$, what is the value of $b$?

**Back:** $$4$

**Choices:**

- $$4$ ✔
  - _why:_ When raising a power to another power, multiply the indices. Thus $(x^5)^b=x^{5b}$, so $5b=20$ and dividing by $5$ gives $b=4$.
- $$15$
  - _why:_ Subtracting the indices confuses the power-of-a-power rule with rearranging an addition equation. Here the indices multiply, so $5b=20$ and the correct value is $b=4$.
- $$25$
  - _why:_ Adding the indices is used when multiplying powers with the same base, not when raising a power to a power. Since $5b=20$, the correct value is $b=4$.
- $$100$
  - _why:_ Multiplying $5$ by $20$ does not solve for the unknown index. The power-of-a-power rule gives $5b=20$, so division by $5$ gives $b=4$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** If $\frac{x^5}{x^a} = x^7$, what is the value of $a$?

**Back:** $$-2$

**Choices:**

- $$-2$ ✔
  - _why:_ When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the denominator’s index: $x^5/x^a=x^{5-a}$. Therefore $5-a=7$, which rearranges to $a=-2$.
- $$2$
  - _why:_ The negative sign before $a$ must be preserved when solving $5-a=7$. Subtracting $5$ gives $-a=2$, so multiplying by $-1$ gives $a=-2$.
- $$12$
  - _why:_ Adding $5$ and $7$ ignores the quotient law and does not solve the exponent equation. Division gives $5-a=7$, which rearranges to the correct value $a=-2$.
- $$-12$
  - _why:_ Combining $5$ and $7$ with the wrong operation produces an excessive negative value. From $5-a=7$, subtracting $5$ gives $-a=2$, so $a=-2$.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Write $16 \times 4^6$ as a single power of 4.

**Back:** $$4^8$

**Choices:**

- $$4^8$ ✔
  - _why:_ Since $16=4^2$, the expression is $4^2\times4^6$. Multiplying powers with the same base means adding the indices, so the result is $4^{2+6}=4^8$.
- $$4^7$
  - _why:_ This treats $16$ as one additional factor of $4$, but $16=4^2$. Therefore the indices are $2$ and $6$, which add to give the correct result $4^8$.
- $$4^{12}$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the indices confuses multiplication of equal-base powers with the power-of-a-power rule. Since $16=4^2$, the calculation is $4^2\times4^6=4^{2+6}=4^8$.
- $$4^6$
  - _why:_ This effectively ignores the factor of $16$. Rewriting $16$ as $4^2$ gives $4^2\times4^6$, and adding the indices produces the correct single power $4^8$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** If $1000^3 = 10^x$, what is the value of $x$?

**Back:** $$9$

**Choices:**

- $$9$ ✔
  - _why:_ Since $1000=10^3$, cubing gives $(10^3)^3=10^{3\times3}=10^9$. Therefore the exponent satisfying $1000^3=10^x$ is $x=9$.
- $$6$
  - _why:_ Adding the two $3$s confuses a power of a power with multiplication of equal-base powers. The indices multiply, so $(10^3)^3=10^9$ and $x=9$.
- $$27$
  - _why:_ Cubing the index itself is not the power-of-a-power rule. Since $1000=10^3$, the indices multiply as $3\times3$, giving $10^9$ and therefore $x=9$.
- $$3000$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the base value by the outer exponent does not express the result as a power of $10$. Writing $1000=10^3$ gives $(10^3)^3=10^9$, so $x=9$.
