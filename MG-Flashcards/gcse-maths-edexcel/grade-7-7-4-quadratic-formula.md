## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** The standard form of a quadratic equation is $\text{[...]}$ where $a \neq 0$.

**Back:** The standard form of a quadratic equation is $\boxed{ax^2+bx+c=0}$ where $a \neq 0$.

**Choices:**

- $$ax^2+bx+c=0$ ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic in standard form has all terms on one side, ordered by descending powers of $x$, and equals zero, with $a \neq 0$.
- $$ax^2+bx+c=x$
  - _why:_ Leaving an $x$ on the right does not give standard form. All terms must be collected on one side to produce $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- $$ax^2+bx=c$
  - _why:_ This leaves the constant on the right-hand side rather than collecting every term on one side. Standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- $$ax^3+bx+c=0$
  - _why:_ Using $x^3$ makes the equation cubic, not quadratic. A quadratic has highest power $2$, so its standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the first step required before using the quadratic formula?

**Back:** Rearrange the equation into standard form ($ax^2+bx+c=0$).

**Choices:**

- Rearrange the equation into standard form ($ax^2+bx+c=0$). ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$ can only be identified reliably after every term is collected on one side and the equation equals zero.
- Rearrange the equation into factorised form ($(ax+b)(cx+d)=0$).
  - _why:_ Factorised form is not required and may be difficult or impossible to obtain neatly. The quadratic formula requires standard form, $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- Rearrange the equation into completed square form ($a(x+b)^2+c=0$).
  - _why:_ Completing the square is a different solving method and is unnecessary before applying the formula. First rearrange the equation into $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
- Rearrange the equation into linear form ($ax+b=0$).
  - _why:_ Removing the squared term changes the equation and loses its quadratic solutions. Instead, retain all terms and rearrange into standard form, $ax^2+bx+c=0$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the quadratic formula used instead of factorising?

**Back:** It works for any quadratic, including those with decimal or surd answers that do not factorise neatly.

**Choices:**

- It works for any quadratic, including those with decimal or surd answers that do not factorise neatly. ✔
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula applies to every quadratic written in standard form, so it remains effective when integer factorisation is not available and roots are irrational or decimal.
- It works only for factorisable quadratics, including those with integer answers that can be found using brackets neatly.
  - _why:_ Restricting the formula to factorisable quadratics reverses its main advantage. It works for any quadratic, including equations whose decimal or surd roots prevent neat factorisation.
- It works for linear equations, including those with decimal or fraction answers that do not rearrange neatly.
  - _why:_ The quadratic formula is specifically derived for equations containing an $x^2$ term, not linear equations. It solves any quadratic, including ones that do not factorise neatly.
- It works for some quadratics, including only those with whole-number answers that do not require square roots.
  - _why:_ The formula is not limited to whole-number roots. It works for any quadratic and can produce fractions, decimals, surds, or no real solutions.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** State the quadratic formula used to solve $ax^2+bx+c=0$.

**Back:** $$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$

**Choices:**

- $$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The formula gives both roots by using plus and minus before the square root of the discriminant, with the entire numerator divided by $2a$.
- $$x=\frac{b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ A common sign error is using $b$ instead of its additive inverse. The numerator must begin with $-b$, giving $x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$.
- $$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2+4ac}}{2a}$
  - _why:_ The discriminant uses subtraction, not addition. The correct expression under the square root is $b^2-4ac$, so the formula is $x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$.
- $$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2}$
  - _why:_ Omitting $a$ from the denominator incorrectly changes both roots unless $a=1$. The whole numerator must be divided by $2a$.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the quadratic formula, the expression $\text{[...]}$ is known as the discriminant.

**Back:** In the quadratic formula, the expression $\boxed{b^2-4ac}$ is known as the discriminant.

**Choices:**

- $$b^2-4ac$ ✔
  - _why:_ The discriminant is the expression beneath the square root in the quadratic formula. Its value determines the number and type of solutions to the quadratic.
- $$b^2+4ac$
  - _why:_ Confusing subtraction with addition changes the value under the square root. The discriminant is $b^2-4ac$, which determines the number and type of roots.
- $$b-4ac$
  - _why:_ The coefficient $b$ must be squared in the discriminant. Omitting this square is a formula-recall error; the correct expression is $b^2-4ac$.
- $$b^2-2ac$
  - _why:_ Using $2ac$ confuses the denominator $2a$ with the discriminant. The expression under the square root is $b^2-4ac$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$ for the equation $5x^2=4x+7$?

**Back:** $a=5$$b=-4$$c=-7$

**Choices:**

- $a=5$$b=-4$$c=-7$ ✔
  - _why:_ Rearranging gives $5x^2-4x-7=0$. Comparing this with $ax^2+bx+c=0$ shows that $a=5$, $b=-4$, and $c=-7$.
- $a=5$$b=4$$c=7$
  - _why:_ These signs are taken before moving the right-hand terms. Rearranging gives $5x^2-4x-7=0$, so $b=-4$ and $c=-7$.
- $a=-5$$b=4$$c=7$
  - _why:_ Moving $5x^2$ to the right can produce these coefficients, but the equation would then need consistent standard form. Using $5x^2-4x-7=0$ gives $a=5$, $b=-4$, $c=-7$.
- $a=5$$b=-4$$c=7$
  - _why:_ Only changing the sign of the $4x$ term overlooks that both right-hand terms move left. Rearranging gives $5x^2-4x-7=0$, so $c=-7$.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** The symbol $\pm$ in the quadratic formula means [ ___ ], which usually results in two solutions.

**Back:** The symbol $\pm$ in the quadratic formula means plus or minus, which usually results in two solutions.

**Choices:**

- plus or minus ✔
  - _why:_ The symbol instructs you to calculate once using addition and once using subtraction. These two calculations usually produce the two solutions of a quadratic equation.
- plus then multiply
  - _why:_ The second symbol is a minus sign, not an instruction to multiply. The notation means plus or minus, requiring separate calculations for the two possible roots.
- positive or negative
  - _why:_ This confuses the operation with the signs of the resulting answers, which need not be one positive and one negative. The symbol means plus or minus.
- plus and minus
  - _why:_ Treating both operations as simultaneous is misleading. The notation means plus or minus: calculate one solution with addition and another with subtraction.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Why should you write out the substitution step in an exam before using a calculator?

**Back:** To ensure you get method marks even if the final calculation is wrong.

**Choices:**

- To ensure you get method marks even if the final calculation is wrong. ✔
  - _why:_ A correct substitution demonstrates that the formula and coefficients were used properly. Examiners can therefore award method marks even when a later calculator or rounding error occurs.
- To ensure you get accuracy marks even if the formula you selected is wrong.
  - _why:_ Accuracy marks require a correct result or valid stage, not merely written substitution into a wrong formula. Showing correct substitution protects method marks if later calculation goes wrong.
- To ensure you get full marks even if the coefficients you substituted are wrong.
  - _why:_ Writing an incorrect substitution cannot guarantee full marks because wrong coefficients undermine the method. Correctly showing the substitution can secure method marks despite a later calculation error.
- To ensure you get presentation marks even if no final solutions are written down.
  - _why:_ GCSE quadratic questions generally reward mathematical method rather than presentation alone. Writing the substitution can earn method marks, but the solutions should still be calculated and stated.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving a quadratic, at what stage should you round your answers?

**Back:** Only at the final step (do not round the square root halfway through).

**Choices:**

- Only at the final step (do not round the square root halfway through). ✔
  - _why:_ Keeping the square root and intermediate values exact prevents rounding errors from accumulating. Round only the final solutions to the accuracy requested.
- At the square root step (round the square root before continuing).
  - _why:_ Rounding the square root early introduces an approximation that can affect later calculations. Keep full calculator precision and round only at the final step.
- After calculating each term (round every value before combining them).
  - _why:_ Rounding each term causes several small errors to accumulate in the final solutions. Intermediate values should remain exact or unrounded until the final step.
- Before using the quadratic formula (round all the coefficients first).
  - _why:_ Rounding the coefficients changes the original quadratic and may produce inaccurate roots. Use the given coefficients and round only the final answers.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a surd?

**Back:** An exact square root that cannot be simplified to a whole number.

**Choices:**

- An exact square root that cannot be simplified to a whole number. ✔
  - _why:_ A surd represents an irrational root exactly rather than as a rounded decimal, such as $\sqrt{3}$. It cannot be simplified to a whole number.
- An approximate square root that has been rounded to a decimal number.
  - _why:_ A rounded decimal is only an approximation, whereas a surd preserves the exact value. A surd is an exact square root that cannot simplify to a whole number.
- An exact square root that can be simplified to a whole number.
  - _why:_ Roots such as $\sqrt{25}=5$ simplify to whole numbers and are therefore not surds. A surd cannot be simplified to a whole number.
- An exact cube root that can always be written as a whole number.
  - _why:_ This confuses square roots with cube roots and incorrectly requires a whole-number result. A surd is an exact root that cannot simplify to a rational number.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you simplify a surd like $\sqrt{108}$?

**Back:** Find the largest square number that divides into it (e.g., $\sqrt{36 \times 3} = 6\sqrt{3}$).

**Choices:**

- Find the largest square number that divides into it (e.g., $\sqrt{36 \times 3} = 6\sqrt{3}$). ✔
  - _why:_ Factoring out the largest square factor simplifies the surd fully. Since $108=36\times3$, $\sqrt{108}=\sqrt{36}\sqrt{3}=6\sqrt{3}$.
- Find the smallest square number that divides into it (e.g., $\sqrt{4 \times 27} = 2\sqrt{27}$).
  - _why:_ Using a smaller square factor may leave another square factor inside the root, so the result is not fully simplified. The largest square factor of $108$ is $36$.
- Find the largest prime number that divides into it (e.g., $\sqrt{3 \times 36} = 3\sqrt{36}$).
  - _why:_ Only square factors can be taken out of a square root directly; a factor of $3$ does not become $3$. Using $36$ gives $\sqrt{108}=6\sqrt{3}$.
- Find the nearest square number to its value (e.g., $\sqrt{100 + 8} = 10\sqrt{8}$).
  - _why:_ Square roots do not distribute over addition, so $\sqrt{100+8}$ is not $10\sqrt{8}$. Factorise using the largest square divisor: $108=36\times3$.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you give an exact answer for a quadratic equation?

**Back:** Leave the answer in surd form rather than converting to a decimal.

**Choices:**

- Leave the answer in surd form rather than converting to a decimal. ✔
  - _why:_ Surd form preserves the exact value of an irrational root. Converting it to a terminating calculator decimal would round the value and make it approximate.
- Give the answer in decimal form rounded to three significant figures.
  - _why:_ A rounded decimal is an approximation, even when several significant figures are included. An exact quadratic solution containing an irrational root must remain in surd form.
- Leave the answer in fraction form after removing every square root.
  - _why:_ An irrational solution cannot be represented exactly as an ordinary fraction, so removing every square root is impossible. The exact answer should be left in surd form.
- Give the answer in integer form by rounding to the nearest whole number.
  - _why:_ Rounding an irrational solution to an integer loses accuracy and does not give an exact value. Exact quadratic solutions should remain in surd form.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving for a length in a geometry problem, why might you reject one of the quadratic roots?

**Back:** Lengths cannot be negative; any root that results in a negative side length is invalid in context.

**Choices:**

- Lengths cannot be negative; any root that results in a negative side length is invalid in context. ✔
  - _why:_ A quadratic may produce two algebraic roots, but a physical length must be non-negative. Any root giving a negative side length is rejected in the geometry context.
- Lengths must be whole numbers; any root that results in a decimal side length is invalid in context.
  - _why:_ Lengths may be decimals, fractions or surds, so a non-integer root can still be valid. Only a root producing a negative length must be rejected.
- Lengths cannot be irrational; any root that results in a surd side length is invalid in context.
  - _why:_ Geometric lengths can be irrational, as diagonals often demonstrate, so a surd length may be exact and valid. A root is rejected if it gives a negative length.
- Lengths must use both roots; any solution that rejects one side length is invalid in context.
  - _why:_ Although a quadratic can have two roots, both need not satisfy the physical conditions of the problem. A root producing a negative length must be rejected.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** How is Pythagoras' Theorem used to form a quadratic?

**Back:** Substitute side expressions into $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and expand/rearrange into $ax^2+bx+c=0$.

**Choices:**

- Substitute side expressions into $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and expand/rearrange into $ax^2+bx+c=0$. ✔
  - _why:_ Pythagoras’ Theorem relates the squared side lengths of a right-angled triangle. Substituting algebraic expressions, expanding and rearranging can therefore produce a quadratic equation.
- Substitute side expressions into $a+b=c$ and expand/rearrange into $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
  - _why:_ This omits the squares required by Pythagoras’ Theorem. For a right-angled triangle, substitute the side expressions into $a^2+b^2=c^2$, then expand and rearrange.
- Substitute side expressions into $a^2-b^2=c^2$ and expand/rearrange into $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
  - _why:_ This uses subtraction instead of the Pythagorean sum. The correct relationship is $a^2+b^2=c^2$, with $c$ representing the hypotenuse.
- Substitute side expressions into $2a+2b=c$ and expand/rearrange into $ax^2+bx+c=0$.
  - _why:_ This resembles part of a perimeter formula rather than Pythagoras’ Theorem. Use $a^2+b^2=c^2$ for a right-angled triangle, then expand and rearrange.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find significant figures, you must start counting from the [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find significant figures, you must start counting from the first non-zero digit.

**Choices:**

- first non-zero digit ✔
  - _why:_ Leading zeros only locate the decimal point and are not significant. Counting begins with the first digit that is not zero, wherever it appears.
- first digit after the decimal point
  - _why:_ Decimal places begin immediately after the decimal point, but significant figures may begin before or several places after it. Start at the first non-zero digit.
- first zero digit
  - _why:_ Leading zeros are placeholders and do not count as significant figures. Counting starts from the first non-zero digit, after any leading zeros.
- last non-zero digit
  - _why:_ Starting at the last non-zero digit would omit earlier significant digits and reverse the counting process. Significant figures are counted from the first non-zero digit.

## Card 16 — `basic`

**Front:** If $x=\frac{-10\pm6\sqrt{3}}{2}$, what is the simplified exact form?

**Back:** $$x=-5\pm3\sqrt{3}$

**Choices:**

- $$x=-5\pm3\sqrt{3}$ ✔
  - _why:_ Both terms in the numerator are divided by $2$: $-10\div2=-5$ and $6\sqrt{3}\div2=3\sqrt{3}$, giving the two exact solutions.
- $$x=-5\pm6\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This divides $-10$ by $2$ but forgets to divide the surd term. Dividing both numerator terms gives $x=-5\pm3\sqrt{3}$.
- $$x=-10\pm3\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This divides the surd term by $2$ but leaves the first term unchanged. The denominator divides both terms, giving $x=-5\pm3\sqrt{3}$.
- $$x=5\pm3\sqrt{3}$
  - _why:_ This loses the negative sign when dividing $-10$ by $2$. Since $-10\div2=-5$, the simplified exact form is $x=-5\pm3\sqrt{3}$.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** In a shape problem, if a rectangle has area 40, width $(x-2)$, and height $(2x+3)$, what is the initial equation?

**Back:** $$(x-2)(2x+3)=40$

**Choices:**

- $$(x-2)(2x+3)=40$ ✔
  - _why:_ A rectangle’s area equals width multiplied by height. Substituting the given expressions and area therefore gives $(x-2)(2x+3)=40$.
- $$(x-2)+(2x+3)=40$
  - _why:_ This adds the side lengths rather than multiplying them, confusing an area calculation with part of a perimeter calculation. Area is width times height, so $(x-2)(2x+3)=40$.
- $$2(x-2)+2(2x+3)=40$
  - _why:_ This forms the rectangle’s perimeter by doubling and adding its side lengths. The given value is the area, so the required equation is $(x-2)(2x+3)=40$.
- $$(x+2)(2x-3)=40$
  - _why:_ This reverses both signs in the given dimensions. The width and height must be copied exactly before multiplying, giving $(x-2)(2x+3)=40$.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the difference between rounding to 3 decimal places (DP) and 3 significant figures (SF)?

**Back:** 3 DP: counts 3 digits after the decimal point.3 SF: counts 3 digits starting from the first non-zero number.

**Choices:**

- 3 DP: counts 3 digits after the decimal point.3 SF: counts 3 digits starting from the first non-zero number. ✔
  - _why:_ Decimal places are counted from the decimal point, whereas significant figures begin at the first non-zero digit. Zeros after that first significant digit may also count.
- 3 DP: counts 3 digits starting from the first non-zero number.3 SF: counts 3 digits after the decimal point.
  - _why:_ This swaps the two definitions. Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, while significant figures count from the first non-zero digit.
- 3 DP: counts 3 digits before the decimal point.3 SF: counts 3 digits starting from the first non-zero number.
  - _why:_ The significant-figures definition is right, but decimal places are not digits before the decimal point. They are counted to the right of the decimal point.
- 3 DP: counts 3 non-zero digits after the decimal point.3 SF: counts 3 digits starting from the decimal point.
  - _why:_ Decimal places include zero digits and count positions after the decimal point. Significant figures instead begin at the first non-zero digit, wherever it occurs.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** In Pythagoras problems, the side used as $c$ in $a^2+b^2=c^2$ must always be the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In Pythagoras problems, the side used as $c$ in $a^2+b^2=c^2$ must always be the hypotenuse.

**Choices:**

- hypotenuse ✔
  - _why:_ The hypotenuse is the longest side of a right-angled triangle and lies opposite the right angle. It is always represented by $c$ in $a^2+b^2=c^2$.
- shortest side
  - _why:_ The shortest side is one of the two perpendicular sides, not necessarily $c$. In Pythagoras’ theorem, $c$ must be the hypotenuse opposite the right angle.
- adjacent side
  - _why:_ The adjacent side depends on which acute angle is being considered and is terminology commonly used in trigonometry. In Pythagoras’ theorem, $c$ is always the hypotenuse.
- opposite side
  - _why:_ The opposite side depends on the chosen acute angle, so it is not automatically $c$. The side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse and must be used as $c$.
