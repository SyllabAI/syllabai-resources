# Place Value Flashcards

_Lane: `gcse-maths-edexcel` · grade 1 · 22 cards (source: MathsGenie flashcards)_

## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** A number is made from [ ___ ]; a digit's [ ___ ] gives its place value.

**Back:** A number is made from digits; a digit's position gives its place value.

**Choices:**

- A number is made from digits; a digit's position gives its place value. ✔
  - _why:_ Numbers are written using digits, and a digit’s position determines whether it represents units, tens, hundreds or another place value.
- A number is made from digits; a digit's size gives its place value.
  - _why:_ The printed size of a digit does not affect its value. Numbers are made from digits, and each digit’s position gives its place value.
- A number is made from columns; a digit's position gives its place value.
  - _why:_ Columns organise place values, but the number itself is made from digits. A digit’s position within those columns determines its place value.
- A number is made from place values; a digit's shape gives its place value.
  - _why:_ A digit’s shape identifies which digit it is, not its place value. A number is made from digits, whose positions determine their place values.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** In 642, what is the value of the 6?

**Back:** 600

**Choices:**

- 600 ✔
  - _why:_ The 6 is in the hundreds column, so it represents six hundreds. Therefore, its value in 642 is 600.
- 60
  - _why:_ This treats the 6 as if it were in the tens column. It is in the hundreds column, so its value is 600.
- 6
  - _why:_ This gives the digit itself rather than its place value. The 6 occupies the hundreds column, making its value 600.
- 6000
  - _why:_ This places the 6 one column too far left, in the thousands column. In 642 it is in the hundreds column, so it is worth 600.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth [ ___ ], but in 500 it is worth [ ___ ].

**Back:** The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth 50, but in 500 it is worth 500.

**Choices:**

- The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth 50, but in 500 it is worth 500. ✔
  - _why:_ In 50, the 5 represents five tens, while in 500 it represents five hundreds. Moving one column left multiplies its value by 10.
- The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth 5, but in 500 it is worth 50.
  - _why:_ This uses values one column too small by ignoring each zero’s effect. The 5 is worth 50 in 50 and 500 in 500.
- The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth 500, but in 500 it is worth 50.
  - _why:_ This reverses the place-value relationship. Moving the 5 one column left from tens to hundreds increases its value from 50 to 500.
- The same digit changes value by column: in 50 the 5 is worth 50, but in 500 it is worth 5000.
  - _why:_ This moves the 5 two columns left instead of one, making its value ten times too large. In 500, the 5 represents 500.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** The decimal point separates the [ ___ ] from the part [ ___ ].

**Back:** The decimal point separates the whole-number part from the part smaller than 1.

**Choices:**

- The decimal point separates the whole-number part from the part smaller than 1. ✔
  - _why:_ Digits to the left of the decimal point form the whole-number part, while digits to the right represent tenths, hundredths and other values smaller than 1.
- The decimal point separates the fractional part from the part greater than 1.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two sides of the decimal point. The whole-number part is on the left, while the part representing values smaller than 1 is on the right.
- The decimal point separates the whole-number part from the part greater than 1.
  - _why:_ Digits after the decimal point represent fractional place values, not a part greater than 1. The point separates the whole-number part from the part smaller than 1.
- The decimal point separates the decimal part from the part equal to 1.
  - _why:_ The decimal point does not mark a part equal to 1, and the decimal part lies to its right. It separates the whole-number part from the part smaller than 1.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** After the decimal point, the first column is [ ___ ] and the second is [ ___ ].

**Back:** After the decimal point, the first column is tenths and the second is hundredths.

**Choices:**

- After the decimal point, the first column is tenths and the second is hundredths. ✔
  - _why:_ Moving right from the decimal point divides place value by 10 each time. The first column is tenths, followed by hundredths in the second column.
- After the decimal point, the first column is hundredths and the second is tenths.
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the first two decimal columns. Tenths come immediately after the decimal point, with hundredths in the next column.
- After the decimal point, the first column is ones and the second is tenths.
  - _why:_ The ones column is immediately before the decimal point, not after it. The first two columns after the point are tenths and hundredths.
- After the decimal point, the first column is tenths and the second is thousandths.
  - _why:_ This skips the hundredths column when moving right. The first decimal column is tenths, the second is hundredths, and thousandths is the third.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Back:** One million is written as 1,000,000, a 1 followed by six zeros.

**Choices:**

- One million is written as 1,000,000, a 1 followed by six zeros. ✔
  - _why:_ Each group of three zeros represents another factor of one thousand. One million is one thousand thousands, so it is written as 1 followed by six zeros.
- One million is written as 100,000, a 1 followed by five zeros.
  - _why:_ A 1 followed by five zeros is one hundred thousand, not one million. One million requires six zeros and is written as 1,000,000.
- One million is written as 10,000,000, a 1 followed by seven zeros.
  - _why:_ A 1 followed by seven zeros is ten million, so this is ten times too large. One million is 1 followed by six zeros: 1,000,000.
- One million is written as 1,000,000, a 1 followed by five zeros.
  - _why:_ The figure shown contains six zeros, not five. One million is correctly written as 1,000,000 because it consists of a 1 followed by six zeros.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** When ordering whole numbers, which digit do you compare first?

**Back:** The leftmost digit in the largest place-value column.

**Choices:**

- The leftmost digit in the largest place-value column. ✔
  - _why:_ The largest place-value column has the greatest effect on a whole number’s size. Compare the leftmost digits first, then move right only if they are equal.
- The rightmost digit in the units place-value column.
  - _why:_ Starting with the units digit can give the wrong order because higher place values have more influence. Whole numbers must be compared from the leftmost place-value column.
- The leftmost digit in the smallest place-value column.
  - _why:_ The leftmost digit occupies the largest available place-value column, not the smallest. Comparison begins there because that column determines the numbers’ order before lower columns matter.
- The rightmost digit in the largest place-value column.
  - _why:_ The largest place-value column is found at the left, so describing its digit as rightmost confuses the direction of the columns. Compare the leftmost digits first.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** Adding zeros at the end of a decimal [ ___ ]; for example, 0.4 = [ ___ ].

**Back:** Adding zeros at the end of a decimal does not change its value; for example, 0.4 = 0.400.

**Choices:**

- Adding zeros at the end of a decimal does not change its value; for example, 0.4 = 0.400. ✔
  - _why:_ Trailing zeros after the final decimal digit do not alter any digit’s place value. Both decimals represent four tenths, so 0.4 and 0.400 are equal.
- Adding zeros at the end of a decimal multiplies its value by 10; for example, 0.4 = 4.00.
  - _why:_ Moving the decimal point right multiplies by 10, but merely appending trailing zeros does not. Therefore, 0.4 remains equal to 0.400, not 4.00.
- Adding zeros at the end of a decimal does not change its value; for example, 0.4 = 0.040.
  - _why:_ Placing a zero before the 4 moves it from the tenths column to the hundredths column, making the value ten times smaller. Appending zeros gives 0.400.
- Adding zeros at the end of a decimal divides its value by 10; for example, 0.4 = 0.04.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10 moves every digit one place to the right; appending trailing zeros does not move any digits. Thus, 0.4 equals 0.400, not 0.04.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** Before ordering decimals with different lengths, what should you do?

**Back:** Add trailing zeros so they have the same number of decimal places.

**Choices:**

- Add trailing zeros so they have the same number of decimal places. ✔
  - _why:_ Trailing zeros do not change a decimal’s value. Giving every number the same number of decimal places makes corresponding place-value digits easier to compare.
- Add leading zeros so they have the same number of digits.
  - _why:_ Leading zeros do not align the digits after the decimal point. The correct method is to add trailing zeros so all numbers have the same number of decimal places.
- Remove trailing zeros so they have the fewest possible decimal places.
  - _why:_ Removing zeros does not make decimals of different lengths easier to compare. Add trailing zeros instead, aligning digits with the same place value.
- Round each decimal so they have the same number of decimal places.
  - _why:_ Rounding can change a decimal’s value and therefore its position in the order. Add trailing zeros instead, because these preserve the original values.

## Card 20 — `cloze`

**Front:** In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the [ ___ ] columns matter more than the [ ___ ] columns.

**Back:** In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the tens columns matter more than the ones columns.

**Choices:**

- In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the tens columns matter more than the ones columns. ✔
  - _why:_ Each digit in a tens column contributes ten times as much as a digit in a ones column, so choosing the tens digits has the greater effect.
- In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the ones columns matter more than the tens columns.
  - _why:_ This reverses the place-value relationship. A digit in a tens column contributes ten times its value, so the tens columns matter more than the ones columns.
- In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the hundreds columns matter more than the ones columns.
  - _why:_ Two-digit numbers do not have hundreds columns. Their relevant columns are tens and ones, and the tens columns have the greater effect on the sum.
- In a two-digit plus two-digit card sum, the tens columns matter more than the tenths columns.
  - _why:_ Tenths columns belong to decimal numbers, not ordinary two-digit whole numbers. The two columns here are tens and ones, with tens having the greater effect.

**Back:** 44

**Choices:**

- 44 ✔
  - _why:_ Place 1 and 2 in the tens columns and 5 and 9 in the ones columns. Either 15 + 29 or 19 + 25 equals 44.
- 34
  - _why:_ This results from forgetting the carried ten when adding 5 and 9. The ones total is 14, so carrying 1 gives 15 + 29 = 44.

  - _why:_ This can result from placing 1 and 5 in the tens columns, as in 12 + 59. Using the smaller tens digits 1 and 2 gives 44.
- 143
  - _why:_ This places the largest digits, 5 and 9, in the tens columns, producing the maximum sum instead. Using 1 and 2 as tens digits gives 44.
