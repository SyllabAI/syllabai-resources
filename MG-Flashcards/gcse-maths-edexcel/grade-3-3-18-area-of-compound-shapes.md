## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What units are used to measure area?

**Back:** Square units (e.g. cm² or m²).

**Choices:**

- Square units (e.g. cm² or m²). ✔
  - _why:_ Area measures the amount of two-dimensional surface covered, so its units are squared. For example, centimetres produce cm² and metres produce m².
- Cubic units (e.g. cm³ or m³).
  - _why:_ Cubic units measure three-dimensional volume, not area. Area is two-dimensional, so it is measured in square units such as cm² or m².
- Linear units (e.g. cm or m).
  - _why:_ Linear units measure one-dimensional lengths such as sides or perimeters. Area combines two dimensions, so it requires square units such as cm² or m².
- Angle units (e.g. degrees or radians).
  - _why:_ Degrees and radians measure angles rather than surface coverage. Area is measured in square units because it represents a two-dimensional region.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** The formula for the area of a rectangle is $A = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The formula for the area of a rectangle is $A = \boxed{l \times w}$.

**Choices:**

- $$l \times w$ ✔
  - _why:_ A rectangle’s area is found by multiplying its length by its width, giving the number of square units covering its surface.
- $$l + w$
  - _why:_ Adding length and width does not calculate area and also gives linear units. The correct formula multiplies the dimensions: $A = l \times w$.
- $$2(l + w)$
  - _why:_ This is the formula for a rectangle’s perimeter, which measures the distance around it. Its area is calculated using $A = l \times w$.
- $$\frac{1}{2} \times l \times w$
  - _why:_ Halving the product is appropriate for a triangle, not a rectangle. A rectangle uses the full product of its dimensions: $A = l \times w$.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** If a diagram states it is not accurately drawn, how should you determine side lengths?

**Back:** Use the written numbers only; do not use a ruler.

**Choices:**

- Use the written numbers only; do not use a ruler. ✔
  - _why:_ A diagram that is not accurately drawn is not reliably to scale. The labelled measurements provide the intended side lengths, so only those values should be used.
- Use a ruler measurement only; ignore the written numbers.
  - _why:_ A ruler gives unreliable results when the diagram is not drawn accurately or to scale. The correct method is to use only the written measurements.
- Use the visual proportions only; estimate each side length.
  - _why:_ Visual proportions can be misleading when a diagram is not accurately drawn. Side lengths must instead be determined from the written measurements and any valid calculations.
- Use the longest side only; scale the other sides from it.
  - _why:_ Scaling from a pictured side assumes the diagram is accurately drawn, which the warning explicitly denies. Use the written numbers and calculate any missing lengths from them.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** What property defines perpendicular height in a triangle?

**Back:** It meets the base at a right angle (90°).

**Choices:**

- It meets the base at a right angle (90°). ✔
  - _why:_ Perpendicular lines meet at 90°, so a triangle’s perpendicular height must form a right angle with the chosen base.
- It meets the base at a straight angle (180°).
  - _why:_ A 180° angle forms a straight line rather than a perpendicular height. Perpendicular height meets the chosen base at a right angle of 90°.
- It meets the base at an acute angle (less than 90°).
  - _why:_ An acute angle is smaller than 90°, so a line meeting the base this way is slanted. Perpendicular height must meet the base at exactly 90°.
- It runs alongside the base in a parallel direction.
  - _why:_ Parallel lines do not meet, so a parallel line cannot be the perpendicular height. The height must meet the chosen base at a 90° angle.

## Card 5 — `cloze`

**Front:** The formula for the area of a triangle is $A = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** The formula for the area of a triangle is $A = \boxed{\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h}$.

**Choices:**

- $$\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$ ✔
  - _why:_ A triangle occupies half the area of a rectangle or parallelogram with the same base and perpendicular height, giving $A = \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$.
- $$b \times h$
  - _why:_ Multiplying the base and height without halving gives the area of the corresponding rectangle or parallelogram. A triangle’s area is $\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$.
- $$\frac{1}{2} \times (b + h)$
  - _why:_ Adding the base and height does not produce an area because the dimensions are not multiplied. The correct formula is $\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$.
- $$2 \times b \times h$
  - _why:_ Doubling the product makes the result four times the triangle’s actual area. The base-height product must instead be halved using $\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the sloping side of a triangle usually excluded from area calculations?

**Back:** It is not the perpendicular height.

**Choices:**

- It is not the perpendicular height. ✔
  - _why:_ The triangle area formula requires the height measured at 90° to the base. A sloping side usually does not provide this perpendicular distance.
- It is always longer than the base.
  - _why:_ A sloping side can be longer or shorter than the base, so relative length is not the reason. It is excluded because it is not the perpendicular height.
- It forms a right angle with the base.
  - _why:_ If the side formed a right angle with the chosen base, it could be used as the perpendicular height. A typical sloping side is excluded because it does not.
- It has no measurable side length.
  - _why:_ A sloping side can have a known and measurable length, often used for perimeter. It is excluded from the area formula because it is not perpendicular to the base.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is a compound shape?

**Back:** A shape made from two or more simpler shapes (e.g. rectangles, triangles).

**Choices:**

- A shape made from two or more simpler shapes (e.g. rectangles, triangles). ✔
  - _why:_ A compound shape combines multiple familiar shapes. Splitting it into rectangles, triangles or other simple parts allows their areas to be calculated separately.
- A shape made from one enlarged simpler shape (e.g. a large rectangle).
  - _why:_ Enlarging one simple shape does not make it compound. A compound shape consists of two or more simpler shapes joined or arranged together.
- A shape made from two identical simpler shapes (e.g. matching rectangles).
  - _why:_ The simpler parts do not need to be identical; they may be different shapes and sizes. A compound shape simply contains two or more simpler shapes.
- A shape made with one curved boundary (e.g. a circle or semicircle).
  - _why:_ Having a curved boundary does not by itself make a shape compound. A compound shape is formed from two or more simpler shapes.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What are the three general steps for finding the area of a joined compound shape?

**Back:** Split the shape into simple parts.Find the area of each part.Add the areas together.

**Choices:**

- Split the shape into simple parts.Find the area of each part.Add the areas together. ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the compound shape creates familiar regions whose areas can be calculated separately. Because the parts are joined without overlapping, their areas are then added.
- Split the shape into simple parts.Find the perimeter of each part.Add the perimeters together.
  - _why:_ Perimeter measures boundary length, not the surface covered, and adding internal boundaries would also overcount. Find each part’s area and add those areas instead.
- Split the shape into simple parts.Find the area of each part.Subtract the areas from one another.
  - _why:_ Subtraction is used for a cut-out or missing region, not for simple parts joined together. For a joined compound shape, calculate each area and add them.
- Enclose the shape in a rectangle.Find the area of the rectangle.Add the missing areas.
  - _why:_ A surrounding rectangle includes regions outside the compound shape, so adding those missing areas increases the error. Either subtract them or split the shape and add its parts.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find a missing horizontal length in a rectilinear shape?

**Back:** Compare the total top distance to the total bottom distance.

**Choices:**

- Compare the total top distance to the total bottom distance. ✔
  - _why:_ Opposite horizontal journeys across a closed rectilinear shape cover the same overall width. Equating the top and bottom totals allows the unknown horizontal segment to be found.
- Add the total top distance to the total bottom distance.
  - _why:_ Adding the two totals combines distances that should be equal rather than helping isolate the unknown. The missing length is found by comparing the total top and bottom distances.
- Compare the total left distance to the total right distance.
  - _why:_ Vertical measurements cannot directly determine an unknown horizontal length. Compare the total horizontal distance along the top with the total horizontal distance along the bottom.
- Multiply the total top distance by the total bottom distance.
  - _why:_ Multiplying lengths calculates an area, not a missing one-dimensional length. Equate the total top and bottom horizontal distances, then use subtraction to find the unknown segment.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** In a rectilinear shape, how do vertical distances relate to one another?

**Back:** The total distance up must match the total distance down.

**Choices:**

- The total distance up must match the total distance down. ✔
  - _why:_ Following the boundary of a closed rectilinear shape must return to the starting height. Therefore, all upward vertical distances together equal all downward vertical distances together.
- The total distance up must be added to the total distance down.
  - _why:_ Adding upward and downward distances gives the total vertical travel, not the relationship needed for missing lengths. The total distance up must equal the total distance down.
- Each individual distance up must match an individual distance down.
  - _why:_ A closed shape can have several unequal individual vertical sides, so corresponding sides need not match. It is their totals that matter: total distance up equals total distance down.
- The total distance up must be twice the total distance down.
  - _why:_ The direction chosen for tracing the boundary does not make one vertical total twice the other. Returning to the starting height requires equal total distances up and down.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the common mistake when calculating the area of L-shaped rectilinear shapes?

**Back:** Multiplying all the numbers shown instead of selecting specific lengths for sub-rectangles.

**Choices:**

- Multiplying all the numbers shown instead of selecting specific lengths for sub-rectangles. ✔
  - _why:_ Area is calculated using the dimensions of chosen rectangles, not every label in the diagram. Multiplying all displayed values usually combines unrelated lengths and gives an incorrect result.
- Splitting the shape into rectangles and adding the separately calculated areas.
  - _why:_ Dividing an L-shape into non-overlapping rectangles and adding their areas is a valid method, not the common error. The mistake is multiplying every number shown indiscriminately.
- Completing the large rectangle and subtracting the area of the missing section.
  - _why:_ Treating the L-shape as a large rectangle and subtracting the missing rectangle is a correct alternative method. The common mistake is multiplying all labelled numbers together.
- Finding the missing lengths before calculating the areas of the sub-rectangles.
  - _why:_ Finding missing side lengths before calculating the sub-rectangles is necessary when dimensions are not directly labelled. The error is multiplying every displayed measurement rather than selecting relevant pairs.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the shaded area if a shape has a hole inside it?

**Back:** Find the large area and subtract the area of the hole.

**Choices:**

- Find the large area and subtract the area of the hole. ✔
  - _why:_ The large area includes both the shaded region and the hole. Subtracting the hole’s area therefore leaves exactly the area that remains shaded.
- Find the large area and add the area of the hole.
  - _why:_ Adding the hole counts empty space on top of the complete outer area, making the result too large. Find the large area and subtract the hole’s area.
- Find the hole area and subtract the area of the large shape.
  - _why:_ Reversing the subtraction usually produces a negative value because the hole is smaller than the outer shape. The hole’s area must be subtracted from the large area.
- Find the large area and multiply it by the area of the hole.
  - _why:_ Multiplying two areas does not represent the remaining region and produces square-units squared. Find the large area, then subtract the area occupied by the hole.

## Card 13 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find a shaded region with a section removed, use the formula: $A_{\text{shaded}} = \text{[...]}$.

**Back:** To find a shaded region with a section removed, use the formula: $A_{\text{shaded}} = \boxed{A_{\text{large}} - A_{\text{removed}}}$.

**Choices:**

- $$A_{\text{large}} - A_{\text{removed}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The removed section is included within the large area, so subtracting its area leaves only the shaded portion. Both areas must be measured in the same square units.
- $$A_{\text{large}} + A_{\text{removed}}$
  - _why:_ Adding the removed area counts the missing section twice: once within the large area and once again separately. The shaded area is the large area minus the removed area.
- $$A_{\text{removed}} - A_{\text{large}}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the subtraction, typically producing a negative area because the removed region is smaller. Subtract the removed area from the large area instead.
- $$A_{\text{large}} \div A_{\text{removed}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the two areas gives a ratio with no area units, not the size of the shaded region. The required calculation is large area minus removed area.

## Card 14 — `cloze`

**Front:** A rectilinear shape is made from horizontal and vertical sides, meaning all corners are [ ___ ].

**Back:** A rectilinear shape is made from horizontal and vertical sides, meaning all corners are right angles (90°).

**Choices:**

- right angles (90°) ✔
  - _why:_ Horizontal and vertical sides meet perpendicularly, creating quarter-turn corners. A quarter turn is a right angle measuring 90°, which characterises rectilinear shapes at ordinary corners.
- acute angles (45°)
  - _why:_ An acute 45° corner requires at least one sloping side, contradicting the use of horizontal and vertical sides. Such sides meet at right angles of 90°.
- obtuse angles (120°)
  - _why:_ An obtuse 120° corner cannot be formed where a horizontal side meets a vertical side. Those directions are perpendicular, so the corner is a right angle of 90°.
- straight angles (180°)
  - _why:_ A straight angle would mean the boundary continues in the same line rather than forming a corner. Horizontal and vertical sides turn through a right angle of 90°.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** A right-angled trapezium can be split into which two simpler shapes?

**Back:** A rectangle and a right-angled triangle.

**Choices:**

- A rectangle and a right-angled triangle. ✔
  - _why:_ Drawing a perpendicular from an endpoint of the shorter parallel side separates a right-angled trapezium into a rectangle and a right-angled triangle.
- A square and a semicircle.
  - _why:_ The four right angles required for a square are not generally present in a trapezium, and the remaining section is not a semicircle. The standard split gives a rectangle and right-angled triangle.
- A rectangle and another rectangle.
  - _why:_ A right-angled trapezium has one sloping side, so dividing it naturally creates a triangle rather than a second rectangle. The correct components are a rectangle and right-angled triangle.
- An equilateral triangle and an isosceles triangle.
  - _why:_ Splitting into two triangles is associated with drawing a diagonal, but it misses the useful standard decomposition based on the parallel sides: a rectangle and a right-angled triangle.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** A trapezium is a 4-sided shape with [ ___ ] pair of parallel sides.

**Back:** A trapezium is a 4-sided shape with one pair of parallel sides.

**Choices:**

- one ✔
  - _why:_ Under the GCSE definition used here, a trapezium is a quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides that are parallel. Its other pair is not parallel.
- two
  - _why:_ Choosing two pairs confuses a trapezium with a parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus or square. The GCSE definition here requires one pair of parallel sides.
- no
  - _why:_ A quadrilateral with no parallel sides does not meet the defining property of a trapezium. A trapezium has one pair of opposite sides that are parallel.
- three
  - _why:_ A four-sided polygon has only two pairs of opposite sides, so it cannot have three pairs of parallel sides. A trapezium has one parallel pair.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** A trapezium is a 4-sided shape with one pair of [ ___ ] sides.

**Back:** A trapezium is a 4-sided shape with one pair of parallel sides.

**Choices:**

- parallel ✔
  - _why:_ In GCSE geometry, a trapezium is defined as a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides, which remain the same distance apart and never meet.
- equal
  - _why:_ Equal side lengths are not required for a trapezium. Its defining feature is one pair of parallel sides, which remain the same distance apart.
- perpendicular
  - _why:_ Perpendicular sides meet at a right angle, but trapeziums do not have to contain right angles. The required pair of sides is parallel.
- adjacent
  - _why:_ Adjacent sides share a vertex, which occurs in every quadrilateral and does not define a trapezium. A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** When solving an area problem involving cost, when should you multiply by the price?

**Back:** Only after calculating the total area.

**Choices:**

- Only after calculating the total area. ✔
  - _why:_ The complete area must be found first because the price is charged for each unit of area. Multiplying that total by the unit price gives the total cost.
- Before calculating the total area.
  - _why:_ Multiplying too early means the required number of square units is not yet known. Calculate the total area first, then multiply it by the price per square unit.
- Only after calculating the total perimeter.
  - _why:_ Perimeter measures the boundary length, whereas an area-based price applies to the surface covered. Calculate the total area first and then multiply by the price.
- Before converting all lengths to consistent units.
  - _why:_ Using inconsistent length units produces an invalid area and therefore an incorrect cost. Convert the measurements, calculate the total area, and only then apply the price.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** What does the term per square metre mean in a money question?

**Back:** The cost for each 1 m² of area.

**Choices:**

- The cost for each 1 m² of area. ✔
  - _why:_ A price stated per square metre is a unit rate: every square metre of surface costs that amount. Multiply this rate by the area in m².
- The cost for each 1 m of length.
  - _why:_ A metre measures linear length rather than area. “Per square metre” means the cost for each 1 m² of area, not each metre of length.
- The cost for the whole area in total.
  - _why:_ The word “per” gives a rate for one square metre, not a price for the entire region. The total cost depends on how many square metres there are.
- The cost for each 1 m³ of volume.
  - _why:_ Cubic metres measure volume, so they would apply to three-dimensional capacity. “Per square metre” instead gives the cost for each 1 m² of area.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is the area of a triangle half of a rectangle with the same base and height?

**Back:** A triangle is effectively half of a rectangle created by those dimensions.

**Choices:**

- A triangle is effectively half of a rectangle created by those dimensions. ✔
  - _why:_ A diagonal divides a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height into two congruent triangles. Each triangle therefore occupies exactly half the rectangle’s area.
- A triangle is effectively one quarter of a rectangle created by those dimensions.
  - _why:_ Dividing the rectangle along a diagonal produces two equal triangles, not four. A triangle with the same base and height is therefore half, rather than one quarter, of the rectangle.
- A triangle is effectively the same area as a rectangle created by those dimensions.
  - _why:_ This overlooks the factor of one half in the triangle area formula. A diagonal splits the corresponding rectangle into two equal triangles, so each has half its area.
- A triangle is effectively one third of a rectangle created by those dimensions.
  - _why:_ The rectangle is divided into two equal triangular halves, not three equal parts. Consequently, the triangle’s area is half of base times height, rather than one third.
