## Card 1 — `cloze`

**Front:** Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase or decrease by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase or decrease by the same multiplier.

**Choices:**

- same multiplier ✔
  - _why:_ In direct proportion, both quantities are scaled by the same multiplier. For example, if one quantity doubles, the other quantity must also double.
- same amount
  - _why:_ Adding or subtracting the same amount describes a constant difference, not direct proportion. Directly proportional quantities increase or decrease by the same multiplier.
- same difference
  - _why:_ A constant difference does not guarantee direct proportion because the ratio may change. Direct proportion requires both quantities to be changed by the same multiplier.
- reciprocal multiplier
  - _why:_ Using reciprocal multipliers would make one quantity increase while the other decreases. In direct proportion, both quantities are changed by the same multiplier.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** If a recipe is doubled from 10 cakes to 20 cakes, what happens to every ingredient?

**Back:** Every ingredient doubles.

**Choices:**

- Every ingredient doubles. ✔
  - _why:_ The number of cakes is multiplied by 2, so direct proportion requires every ingredient amount to be multiplied by 2 as well.
- Every ingredient halves.
  - _why:_ Halving confuses the direction of the scale change. The number of cakes rises from 10 to 20, so every ingredient doubles.
- Every ingredient stays unchanged.
  - _why:_ Keeping the ingredients unchanged would make the same mixture produce twice as many cakes. To preserve the recipe, every ingredient must double.
- Every ingredient increases by 10.
  - _why:_ The increase of 10 applies to the number of cakes, not each ingredient amount. Each ingredient must be multiplied by 2, so it doubles.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** To scale a recipe from an old number made to a new number made, what multiplier do you use?

**Back:** $\frac{\text{new number made}}{\text{old number made}}$

**Choices:**

- $\frac{\text{new number made}}{\text{old number made}}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The scale multiplier compares the required new output with the original output. Multiplying each old ingredient amount by this ratio gives the new amount.
- $\frac{\text{old number made}}{\text{new number made}}$
  - _why:_ This reverses the required ratio and scales in the opposite direction. The correct multiplier is new number made divided by old number made.
- $\frac{\text{new number made}}{\text{new number made}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the new number by itself always gives 1, so no scaling would occur. The correct multiplier compares the new number with the old number.
- $\frac{\text{old number made}}{\text{old number made}}$
  - _why:_ Dividing the old number by itself gives 1 and leaves every ingredient unchanged. Use new number made divided by old number made instead.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by [ ___ ] divided by [ ___ ].

**Back:** For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by new number made divided by old number made.

**Choices:**

- For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by new number made divided by old number made. ✔
  - _why:_ The required scale factor is the new number made divided by the old number made. Multiplying the old ingredient amount by this factor gives the new amount.
- For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by old number made divided by new number made.
  - _why:_ The ratio has been reversed, so it would scale the recipe in the opposite direction. Use new number made divided by old number made.
- For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by old number made divided by old number made.
  - _why:_ This ratio equals 1, so the ingredient amount would remain unchanged. The numerator must be the new number made, with the old number made as denominator.
- For recipe proportion, multiply the old amount by new number made divided by new number made.
  - _why:_ Dividing the new number by itself gives a multiplier of 1. The denominator should be the old number made so the ratio measures the required scale change.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** What multiplier changes 12 cakes to 6 cakes?

**Back:** $\frac{1}{2}$

**Choices:**

- $\frac{1}{2}$ ✔
  - _why:_ The multiplier is $\frac{6}{12}=\frac{1}{2}$. Multiplying 12 by $\frac{1}{2}$ gives the required 6 cakes.
- $2$
  - _why:_ This reverses the scale factor and would change 12 cakes to 24. The correct multiplier is $\frac{6}{12}=\frac{1}{2}$.
- $\frac{1}{3}$
  - _why:_ Dividing by 3 would reduce 12 cakes to 4, not 6. The required multiplier is $\frac{6}{12}=\frac{1}{2}$.
- $\frac{3}{2}$
  - _why:_ This multiplier increases 12 cakes to 18 instead of reducing the quantity. The correct scale factor is $\frac{6}{12}=\frac{1}{2}$.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** What multiplier changes 30 biscuits to 45 biscuits?

**Back:** $\frac{3}{2}$ or 1.5.

**Choices:**

- $\frac{3}{2}$ or 1.5. ✔
  - _why:_ The multiplier is $\frac{45}{30}=\frac{3}{2}=1.5$. Applying it to 30 gives $30\times1.5=45$ biscuits.
- $\frac{2}{3}$ or 0.67.
  - _why:_ This is the reciprocal ratio $\frac{30}{45}$, so it scales in the wrong direction. The required multiplier is $\frac{45}{30}=\frac{3}{2}=1.5$.
- $\frac{1}{2}$ or 0.5.
  - _why:_ Using only the increase of 15 as a fraction of 30 leads to this incorrect multiplier. The total scale factor is $\frac{45}{30}=1.5$.
- $\frac{5}{2}$ or 2.5.
  - _why:_ This incorrectly adds 1 to the required multiplier and would produce 75 biscuits. The correct multiplier is $\frac{45}{30}=\frac{3}{2}=1.5$.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by [ ___ ], then multiply by [ ___ ].

**Back:** Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 2, then multiply by 3.

**Choices:**

- Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 2, then multiply by 3. ✔
  - _why:_ For a fractional multiplier, divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator. Therefore $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 2, then multiply by 3.
- Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 3, then multiply by 2.
  - _why:_ This swaps the numerator and denominator, giving multiplication by $\frac{2}{3}$ instead. For $\frac{3}{2}$, divide by 2 and multiply by 3.
- Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 2, then multiply by 2.
  - _why:_ Dividing and multiplying by 2 cancel, producing no overall change. To multiply by $\frac{3}{2}$, divide by 2 and then multiply by 3.
- Multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ means divide by 3, then multiply by 3.
  - _why:_ Dividing and multiplying by 3 gives an overall multiplier of 1. The denominator is 2, so divide by 2 before multiplying by 3.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use [ ___ ] divided by [ ___ ].

**Back:** When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use new amount divided by old amount.

**Choices:**

- When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use new amount divided by old amount. ✔
  - _why:_ A scale multiplier compares the required new amount with the original amount, so it is calculated using new amount divided by old amount.
- When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use old amount divided by new amount.
  - _why:_ This reverses the division and produces the reciprocal multiplier. The correct scale multiplier is new amount divided by old amount.
- When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use new amount divided by difference.
  - _why:_ Dividing by the difference does not compare the new quantity with the original quantity. The correct multiplier is new amount divided by old amount.
- When finding a multiplier from an ingredient amount, use old amount divided by difference.
  - _why:_ This uses the change between the amounts rather than the original amount as the denominator. A proportional multiplier is new amount divided by old amount.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** A recipe makes 12 shortcakes with 15 ml milk. If 40 ml milk is used, how many shortcakes are made?

**Back:** 32 shortcakes

**Choices:**

- 32 shortcakes ✔
  - _why:_ The milk multiplier is $40 \div 15 = \frac{8}{3}$. Multiplying $12$ shortcakes by $\frac{8}{3}$ gives $32$ shortcakes.
- 27 shortcakes
  - _why:_ This effectively adds the $15$ ml increase to the original number of shortcakes. Direct proportion requires $12 \times (40 \div 15) = 32$ shortcakes.
- 30 shortcakes
  - _why:_ This uses an inaccurate scale factor of $2.5$. The correct milk multiplier is $40 \div 15 = \frac{8}{3}$, producing $32$ shortcakes.
- 36 shortcakes
  - _why:_ This assumes the milk amount has tripled, but $40$ ml is only $\frac{8}{3}$ times $15$ ml. The recipe therefore makes $32$ shortcakes.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** A recipe needs 225 g flour for 9 cakes. How much flour is needed per cake?

**Back:** 25 g per cake.

**Choices:**

- 25 g per cake. ✔
  - _why:_ Dividing the total flour equally among $9$ cakes gives $225 \div 9 = 25$, so each cake needs $25$ g.
- 20 g per cake.
  - _why:_ This comes from an incorrect division of $225$ by $9$. Since $9 \times 25 = 225$, the amount required is $25$ g per cake.
- 24 g per cake.
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one division error, as $9 \times 24 = 216$, not $225$. Dividing correctly gives $25$ g per cake.
- 27 g per cake.
  - _why:_ This confuses the number of cakes with a nearby factor of $225$. Sharing $225$ g equally between $9$ cakes gives $25$ g per cake.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** A student has 500 g flour and needs 25 g per cake. What is the maximum number of cakes?

**Back:** 20 cakes

**Choices:**

- 20 cakes ✔
  - _why:_ The number of complete cakes is the available flour divided by the flour per cake: $500 \div 25 = 20$ cakes.
- 15 cakes
  - _why:_ This results from an incorrect division of $500$ by $25$. Since $25 \times 20 = 500$, there is enough flour for $20$ cakes.
- 19 cakes
  - _why:_ This unnecessarily rounds the exact quotient down by one. The calculation $500 \div 25$ equals exactly $20$, so $20$ complete cakes can be made.
- 25 cakes
  - _why:_ This confuses the flour needed per cake with the number of cakes. Dividing $500$ g by $25$ g per cake gives $20$ cakes.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** The limiting ingredient is the ingredient that allows the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The limiting ingredient is the ingredient that allows the fewest complete items.

**Choices:**

- fewest complete items ✔
  - _why:_ The ingredient producing the fewest complete items runs out first. It therefore limits the total number of complete items that can be made.
- most complete items
  - _why:_ Choosing the most complete items ignores the ingredient that runs out first. The limiting ingredient is the one allowing the fewest complete items.
- fewest leftover items
  - _why:_ The calculation compares complete items that each ingredient can produce, not leftovers. The limiting ingredient allows the fewest complete items.
- most leftover items
  - _why:_ A large amount left over does not determine the production limit. The limiting ingredient is whichever one allows the fewest complete items.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** For several available ingredients, which result decides the greatest number of complete items?

**Back:** The smallest number any ingredient allows.

**Choices:**

- The smallest number any ingredient allows. ✔
  - _why:_ Every complete item needs all the required ingredients, so production stops when the first ingredient runs out. This corresponds to the smallest number allowed.
- The largest number any ingredient allows.
  - _why:_ The largest value assumes the most plentiful ingredient sets the limit, but another ingredient would run out earlier. The smallest number allowed is decisive.
- The total number all ingredients allow.
  - _why:_ Adding the separate results wrongly treats ingredients as substitutes. Because every item needs each ingredient, the smallest number any ingredient allows sets the maximum.
- The average number the ingredients allow.
  - _why:_ Averaging can give a quantity that some ingredients cannot support. The greatest feasible number of complete items is the smallest number allowed by any ingredient.
