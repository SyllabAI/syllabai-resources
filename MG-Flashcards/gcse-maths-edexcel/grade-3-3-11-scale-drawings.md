## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the scale factor in a scale drawing?

**Back:** The multiplier used to convert a drawing/model length to the real-life length.

**Choices:**

- The multiplier used to convert a drawing/model length to the real-life length. ✔
  - _why:_ A scale factor gives the multiplicative relationship between corresponding lengths. Multiplying a drawing or model length by it produces the associated real-life length.
- The amount added to convert a drawing/model length to the real-life length.
  - _why:_ Scale drawings preserve proportions, so lengths are multiplied rather than increased by a fixed amount. The scale factor is the multiplier converting drawing lengths to real-life lengths.
- The difference between a drawing/model length and the real-life length.
  - _why:_ The difference between two lengths changes depending on which length is measured, so it cannot define the scale. The scale factor is a constant multiplier.
- The ratio of a drawing/model’s width to its height.
  - _why:_ Comparing width with height describes an object’s proportions, not the relationship between the drawing and reality. The scale factor multiplies a drawing length to obtain its real-life length.

## Card 2 — `cloze`

**Front:** A scale drawing is a drawing where all lengths have been enlarged or reduced by the [ ___ ].

**Back:** A scale drawing is a drawing where all lengths have been enlarged or reduced by the same multiplier.

**Choices:**

- same multiplier ✔
  - _why:_ Every length must be multiplied by one common value so that the drawing keeps the same proportions as the real object.
- same fixed amount
  - _why:_ Adding or subtracting a fixed amount would change shorter lengths proportionally more than longer ones. A scale drawing changes every length using the same multiplier.
- different multiplier
  - _why:_ Using a different multiplier for each length would distort the object’s proportions. All corresponding lengths must be enlarged or reduced by the same multiplier.
- measured perimeter
  - _why:_ A perimeter is a total boundary length, not a rule for resizing individual dimensions. A scale drawing enlarges or reduces every length by the same multiplier.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** When is it appropriate to measure or compare lengths directly on a drawing?

**Back:** Only when the drawing is explicitly stated to be accurate or a scale is provided.

**Choices:**

- Only when the drawing is explicitly stated to be accurate or a scale is provided. ✔
  - _why:_ An accurate drawing or a stated scale guarantees that measured lengths and their ratios represent the real objects consistently, allowing valid comparisons.
- Only when the drawing appears to be realistic or a photograph is provided.
  - _why:_ A realistic appearance does not guarantee mathematically correct proportions, and photographs can contain perspective distortion. Direct measurement requires an accurate drawing or a stated scale.
- Only when the drawing is explicitly stated to be not to scale or no scale is provided.
  - _why:_ The phrase “not to scale” specifically warns that direct measurements are unreliable. Measurements can be compared only when accuracy is stated or a scale is given.
- Only when the drawing is sufficiently large or a ruler is provided.
  - _why:_ Drawing size and access to a ruler do not ensure that proportions are correct. Direct comparisons are justified only by stated accuracy or a provided scale.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** If Object A is four times the height of Object B on an accurate drawing, how do their real heights compare?

**Back:** The real height of Object A is four times the real height of Object B.

**Choices:**

- The real height of Object A is four times the real height of Object B. ✔
  - _why:_ An accurate drawing preserves the ratio of corresponding lengths. Therefore, a height ratio of four to one on the drawing also applies in real life.
- The real height of Object A is one quarter the real height of Object B.
  - _why:_ This reverses the height ratio. Since A is four times as tall as B on an accurate drawing, its real height is also four times B’s height.
- The real height of Object A is four units more than the real height of Object B.
  - _why:_ “Four times” describes multiplication, not a fixed difference of four units. The accurate drawing shows that A’s real height is four times B’s real height.
- The real height of Object A is the same as the real height of Object B.
  - _why:_ Equal real heights would appear equal on an accurate drawing. Since A appears four times taller, A’s real height must be four times B’s.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** A drawing shows a plant pot (real height 25 cm) and a cupboard. The cupboard is 4 times taller on the drawing. What is the estimated real height of the cupboard?

**Back:** 100 cm ($25 \times 4$)

**Choices:**

- 100 cm ($25 \times 4$) ✔
  - _why:_ The accurate drawing shows a height ratio of four to one. Multiplying the plant pot’s real height by four gives $25 \times 4 = 100$ cm.
- 6.25 cm ($25 \div 4$)
  - _why:_ Dividing reverses the stated comparison and would make the cupboard shorter than the plant pot. Since it is four times taller, calculate $25 \times 4 = 100$ cm.
- 75 cm ($25 \times 3$)
  - _why:_ This uses a scale factor of three instead of the given factor of four. The correct calculation is $25 \times 4$, giving a real height of 100 cm.
- 50 cm ($25 \times 2$)
  - _why:_ This mistakenly doubles the plant pot’s height rather than multiplying it by four. The cupboard’s estimated real height is $25 \times 4 = 100$ cm.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** If a vehicle's height is about $\frac{1}{3}$ of its length on a drawing, how do you find the real length from the real height?

**Back:** Multiply the real height by 3.

**Choices:**

- Multiply the real height by 3. ✔
  - _why:_ If height is one third of length, the full length contains three of those height-sized parts. Therefore, the real height must be multiplied by 3.
- Divide the real height by 3.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 3 would make the length smaller than the height, contradicting the drawing. Since height is one third of length, multiply the height by 3.
- Multiply the real height by 2.
  - _why:_ Doubling treats the height as one half of the length rather than one third. Three height-sized parts form the length, so multiply by 3.
- Add 3 to the real height.
  - _why:_ The relationship is proportional, so a multiplicative calculation is needed rather than adding a fixed number. Multiply the real height by 3 to obtain the length.

## Card 7 — `cloze`

**Front:** If the height of a drawing is about one quarter of the length, then the length is about [ ___ ] times the height.

**Back:** If the height of a drawing is about one quarter of the length, then the length is about 4 times the height.

**Choices:**

- 4 ✔
  - _why:_ If the height is one quarter of the length, four equal height-sized parts make the full length. The length is therefore 4 times the height.
- 0.25
  - _why:_ This repeats the fraction describing height relative to length instead of finding the inverse relationship. The length is 4 times the height.
- 3
  - _why:_ This treats one quarter as though only three more equal parts are needed, overlooking the original part. Four height-sized parts make the full length.
- 5
  - _why:_ This includes one extra height-sized part. A whole divided into quarters contains exactly four quarters, so the length is 4 times the height.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a written scale of $1:50$ mean?

**Back:** 1 cm on the drawing represents 50 cm in real life.

**Choices:**

- 1 cm on the drawing represents 50 cm in real life. ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio $1:50$ compares drawing length with real-life length in the same units. Each 1 cm measured on the drawing corresponds to 50 cm in reality.
- 50 cm on the drawing represents 1 cm in real life.
  - _why:_ This reverses the order of the scale ratio. In $1:50$, the first number refers to the drawing, so 1 cm represents 50 cm in reality.
- 1 cm on the drawing represents 50 m in real life.
  - _why:_ This changes centimetres to metres without conversion, making the real distance 100 times too large. Using matching units, 1 cm represents 50 cm.
- 1 cm on the drawing represents 5 cm in real life.
  - _why:_ This drops the zero and interprets the scale as $1:5$. A scale of $1:50$ means 1 cm represents 50 cm in real life.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the real length from a model with a scale of $1:n$, you must [ ___ ] the model length by n.

**Back:** To find the real length from a model with a scale of $1:n$, you must multiply the model length by n.

**Choices:**

- multiply ✔
  - _why:_ In a $1:n$ scale, one unit on the model represents $n$ of the same units in reality, so multiplying by $n$ gives the real length.
- divide
  - _why:_ Dividing reverses the required scale conversion and would make the real object smaller than the model. For a $1:n$ scale, multiply the model length by $n$.
- add
  - _why:_ Adding $n$ treats the scale factor as a fixed increase rather than a multiplier. The real length is found by multiplying the model length by $n$.
- subtract
  - _why:_ Subtracting $n$ does not apply the scale factor and may even produce a negative length. A $1:n$ scale requires multiplication by $n$.

## Card 10 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the real length from a model with a scale of $1:n$, you must multiply the model length by [ ___ ].

**Back:** To find the real length from a model with a scale of $1:n$, you must multiply the model length by n.

**Choices:**

- n ✔
  - _why:_ The ratio $1:n$ means every one unit on the model represents $n$ of the same units in reality, so the model length is multiplied by $n$.
- 1/n
  - _why:_ Multiplying by $1/n$ is equivalent to dividing by $n$, which converts in the opposite direction. To find the real length, multiply by $n$.
- n²
  - _why:_ Squaring the scale factor applies an area scale rather than a length scale. For corresponding lengths on a $1:n$ model, the multiplier is simply $n$.
- 100n
  - _why:_ The factor 100 is only relevant to particular unit conversions, such as metres to centimetres. The scale itself requires the model length to be multiplied by $n$.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** A model bus is 12 cm long with a scale of $1:60$. What is the real length of the bus in metres?

**Back:** 7.2 m ($12 \times 60 = 720$ cm; $720 \div 100 = 7.2$ m)

**Choices:**

- 7.2 m ($12 \times 60 = 720$ cm; $720 \div 100 = 7.2$ m) ✔
  - _why:_ Multiplying 12 cm by the scale factor 60 gives 720 cm. Dividing by 100 then converts centimetres to metres, giving 7.2 m.
- 72 m ($12 \times 60 = 720$ cm; $720 \div 10 = 72$ m)
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10 instead of 100 causes the centimetre-to-metre conversion to be ten times too large. Since 720 cm equals 7.2 m, the real length is 7.2 m.
- 0.72 m ($12 \times 60 = 720$ cm; $720 \div 1,000 = 0.72$ m)
  - _why:_ Dividing by 1,000 instead of 100 makes the converted length ten times too small. The correct conversion is $720 \div 100 = 7.2$ m.
- 0.12 m ($12 \div 100 = 0.12$ m)
  - _why:_ This converts the 12 cm model length into metres but fails to apply the scale factor of 60. Scaling first gives 720 cm, which is 7.2 m.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** Why is it incorrect to say a $1:60$ scale means 1 cm represents 60 m?

**Back:** A ratio scale must use the same units on both sides (e.g., 1 cm : 60 cm).

**Choices:**

- A ratio scale must use the same units on both sides (e.g., 1 cm : 60 cm). ✔
  - _why:_ A scale ratio compares like units, so $1:60$ means one unit represents 60 of that same unit. Thus 1 cm represents 60 cm, not 60 m.
- A ratio scale must use different units on each side (e.g., 1 cm : 60 m).
  - _why:_ A numerical ratio does not automatically compare centimetres with metres. Both sides must use the same units, so $1:60$ can mean 1 cm : 60 cm.
- A ratio scale means the real measurement comes first (e.g., 1 m : 60 cm).
  - _why:_ Reversing the direction of the scale does not address the unit error. A $1:60$ scale uses matching units, such as 1 cm representing 60 cm.
- A ratio scale gives an area factor, so lengths must be multiplied by $\sqrt{60}$.
  - _why:_ The square root is used when deriving a length factor from an area factor, not when reading a length scale. Here, 1 cm represents 60 cm.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you convert a measurement from centimetres to metres?

**Back:** Divide by 100.

**Choices:**

- Divide by 100. ✔
  - _why:_ There are 100 centimetres in one metre, so converting a centimetre measurement to metres requires division by 100.
- Multiply by 100.
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 100 converts metres to centimetres, which is the opposite direction. To convert centimetres to metres, divide by 100.
- Divide by 10.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10 incorrectly treats a metre as containing only 10 centimetres. Since 1 m equals 100 cm, divide by 100.
- Divide by 1,000.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 1,000 confuses the metre conversion with a different metric factor and makes the answer ten times too small. Centimetres are converted to metres by dividing by 100.

**Front:** How do you convert a measurement from centimetres to kilometres?

**Back:** Divide by 100,000.

**Choices:**

- Divide by 100,000. ✔
  - _why:_ One kilometre contains 1,000 metres, and each metre contains 100 centimetres. Therefore, 1 km equals 100,000 cm, so divide by 100,000.
- Divide by 1,000.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 1,000 converts metres to kilometres, not centimetres to kilometres. Since one kilometre contains 100,000 cm, divide by 100,000.
- Divide by 10,000.
  - _why:_ Dividing by 10,000 misses a factor of 10 and makes the kilometre value ten times too large. The correct conversion factor is 100,000.
- Multiply by 100,000.
  - _why:_ Multiplication converts from a larger unit to a smaller numerical unit, not from centimetres to kilometres. To obtain kilometres, divide by 100,000.

  - _why:_ Dividing by 1,000,000 makes the conversion ten times too small. There are 100,000 cm in a kilometre, so 350,000 cm is 3.5 km.

  - _why:_ This labels a distance measured in centimetres as kilometres without converting it. The 350,000 cm must be divided by 100,000, giving 3.5 km.

## Card 17 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate the perimeter of a rectangular field from a scale drawing?

**Back:** Add the real lengths of all four sides (length + width + length + width).

**Choices:**

- Add the real lengths of all four sides (length + width + length + width). ✔
  - _why:_ A rectangle’s perimeter is the total distance around its boundary, so both lengths and both widths must be converted to real measurements and added.
- Add the real lengths of two adjacent sides (length + width).
  - _why:_ Adding one length and one width gives only half the perimeter. A rectangle has two of each, so add length + width + length + width.
- Multiply the real lengths of two adjacent sides (length × width).
  - _why:_ Multiplying length by width calculates the field’s area, not its perimeter. The perimeter is found by adding the real lengths of all four sides.
- Add the drawing lengths of all four sides without using the scale.
  - _why:_ The drawing lengths are not the field’s real lengths unless the scale is applied. Convert each measurement first, then add all four real side lengths.

## Card 18 — `basic`

**Front:** A rectangular field has a real length of 48 m. On a drawing, the width is half the length. What is the estimated real perimeter?

**Back:** 144 m (Width = 24 m; $48 + 24 + 48 + 24 = 144$ m)

**Choices:**

- 144 m (Width = 24 m; $48 + 24 + 48 + 24 = 144$ m) ✔
  - _why:_ Half of 48 m is 24 m. A rectangle has two lengths and two widths, so its perimeter is $2(48 + 24) = 144$ m.
- 72 m (Width = 24 m; $48 + 24 = 72$ m)
  - _why:_ This adds only one length and one width, giving half the perimeter. Both pairs of opposite sides must be included, producing $144$ m.
- 120 m (Width = 12 m; $48 + 12 + 48 + 12 = 120$ m)
  - _why:_ This finds one quarter of 48 m instead of one half. The width is 24 m, so the correct perimeter is $144$ m.
- 192 m (Width = 48 m; $48 + 48 + 48 + 48 = 192$ m)
  - _why:_ This treats the field as a square by making the width equal to the length. The width is actually 24 m, giving a perimeter of $144$ m.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** Why must you compare heights with heights (rather than heights with widths) on a drawing?

**Back:** Matching lengths must be in the same proportion to ensure the estimate is valid.

**Choices:**

- Matching lengths must be in the same proportion to ensure the estimate is valid. ✔
  - _why:_ Corresponding dimensions must scale by the same factor. Comparing a real height with its drawn height gives a valid proportional relationship for estimating other heights.
- Matching lengths must have the same numerical value to ensure the estimate is valid.
  - _why:_ Scale drawings usually make corresponding lengths numerically different, not equal. Their ratio must remain the same, so matching heights must be in the same proportion.
- Matching lengths must be on the same side of the shape to ensure the estimate is valid.
  - _why:_ Their position on the shape is not the key requirement. The dimensions must correspond, such as height with height, so they remain in the same proportion.
- Matching lengths must be in a different proportion to ensure the estimate is valid.
  - _why:_ Using different proportions would mean applying inconsistent scale factors and would invalidate the estimate. Corresponding heights must be compared using the same proportion.

**Choices:**

  - _why:_ A scale drawing represents real lengths proportionally rather than at full size. An estimate is a sensible approximate answer obtained by measuring or comparing those lengths.
