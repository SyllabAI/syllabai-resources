## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** In the context of sequences, what is a term?

**Back:** One single number or pattern in the sequence.

**Choices:**

- One single number or pattern in the sequence. ✔
  - _why:_ A term is one individual entry in a sequence. Depending on the sequence, that entry may be a number, a shape, or another pattern.
- The position occupied by a number in the sequence.
  - _why:_ This describes a term number or position, not a term. A term is one single number or pattern appearing at that position in the sequence.
- The difference between consecutive numbers in the sequence.
  - _why:_ This confuses a term with the gap between terms. A term is one individual number or pattern, whereas the difference measures the change between two terms.
- The complete list of numbers generated by the sequence.
  - _why:_ This describes the sequence as a whole rather than one term. A term is one single number or pattern within the complete sequence.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** What does a term-to-term rule describe?

**Back:** How to get from one term to the next.

**Choices:**

- How to get from one term to the next. ✔
  - _why:_ A term-to-term rule gives an operation that turns each term into the following term, such as adding 4 or multiplying by 2.
- How to get from a position number to any term.
  - _why:_ This describes an nth-term rule, which uses a position to calculate a term directly. A term-to-term rule explains how to move from one term to the next.
- How to get from the first term to the last.
  - _why:_ A term-to-term rule is applied at every step, not merely between the endpoints. It describes how to obtain each next term from the preceding one.
- How to get from any term directly to its position.
  - _why:_ This reverses the purpose of the rule. A term-to-term rule uses one term to generate the next, rather than finding a term's position.

## Card 3 — `cloze`

**Front:** To continue a sequence by looking at the change between numbers, you should work out the [ ___ ] between terms.

**Back:** To continue a sequence by looking at the change between numbers, you should work out the gaps between terms.

**Choices:**

- gaps ✔
  - _why:_ The gaps show how much the sequence changes from one term to the next. Checking them can reveal a constant, changing, or repeating pattern.
- positions
  - _why:_ Positions label where terms appear, but they do not describe the numerical change between them. The gaps between consecutive terms must be calculated to continue the sequence.
- products
  - _why:_ Multiplying pairs of terms does not usually reveal the change from one term to the next. The required values are the gaps found between consecutive terms.
- averages
  - _why:_ Averages give central values for pairs or groups, not the changes between consecutive terms. To identify how the sequence develops, calculate the gaps between terms.

## Card 4 — `basic`

**Front:** Why must you check all provided gaps in a sequence rather than just the first one?

**Back:** Gaps may change (increase or decrease) rather than staying the same.

**Choices:**

- Gaps may change (increase or decrease) rather than staying the same. ✔
  - _why:_ Some sequences have changing first differences, such as quadratic sequences. Checking every provided gap reveals whether the differences are constant, increasing, decreasing, or following another pattern.
- Gaps must always increase by one after the first gap.
  - _why:_ This assumes one particular pattern that sequences do not have to follow. All gaps must be checked because they may stay constant, increase, decrease, or vary differently.
- The first gap gives the nth term, while later gaps give term values.
  - _why:_ A gap is a difference between consecutive terms, not an nth-term formula. Every gap should be checked because the differences may change instead of remaining constant.
- Sequences always use alternating gaps rather than a constant gap.
  - _why:_ Alternating gaps occur in some sequences but are not universal. Checking all gaps determines whether they are constant, alternating, increasing, decreasing, or following another pattern.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you calculate a Fibonacci-type sequence term?

**Back:** Add the two previous terms together.

**Choices:**

- Add the two previous terms together. ✔
  - _why:_ In a Fibonacci-type sequence, each new term is found by adding the two terms immediately before it, so both preceding values are needed.
- Multiply the two previous terms together.
  - _why:_ This replaces the defining addition with multiplication and produces a different sequence. A Fibonacci-type term is calculated by adding the two previous terms together.
- Add the three previous terms together.
  - _why:_ This is a related recurrence pattern but not the standard Fibonacci-type rule. Each new term should be the sum of only the two previous terms.
- Add the previous term and position together.
  - _why:_ This confuses the term number with a sequence value. A Fibonacci-type rule does not use the position; it adds the two preceding terms.

## Card 6 — `cloze`

**Front:** In an nth-term formula, the letter $n$ represents the [ ___ ].

**Back:** In an nth-term formula, the letter $n$ represents the position number.

**Choices:**

- position number ✔
  - _why:_ The variable $n$ identifies a term's position in the sequence. Substituting $n=1$, $n=2$, and so on gives the corresponding term values.
- value of the term
  - _why:_ This confuses the input with the output of the formula. The letter $n$ is the position number, while the formula's calculated result is the term's value.
- common difference
  - _why:_ The common difference is the fixed change between terms in an arithmetic sequence. In an nth-term formula, $n$ instead represents the position number.
- number of terms
  - _why:_ Although $n$ can count items in other contexts, here it identifies the position of the particular term being calculated, not the sequence's total length.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the standard value of $n$ for the first term in a GCSE sequence?

**Back:** $$n = 1$

**Choices:**

- $$n = 1$ ✔
  - _why:_ GCSE sequences are conventionally indexed from 1, so the first term has position $n=1$, the second has $n=2$, and so forth.
- $$n = 0$
  - _why:_ Starting at zero is common in programming and some advanced contexts, but standard GCSE sequences begin at $n=1$, making that the first term's position.
- $$n = 2$
  - _why:_ This is an off-by-one error: $n=2$ identifies the second term. Standard GCSE indexing assigns $n=1$ to the first term.
- $$n = -1$
  - _why:_ Negative indices are not used for the ordinary term positions in GCSE sequences. The first term is assigned the positive position number $n=1$.

## Card 8 — `cloze`

**Front:** To find the value of a specific term using an nth-term formula, you must [ ___ ] the position number into the formula.

**Back:** To find the value of a specific term using an nth-term formula, you must substitute the position number into the formula.

**Choices:**

- substitute ✔
  - _why:_ Substitution means replacing $n$ in the formula with the required position number. Evaluating the resulting expression then gives the value of that specific term.
- multiply
  - _why:_ Multiplying by the position automatically is not valid unless the formula specifically requires it. The position number must replace $n$, which is the process of substitution.
- add
  - _why:_ Adding the position number to the formula changes its meaning and does not necessarily find the required term. The correct process is to substitute the position for $n$.
- divide
  - _why:_ Dividing by the position is an invented operation unless division appears in the formula. To calculate a particular term, substitute its position number for $n$.

## Card 9 — `basic`

**Front:** If the nth term is $n^2 + 5$, what operation must be performed on the position number first?

**Back:** Square the position number ($n^2$).

**Choices:**

- Square the position number ($n^2$). ✔
  - _why:_ The exponent applies before addition, so the position number must first be squared to obtain $n^2$. The value 5 is then added.
- Add 5 to the position number ($n + 5$).
  - _why:_ Adding 5 first would give $n+5$, not $n^2+5$. The position number must first be squared, after which 5 is added.
- Multiply by 5 the position number ($5n$).
  - _why:_ Multiplying by 5 produces $5n$, which confuses an exponent with a coefficient. The required first operation is squaring the position number to obtain $n^2$.
- Double the position number ($2n$).
  - _why:_ Doubling the position number gives $2n$, whereas the exponent 2 means multiplying $n$ by itself. The required first operation is squaring.

## Card 10 — `basic`

**Front:** What defines an arithmetic sequence?

**Back:** A sequence with a common difference (the same gap) between all consecutive terms.

**Choices:**

- A sequence with a common difference (the same gap) between all consecutive terms. ✔
  - _why:_ An arithmetic sequence changes by a fixed amount each time. Subtracting any term from the next therefore always gives the same common difference.
- A sequence with a common ratio (the same multiplier) between all consecutive terms.
  - _why:_ A fixed ratio defines a geometric sequence, not an arithmetic sequence. Arithmetic sequences have the same difference between every pair of consecutive terms.
- A sequence with a common sum (the same total) between all consecutive terms.
  - _why:_ A constant total for consecutive terms does not define an arithmetic sequence. The defining feature is a constant difference found by subtracting one term from the next.
- A sequence with an increasing difference (a larger gap) between all consecutive terms.
  - _why:_ Increasing gaps describe a non-linear pattern rather than an arithmetic sequence. In an arithmetic sequence, the gap itself remains constant between consecutive terms.

## Card 11 — `basic`

**Front:** In the arithmetic nth term formula $dn + c$, what does $d$ represent?

**Back:** The common difference.

**Choices:**

- The common difference. ✔
  - _why:_ In a linear nth-term rule, the coefficient of $n$ controls how much the sequence changes whenever the position increases by one, so it is the common difference.
- The first term.
  - _why:_ The first term is found by substituting $n=1$, giving $d+c$. It is not represented by $d$ alone; $d$ is the common difference.
- The constant adjustment.
  - _why:_ The adjustment is represented by $c$, which shifts all terms by a constant amount. The coefficient $d$ instead represents the sequence’s common difference.
- The position number.
  - _why:_ The position number is represented by the variable $n$, not by its coefficient. The value $d$ is the common difference between consecutive terms.

## Card 12 — `cloze`

**Front:** In the nth term formula $dn + c$, the value of $d$ is determined by the [ ___ ], not the first term.

**Back:** In the nth term formula $dn + c$, the value of $d$ is determined by the common difference, not the first term.

**Choices:**

- common difference ✔
  - _why:_ The coefficient of $n$ determines how much the nth-term value changes when $n$ increases by one, so $d$ equals the common difference.
- first term
  - _why:_ The first term equals $d+c$ after substituting $n=1$, so it does not determine $d$ by itself. The value of $d$ comes from the common difference.
- constant adjustment
  - _why:_ The adjustment is the value $c$, not the coefficient $d$. The coefficient $d$ is determined by the common difference between consecutive sequence terms.
- common ratio
  - _why:_ A common ratio is used for geometric sequences involving repeated multiplication. In the linear rule $dn+c$, $d$ is determined by the common difference.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** How do you find the adjustment $c$ for the nth term $dn + c$?

**Back:** Calculate $d \times 1$Find the difference between this value and the actual first term

**Choices:**

- Calculate $d \times 1$Find the difference between this value and the actual first term ✔
  - _why:_ At the first position, the linear part contributes $d\times1=d$. Comparing this with the actual first term gives the required adjustment, $c=a_1-d$.
- Calculate $d \times 0$Find the difference between this value and the actual first term
  - _why:_ Using position zero introduces an off-by-one indexing error because the first term corresponds to $n=1$. Calculate $d\times1$, then compare it with the actual first term.
- Calculate $d \times 1$Find the difference between this value and the actual second term
  - _why:_ Comparing $d\times1$ with the second term mixes two different positions. The adjustment must be found using the actual first term because the calculation uses $n=1$.
- Calculate $d \times 1$Find the sum of this value and the actual first term
  - _why:_ Adding the values gives the wrong sign and size for the adjustment. Since the first term satisfies $a_1=d+c$, the correct calculation is $c=a_1-d$.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** What is the common difference $d$ for a sequence that decreases by 4 each time?

**Back:** $$-4$

**Choices:**

- $$-4$ ✔
  - _why:_ The sequence changes by subtracting 4 each time, so the signed difference between consecutive terms is negative. Therefore the common difference is $-4$.
- $$4$
  - _why:_ Using $4$ ignores the direction of change and would produce a sequence that increases by 4. A decrease of 4 gives the common difference $-4$.
- $$-8$
  - _why:_ The size of the decrease is 4, not 8; doubling it is an arithmetic slip. Because the sequence decreases, the correct signed difference is $-4$.
- $$-\frac{1}{4}$
  - _why:_ Taking the reciprocal confuses a difference with a multiplicative scale factor. Subtracting consecutive terms gives a change of negative 4, so $d=-4$.

## Card 15 — `cloze`

**Front:** If a sequence is 26, 22, 18..., the common difference is [ ___ ].

**Back:** If a sequence is 26, 22, 18..., the common difference is -4.

**Choices:**

- -4 ✔
  - _why:_ Subtracting consecutive terms gives $22-26=-4$ and $18-22=-4$. As the same signed change occurs each time, the common difference is -4.
- 4
  - _why:_ The magnitude of each gap is 4, but the positive sign would indicate an increasing sequence. Since each term is 4 smaller, the difference is -4.
- -8
  - _why:_ The gap is found between consecutive terms, not by combining two successive decreases. Both $22-26$ and $18-22$ equal -4, not -8.
- 22
  - _why:_ This is the second term rather than the change between terms. Subtracting one term from the next gives $22-26=-4$, the common difference.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** A number is only part of a sequence if its position $n$ is a [ ___ ].

**Back:** A number is only part of a sequence if its position $n$ is a positive whole number.

**Choices:**

- positive whole number ✔
  - _why:_ Sequence positions are counted using $1, 2, 3, \ldots$, so $n$ must be a positive whole number rather than zero, a negative number or a fraction.
- non-negative whole number
  - _why:_ This wrongly includes zero. Standard sequence positions begin at $n=1$, so $n$ must be a positive whole number: $1, 2, 3, \ldots$.

  - _why:_ This wrongly includes zero and negative integers, which are not standard term positions. The position $n$ must be a positive whole number.
- positive real number
  - _why:_ Real numbers include fractions and decimals, but a term cannot occupy position $2.5$. Sequence positions require positive whole numbers such as $1, 2, 3, \ldots$.

**Choices:**

- The position number ($n$). ✔
  - _why:_ The pattern number identifies which stage of the pattern is being considered, so it corresponds to the position number $n$ used in the nth-term expression.

- 14
  - _why:_ This doubles the latest term rather than adding the two previous terms. The Fibonacci-type rule gives $4+7=11$, and then $7+11=18$.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** How can you quickly check if an nth-term expression you calculated is correct?

**Back:** Substitute $n=1$ and $n=2$ to see if they produce the first two terms.

**Choices:**

- Substitute $n=1$ and $n=2$ to see if they produce the first two terms. ✔
  - _why:_ The first and second sequence terms occupy positions $n=1$ and $n=2$. If substituting these values produces the stated terms, the expression passes a quick initial check.
- Substitute $n=0$ and $n=1$ to see if they produce the first two terms.
  - _why:_ This uses zero-based indexing, but GCSE sequences normally begin at $n=1$. Substitute $n=1$ and $n=2$ to check whether the first two terms are produced.
- Substitute $n=2$ and $n=3$ to see if they produce the first two terms.
  - _why:_ This shifts both positions forward by one, so it tests the second and third terms. The first two terms correspond to $n=1$ and $n=2$.
- Substitute $n=1$ and $n=2$ to see if they produce the first two differences.
  - _why:_ An nth-term expression outputs sequence terms, not the differences between them. Substituting $n=1$ and $n=2$ should produce the first and second terms.
