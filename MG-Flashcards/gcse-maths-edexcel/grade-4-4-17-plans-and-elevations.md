# Plans and Elevations Flashcards

_Lane: `gcse-maths-edexcel` · grade 4 · 22 cards (source: MathsGenie flashcards)_

## Card 1 — `basic`

**Front:** If you are asked to draw a plan, where do you look from?

**Back:** From directly above.

**Choices:**

- From directly above. ✔
  - _why:_ A plan is the view obtained by looking vertically down at the solid from directly above, showing its width and depth but not its height.
- From directly below.
  - _why:_ Looking from below would produce an underside view, not a plan. A plan is drawn by looking directly down at the solid from above.
- From directly in front.
  - _why:_ Looking directly in front produces a front elevation, which shows width and height. A plan is obtained by looking directly from above.
- From directly beside.
  - _why:_ Looking directly beside the solid produces a side elevation, showing depth and height. A plan is drawn from the view directly above.

## Card 2 — `basic`

**Front:** If you draw the front elevation, where do you look from?

**Back:** From the front.

**Choices:**

- From the front. ✔
  - _why:_ A front elevation is the two-dimensional view seen when looking straight at the designated front of the solid, showing its width and height.
- From the back.
  - _why:_ Looking from the back gives a rear elevation, which may differ from the front view. The front elevation is obtained by looking from the front.
- From above.
  - _why:_ Looking from above produces the plan, which shows width and depth. The front elevation is drawn by looking straight at the solid from the front.
- From the side.
  - _why:_ Looking from the side produces a side elevation, usually showing depth and height. A front elevation must be viewed from the solid’s front.

## Card 3 — `basic`

**Front:** For a side elevation, how do you choose the side to view?

**Back:** Use the side shown by the arrow or label.

**Choices:**

- Use the side shown by the arrow or label. ✔
  - _why:_ The diagram’s arrow or label identifies the required viewing direction, so the side elevation must be drawn as seen from that specified side.
- Use the side opposite the arrow or label.
  - _why:_ Reversing the indicated direction produces the elevation from the opposite side. The required side is the one shown by the arrow or label.
- Use the side with the longest visible edge.
  - _why:_ The lengths of the edges do not determine the viewing side. The correct side elevation is selected using the arrow or label on the diagram.
- Use the side nearest to the front of the page.
  - _why:_ The position of a side on the printed page does not determine the required view. Follow the arrow or label that specifies the viewing side.

## Card 4 — `cloze`

**Front:** Width is [ ___ ]; depth is [ ___ ].

**Back:** Width is left-to-right; depth is front-to-back.

**Choices:**

- Width is left-to-right; depth is front-to-back. ✔
  - _why:_ Width measures the horizontal distance from left to right, while depth measures how far the solid extends from its front to its back.
- Width is front-to-back; depth is left-to-right.
  - _why:_ This swaps the meanings of width and depth. Width runs left-to-right, while depth runs front-to-back when describing the dimensions of a solid.
- Width is bottom-to-top; depth is front-to-back.
  - _why:_ Bottom-to-top is height, not width. Width runs left-to-right, while the stated front-to-back direction for depth is correct.
- Width is left-to-right; depth is bottom-to-top.
  - _why:_ Bottom-to-top describes height rather than depth. Width runs left-to-right, and depth measures the front-to-back distance of the solid.

## Card 5 — `basic`

**Front:** A length is measured bottom-to-top on a solid. Which measurement is this?

**Back:** Height.

**Choices:**

- Height. ✔
  - _why:_ Height is the vertical measurement of a solid, taken from its bottom to its top. It is visible in front and side elevations.
- Width.
  - _why:_ Width measures from left to right rather than bottom to top. A bottom-to-top measurement is the solid’s height.
- Depth.
  - _why:_ Depth measures the distance from front to back, not the vertical distance. The bottom-to-top dimension of a solid is its height.
- Length.
  - _why:_ Length is a general term and usually refers to a horizontal extent in this context. The named bottom-to-top dimension is height.

## Card 6 — `basic`

**Front:** Which dimension is not shown in a plan?

**Back:** Height.

**Choices:**

- Height. ✔
  - _why:_ A plan is viewed from above, so it shows the solid’s width and depth. Its vertical height cannot be seen in this view.
- Width.
  - _why:_ A plan shows the left-to-right width because it is viewed from above. Height is the dimension that is not shown.
- Depth.
  - _why:_ A plan shows the front-to-back depth as well as the width. It does not show the solid’s bottom-to-top height.
- Horizontal length.
  - _why:_ Horizontal lengths form the outline visible in a plan, so they can be shown. The missing dimension is the vertical height.

## Card 7 — `basic`

**Front:** Which dimension is not shown in a front elevation?

**Back:** Depth.

**Choices:**

- Depth. ✔
  - _why:_ A front elevation shows the solid’s left-to-right width and bottom-to-top height. It cannot show the full front-to-back depth.
- Height.
  - _why:_ Height is visible in a front elevation as the bottom-to-top measurement. The dimension not shown is the front-to-back depth.
- Width.
  - _why:_ Width is visible in a front elevation as the left-to-right measurement. The front-to-back depth is the dimension that is not shown.
- Width and height.
  - _why:_ A front elevation specifically displays both width and height. It leaves out the third dimension, which is the solid’s depth.

## Card 8 — `basic`

**Front:** Which full measurement does a side elevation not show?

**Back:** The left-to-right width.

**Choices:**

- The left-to-right width. ✔
  - _why:_ A side elevation shows the solid’s depth and height. Looking from the side means the full left-to-right width extends along the viewing direction and is not shown.
- The front-to-back depth.
  - _why:_ A side elevation shows the front-to-back depth as a horizontal measurement. The dimension it does not show is the solid’s left-to-right width.
- The bottom-to-top height.
  - _why:_ A side elevation shows the bottom-to-top height as its vertical measurement. The hidden full dimension is the left-to-right width.
- The front-to-back width.
  - _why:_ Front-to-back is depth, not width, and this distance is visible in a side elevation. The unshown dimension is the left-to-right width.

## Card 9 — `cloze`

**Front:** The plan and front elevation share the [ ___ ].

**Back:** The plan and front elevation share the width.

**Choices:**

- width ✔
  - _why:_ Both views show the solid’s left-to-right measurement, so the plan and front elevation must have the same width.
- height
  - _why:_ Height is visible in the front elevation but not in the plan. The shared dimension is width because both views show the left-to-right measurement.
- depth
  - _why:_ Depth is shown by the plan but not by the front elevation. These two views instead share the solid’s width.
- length
  - _why:_ Length is sometimes used informally for a horizontal measurement, but the corresponding dimension in the plan and front elevation is specifically called width.

- width

- height
  - _why:_ Height appears in the side elevation but is not represented in the plan. The dimension common to these two views is depth.
- length
  - _why:_ Length may be used informally for a horizontal dimension, but plans and elevations specifically identify the shared front-to-back measurement as depth.

## Card 12 — `basic`

**Front:** If a solid is a prism, what happens to its cross-section through the solid?

**Back:** The same cross-section continues all the way through.

**Choices:**

- The same cross-section continues all the way through. ✔
  - _why:_ A prism has a uniform cross-section along its length, meaning the same cross-sectional shape and size continue from one end to the other.
- The cross-section decreases all the way through.
  - _why:_ A steadily shrinking cross-section describes a tapered solid, such as a pyramid or cone. A prism keeps the same cross-section all the way through.
- The cross-section increases all the way through.
  - _why:_ A steadily enlarging cross-section describes a widening or tapered solid, not a prism. A prism has the same cross-section throughout its length.
- The cross-section rotates all the way through.
  - _why:_ Rotation of the cross-section would produce a twisted solid rather than an ordinary prism. In a prism, the same cross-section continues straight through.

## Card 13 — `basic`

**Front:** For a cube solid, what positions should be marked on the plan?

**Back:** Every floor square with at least one cube.

**Choices:**

- Every floor square with at least one cube. ✔
  - _why:_ A plan is a view from directly above, so each floor position occupied by one or more cubes appears as a square.
- Every floor square with exactly one cube.
  - _why:_ Marking only positions with exactly one cube ignores taller stacks, although they still occupy floor space. Every position containing at least one cube must be shown.
- Every floor square with at least two cubes.
  - _why:_ Requiring at least two cubes wrongly omits single cubes. From above, any occupied floor square is shown, regardless of the stack’s height.
- Every floor square with no cubes present.
  - _why:_ Empty floor positions are not part of the solid and therefore do not appear in its plan. Only squares with at least one cube are marked.

## Card 14 — `basic`

**Front:** A tower is 3 cubes high. How many squares does it show in the plan?

**Back:** One square.

**Choices:**

- One square. ✔
  - _why:_ The plan records occupied floor positions rather than the number of cubes in a stack. One vertical tower therefore appears as one square.
- Three squares.
  - _why:_ Counting three squares confuses the tower’s height with its footprint. All three cubes occupy the same floor position, so the plan shows one square.
- Two squares.
  - _why:_ Two squares would imply two occupied floor positions. A single vertical tower occupies only one position, so its plan contains one square.
- Six squares.
  - _why:_ Six squares may come from counting visible cube faces rather than the footprint. A plan shows occupied floor positions, so this tower gives one square.

## Card 15 — `basic`

**Front:** In a cube-solid side elevation, what height is drawn at each depth position?

**Back:** The maximum stack height at that depth.

**Choices:**

- The maximum stack height at that depth. ✔
  - _why:_ Looking from the side combines stacks that share a depth position, so the visible outline reaches the greatest stack height found there.
- The minimum stack height at that depth.
  - _why:_ Using the minimum height ignores taller stacks that block shorter ones in the side view. The elevation must show the maximum stack height at each depth.
- The total stack height at that depth.
  - _why:_ Adding stack heights counts cubes hidden behind one another rather than forming the visible outline. The side elevation uses only the maximum height at each depth.
- The average stack height at that depth.
  - _why:_ An average height does not represent the solid’s outline and could create a height no stack has. The maximum stack height must be drawn at each depth.

## Card 16 — `cloze`

**Front:** A cylinder has a [ ___ ] plan and [ ___ ] elevations.

**Back:** A cylinder has a circular plan and rectangular elevations.

**Choices:**

- A cylinder has a circular plan and rectangular elevations. ✔
  - _why:_ Viewed from above, a vertical cylinder shows its circular base. From either horizontal direction, its curved surface projects as a rectangle.
- A cylinder has a rectangular plan and circular elevations.
  - _why:_ This reverses the two projections. A vertical cylinder appears circular from above in the plan and rectangular from the front or side.
- A cylinder has a circular plan and circular elevations.
  - _why:_ The plan is circular, but a horizontal view includes the cylinder’s height and diameter, producing a rectangular elevation rather than a circle.
- A cylinder has a rectangular plan and rectangular elevations.
  - _why:_ The elevations are rectangular, but the plan shows the cylinder’s circular base rather than a rectangle. Therefore only the plan should be circular.

## Card 17 — `cloze`

**Front:** A cone has a [ ___ ] plan and [ ___ ] elevations.

**Back:** A cone has a circular plan and triangular elevations.

**Choices:**

- A cone has a circular plan and triangular elevations. ✔
  - _why:_ Viewed from above, the circular base determines the plan. Viewed from the side or front, the apex and base diameter form a triangular outline.
- A cone has a square plan and triangular elevations.
  - _why:_ A square plan would belong to an object with a square base, such as a square-based pyramid. A cone has a circular plan and triangular elevations.
- A cone has a circular plan and rectangular elevations.
  - _why:_ The circular plan is correct, but a rectangular elevation is associated with a cylinder. A cone narrows to an apex, so its elevations are triangular.
- A cone has a triangular plan and circular elevations.
  - _why:_ This reverses the shapes seen from above and from the side. A cone has a circular plan because of its base, while its elevations are triangular.

## Card 18 — `cloze`

**Front:** A square-based pyramid has a [ ___ ] plan and [ ___ ] elevations.

**Back:** A square-based pyramid has a square plan and triangular elevations.

**Choices:**

- A square-based pyramid has a square plan and triangular elevations. ✔
  - _why:_ The view from above shows the square base, so the plan is square. From the front or side, the base and apex produce a triangular elevation.
- A square-based pyramid has a rectangular plan and triangular elevations.
  - _why:_ A rectangle is tempting because every square is a type of rectangle, but plans use the precise visible shape. The plan is square and the elevations are triangular.
- A square-based pyramid has a square plan and rectangular elevations.
  - _why:_ The plan is square, but rectangular elevations would not show the sides meeting at an apex. The sloping faces produce triangular elevations.
- A square-based pyramid has a triangular plan and square elevations.
  - _why:_ This confuses the base shape with the shape of a side view. The square base gives a square plan, while the apex creates triangular elevations.

## Card 19 — `basic`

**Front:** A cone has radius 2 cm. What is the base length in its side elevation?

**Back:** 4 cm.

**Choices:**

- 4 cm. ✔
  - _why:_ The base in the side elevation is the cone’s diameter. Since the diameter is twice the radius, its length is $2 \times 2 = 4$ cm.
- 2 cm.
  - _why:_ This uses the radius as the full base length. The side elevation shows the diameter, so the correct base length is $2 \times 2 = 4$ cm.
- 6.28 cm.
  - _why:_ This calculates the circumference using $2\pi r$, but the side elevation needs the straight-line diameter. Doubling the radius gives the correct base length of 4 cm.
- 8 cm.
  - _why:_ This doubles the diameter after already doubling the radius. The base spans one diameter only, so $2 \times 2$ gives the correct length of 4 cm.

## Card 20 — `basic`

**Front:** Where should the apex go in a cone side elevation?

**Back:** Directly above the midpoint of the base.

**Choices:**

- Directly above the midpoint of the base. ✔
  - _why:_ A right cone is symmetrical about its perpendicular axis. Therefore, its apex must be vertically aligned with the midpoint of the base in the side elevation.
- Directly above the left endpoint of the base.
  - _why:_ Placing the apex over an endpoint creates a skewed triangular outline rather than a symmetrical right cone. It should be directly above the midpoint of the base.
- Directly above the right endpoint of the base.
  - _why:_ Aligning the apex with the right endpoint incorrectly makes one side vertical and destroys the cone’s symmetry. The apex belongs above the midpoint of the base.
- Directly above the quarter-point of the base.
  - _why:_ The quarter-point does not lie on the cone’s central axis, so this placement would produce an asymmetrical elevation. The apex should be above the base midpoint.

## Card 21 — `basic`

**Front:** For a cone or pyramid elevation, which height is normally used?

**Back:** The perpendicular height.

**Choices:**

- The perpendicular height. ✔
  - _why:_ An elevation uses the vertical distance from the base to the apex. This is the perpendicular height, measured at a right angle to the base.
- The slant height.
  - _why:_ The slant height runs along a face from the apex to the base edge, so it is not the vertical height normally used. Use the perpendicular height.
- The vertical edge.
  - _why:_ A cone has no vertical edge, and a pyramid’s edges are generally sloping. The required height is the perpendicular distance from the base to the apex.
- The base length.
  - _why:_ The base length controls the width of the elevation rather than its height. The vertical dimension to the apex is given by the perpendicular height.

## Card 22 — `basic`

**Front:** Using a scale of 2 cm to 1 m, how long should 250 cm be drawn?

**Back:** 5 cm.

**Choices:**

- 5 cm. ✔
  - _why:_ First convert 250 cm to 2.5 m. At 2 cm for each metre, the drawing length is $2.5 \times 2 = 5$ cm.
- 2.5 cm.
  - _why:_ This converts 250 cm to 2.5 m but then treats each metre as only 1 cm. Multiplying by the stated 2 cm gives 5 cm.
- 1.25 cm.
  - _why:_ This divides 2.5 m by the scale value instead of multiplying by 2 cm per metre. The correct drawing length is $2.5 \times 2 = 5$ cm.
- 500 cm.
  - _why:_ This multiplies 250 by 2 without first converting centimetres to metres, mixing real and drawing units. Since 250 cm is 2.5 m, draw 5 cm.
