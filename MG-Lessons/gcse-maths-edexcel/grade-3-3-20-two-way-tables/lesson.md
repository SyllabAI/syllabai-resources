## Reading a two-way table

![Labeled two-way table showing rows, columns, one cell, row total, column total, and grand total](../assets/001-3c59dcc7-3d7c-4c7f-acc9-c96ba4e591c1.jpeg)

A two-way table organises information in two different ways at the same time, such as gender and favourite drink. It is read using rows across and columns down.

A cell is one box where a row and a column meet. In the diagram, $18$ is a cell value because it sits where the Girls row meets the Water column.

A row total adds across one row, a column total adds down one column, and the grand total is the whole table. In the diagram, $32$ is a column total and $60$ is the grand total.

## Filling in missing values

When a two-way table has blanks, the totals still have to match. A blank means the number is missing, not that it is zero.

Use subtraction to work backwards from a row total or column total. If a row total is 46 and two entries are 25 and 9, then the missing entry is $46 - 25 - 9 = 12$.

Try to complete a row or column that has only one gap first. When you finish, check every row across and every column down.

## Worked example: completing a table

![Worked example showing an incomplete travel-to-school two-way table and the completed table with subtraction steps labelled](../assets/002-92a73208-c6ec-4815-a1a4-333d62b11891.jpeg)

Start with the totals because they unlock the rest of the table. Here the girls' total is $100 - 54 = 46$.

Now fill any row or column with just one gap. In the Girls row, Walk is $46 - 25 - 9 = 12$, and in the Other column, Boys Other is $20 - 9 = 11$.

Finish the Boys row next: Bus is $54 - 18 - 11 = 25$. The completed table then has column totals Walk 30, Bus 50, and Other 20, so both rows and columns match.

## Probability from a two-way table

Once the table is complete, probabilities come straight from the counts in it. Use $P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$. This means "number you want" over "total number possible".

If one student is chosen from the whole travel table, the denominator is the grand total 100. For example, the probability of choosing a student who walks is $\frac{30}{100} = \frac{3}{10}$.

If the question restricts the choice to one group, use that group's total instead. For example, if one of the girls is chosen, the probability of walking is $\frac{12}{46} = \frac{6}{23}$, not $\frac{12}{100}$.

## Building tables from words and percentages

Sometimes the table is empty and the information is written in sentences. Put in the grand total and any row or column totals first, then place the direct facts into the correct cells.

Translate each sentence carefully. "18 of the walkers are boys" fills the Boys-Walk cell with 18, while "50 girls get the bus" fills the Girls-Bus cell with 50.

With percentages, turn each percentage into a number first. Out of 200 students, $60\%$ walk gives 120 walkers and $45\%$ boys gives 90 boys. If no total is given at all, pretending the total is 100 lets each percentage become a number straight away.

## Check yourself

1. In the drinks table on the previous card, how many students chose water altogether?

   - 32 ✔

   - 18

   - 30

   - 60

   _Explanation:_ 32 is the total at the bottom of the Water column, so it counts everyone who chose water. The most common wrong answer is 18, but that is only the Girls and Water cell, not the whole column.

2. In the completed travel table, how many boys travel by bus?

   - 25 ✔

   - 20

   - 11

   - 46

   _Explanation:_ 25 is the value where the Boys row and Bus column meet. The most common wrong answer is 20, but that is the total for the Other column, not the Boys Bus cell.

3. From the completed travel table, one of the girls is chosen at random. What is the probability that she walks to school?

   - $$\frac{12}{46}$ ✔

   - $$\frac{12}{100}$

   - $$\frac{30}{100}$

   - $$\frac{25}{46}$

   _Explanation:_ The favourable outcomes are the 12 girls who walk, and the total possible outcomes are the 46 girls because the choice is only from girls. The most common wrong answer is $\frac{12}{100}$, but 100 is the grand total for the whole table, not the restricted group. Hint: Ask yourself, "Who can actually be picked?"

4. In a survey of 200 students, "$18\%$ are boys who get the bus" means:

   - $$18\%$ of all 200 students are boys who get the bus ✔

   - $$18\%$ of the boys get the bus

   - 18 boys get the bus

   - $$18\%$ of the bus users are boys

   _Explanation:_ Unless the question says "of the boys" or "of the bus users", the percentage refers to the whole group. The most common wrong answer is B, but that changes the denominator from everyone to just the boys.
