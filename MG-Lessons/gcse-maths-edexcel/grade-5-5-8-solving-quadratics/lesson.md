## What makes an equation quadratic?

![Graph of the quadratic y=(x+2)(x-3) crossing the x-axis at -2 and 3, with roots labelled](../assets/001-d80e0322-905c-45a9-b23b-20e024079e13.jpeg)

A quadratic equation has a squared term as its highest power, such as $x^2-x-6=0$. The graph above is of $y=(x+2)(x-3)=x^2-x-6$, so its roots are $x=-2$ and $x=3$. In this lesson, we focus on equations of the form $x^2+bx+c=0$, where the coefficient of $x^2$ is 1.

When we solve a quadratic, we are finding the values of the variable that make the equation true. On a graph, these values are the roots, where the curve crosses the x-axis.

A common GCSE method is factorising, which rewrites the quadratic as a product of two brackets. Then the zero product rule lets us solve each bracket.

## Factorising reverses expanding

To factorise $x^2+bx+c$, find two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add to $b$. Those numbers go into two brackets, each starting with $x$.

For $x^2+6x+8$, the numbers are 2 and 4 because $2 \times 4 = 8$ and $2+4=6$. So

$$$x^2+6x+8=(x+2)(x+4)$$

Always check the middle term by a quick expansion. Here the outside and inside terms are $4x$ and $2x$, which combine to make $6x$.

## Choosing the signs in the brackets

If the constant term is positive, the bracket signs are the same. A positive middle term means both signs are positive, and a negative middle term means both signs are negative.

If the constant term is negative, the bracket signs are different. The larger number takes the sign that makes the middle term correct.

For $y^2+y-30$, we need two numbers that multiply to $-30$ and add to $1$, so they are $6$ and $-5$. Therefore:

$$$y^2+y-30=(y+6)(y-5)$$ A quick check gives $-5y+6y=y$, so the middle term is right.

## Worked example: factorise, then solve

Solve $n^2+n-42=0$. We need two numbers that multiply to $-42$ and add to 1, so the pair is 7 and -6.

That gives the factorisation below. The equation then becomes a product equal to zero.

$$n^2+n-42=(n+7)(n-6)$$

$$(n+7)(n-6)=0$$

Using the zero product rule, either $n+7=0$ or $n-6=0$. So the solutions are:

$$n=-7 \quad \text{or} \quad n=6$$

Do not stop after solving for one bracket, because a quadratic usually has two roots. Ensure you calculate the value for both factors to provide a complete answer.

## Stretch: solve after rearranging

Sometimes the quadratic is not equal to zero at first. Before factorising, move everything to one side so the equation becomes $x^2+bx+c=0$.

For $x^2+5x=14$, subtract 14 from both sides to get:
$$x^2+5x-14=0$$

Now find two numbers that multiply to $-14$ and add to 5, which are 7 and -2. So the factorised equation is:
$$(x+7)(x-2)=0$$

The final solutions are:
$$x=-7 \quad \text{or} \quad x=2$$
A common mistake is to forget the rearranging step and use the wrong constant term.

## Check yourself

1. Which of these is a quadratic equation?

   - $$x^2+5x+6$

   - $$x^2+5x+6=0$ ✔

   - $$x+5=0$

   - $$x^3+5x+6=0$

   _Explanation:_ B is correct because it is an equation and the highest power of $x$ is 2. The common wrong answer A is only a quadratic expression, because it is not set equal to anything.

2. Which factorisation is correct for $a^2-9a+18$?

   - $$(a+3)(a+6)$

   - $$(a-3)(a-6)$ ✔

   - $$(a-2)(a-9)$

   - $$(a+2)(a-9)$

   _Explanation:_ B is correct because 3 and 6 multiply to 18, and both signs must be negative to make the middle term $-9a$. The common wrong answer A uses the right numbers but gives $+9a$, not $-9a$.

3. Solve $x^2+5x=14$.

   - $$x=7$ or $x=-2$

   - $$x=-7$ or $x=2$ ✔

   - $$x=14$ or $x=1$

   - $$x=-14$ or $x=-1$

   _Explanation:_ B is correct because you must first rearrange to $x^2+5x-14=0$, then factorise to $(x+7)(x-2)=0$. The common wrong answer A comes from solving $x+7=0$ incorrectly and forgetting that it gives $x=-7$.
