## Meet the coordinate grid

![Coordinate grid with labelled x-axis, y-axis, origin, point A at (4,-2), and point B at (-1,3), with arrows showing x first then y second](../assets/001-91894f6b-2e0a-41cf-9785-b3df53fe33c6.jpeg)

A coordinate grid uses two perpendicular axes to show exact positions. The horizontal axis is the x-axis. The vertical axis is the y-axis. They meet at the origin, $(0,0)$.

Coordinates are written as $(x,y)$. The first number tells you how far left or right to move, and the second tells you how far up or down to move. In the diagram, point $A$ is at $(4,-2)$, so you move 4 right and 2 down. Point $B$ is at $(-1,3)$, so you move 1 left and 3 up.

## Reading and plotting negative coordinates

Negative coordinates only change the direction you move. Negative $x$ means left, and negative $y$ means down.

To plot $(-4,-3)$, start at the origin and move 4 squares left. Then move 3 squares down and mark the point.

When reading a point from a grid, reverse the process by counting across first and then up or down. A very common mistake is to swap the order and write the $y$-coordinate first.

## Finding a midpoint

![Two-panel coordinate diagram with segment from B(-3,3) to C(3,0), midpoint M(0,1.5), and examples of the lines x = -2 and y = 4](../assets/002-6c8c974a-ef9a-4cf5-8038-6d2585457064.jpeg)

In the left panel, the midpoint is the point exactly halfway between the endpoints of a line segment. To find it, average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates.

For points $B(-3,3)$ and $C(3,0)$, work out each average separately:

$$x_{\text{mid}} = \frac{-3+3}{2} = 0$$

$$y_{\text{mid}} = \frac{3+0}{2} = 1.5$$

These averages are the two coordinates of the midpoint. So the midpoint is $(0,1.5)$. A common mistake is to find only one coordinate, but a midpoint always needs both.

## Lines where x or y is fixed

![Two coordinate grids showing the vertical line $x = -2$ with point $(-2,3)$ and the horizontal line $y = 4$ with point $(1,4)$](../assets/003-coordinates-fixed-lines-52cbfa96723affdb.png)

A line $x = -2$ is vertical because every point on it has the same $x$-coordinate. A line $y = 4$ is horizontal because every point on it has the same $y$-coordinate.

You can test a point by checking the fixed coordinate. For example, $(-2,3)$ lies on $x = -2$ but $(3,-2)$ does not, and $(1,4)$ lies on $y = 4$ but $(1,3)$ does not.

## Stretch: completing a square on a grid

In a square $ABCD$, the letters go around the shape in order, so the move from $B$ to $C$ matches the move from $A$ to $D$. This lets you find a missing corner by copying a horizontal move and a vertical move.

For $A(-1,3)$, $B(3,2)$, and $C(2,-2)$, the move from $B$ to $C$ is 1 left and 4 down. Apply that same move to $A$ to get $D=(-2,-1)$.

## Check yourself

1. A point is 2 squares left and 5 squares up from the origin. What are its coordinates?

   - $$(-2,5)$ ✔

   - $$(5,-2)$

   - $$(2,-5)$

   - $$(-5,2)$

   _Explanation:_ $$(-2,5)$ is correct because x comes first, so 2 left gives $x=-2$, and 5 up gives $y=5$. The common wrong answer is $(5,-2)$ because it swaps the coordinates and puts the up/down move first.

2. Which statement is true?

   - The line $x=3$ is horizontal.

   - The line $y=3$ is vertical.

   - The line $x=3$ is vertical. ✔

   - The line $x=3$ passes through the origin.

   _Explanation:_ The line $x=3$ is vertical because $x$ stays fixed at $3$ while $y$ can vary. The common wrong answer is A because students often match $x$ with horizontal movement, but a fixed $x$-value makes a vertical line.

3. Points $A(-1,3)$, $B(3,2)$, and $C(2,-2)$ are consecutive vertices of square $ABCD$. What are the coordinates of $D$?

   - $$(-2,-1)$ ✔

   - $$(0,-1)$

   - $$(-2,1)$

   - $$(-1,-1)$

   _Explanation:_ $$(-2,-1)$ is correct because the move from $B$ to $C$ is 1 left and 4 down, and the same move from $A$ gives $D$. The common wrong answer is $(-1,-1)$ because it copies only the 4 down part and forgets the 1 left.
