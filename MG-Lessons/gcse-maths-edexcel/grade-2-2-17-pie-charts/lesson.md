## Pie charts show parts of a whole

![Pie chart of spending with Rent largest and Travel marked as a right angle sector](../assets/001-893a83f3-995a-4695-8168-02313909ada8.jpeg)

A pie chart is a circle split into sectors to show how a whole group is shared between categories. Each sector represents one category, and its size shows the frequency or amount.

The whole pie chart is always a full circle, so the total angle is $360^\circ$. A larger sector means a larger share of the total.

A right angle sector is $90^\circ$, so it is $\frac{90}{360}=\frac{1}{4}$ of the circle. This fact is very useful when reading totals from a pie chart.

## Reading a sector as a fraction

Suppose Leah's travel sector is a right angle and travel costs £180. Since a right angle is $90^\circ$, the travel sector is $\frac{90}{360}=\frac{1}{4}$ of the whole pie chart.

If £180 is one quarter of the total, multiply by 4 to find the whole amount. So Leah's total spending is £720.

Read the angle, turn it into a fraction of $360^\circ$, then scale up to the whole. This is the key method for reading values from a pie chart.

## Changing between frequency and angle

When you start with a table, use the frequency to find each sector angle. When you start with a pie chart, use the sector angle to work back to the frequency.

Use these two rules. To find an angle, use $\frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total frequency}} \times 360^\circ$, and to find a frequency, use $\frac{\text{sector angle}}{360^\circ} \times \text{total frequency}$.

Example: Vanilla has frequency 15 and angle $90^\circ$. Since $90^\circ$ is one quarter of $360^\circ$, the total frequency is $15 \times 4 = 60$.

Then Strawberry is $\frac{120}{360} \times 60 = 20$ and Mint is $\frac{60}{360} \times 60 = 10$. Chocolate is the remaining $90^\circ$, so its frequency is 15.

## Worked example: drawing the chart

![Pie chart for biscuits with sectors 90 degrees, 126 degrees and 144 degrees labelled Plain, Cream and Chocolate](../assets/002-8c0ec564-31c4-418b-93e5-be24f44dad3b.jpeg)

For biscuits with frequencies Plain 10, Cream 14, and Chocolate 16, first add the frequencies to get the total: $10 + 14 + 16 = 40$. Chocolate is the mode because 16 is the largest frequency.

Now convert each frequency into an angle. Plain is $\frac{10}{40}\times 360^\circ = 90^\circ$, Cream is $\frac{14}{40}\times 360^\circ = 126^\circ$, and Chocolate is $\frac{16}{40}\times 360^\circ = 144^\circ$.

Always check the angles before you draw: $90^\circ + 126^\circ + 144^\circ = 360^\circ$. Draw one radius, measure the first two angles from the centre with a protractor, and the last sector should finish the circle exactly.

## Fractions and probability

You can change data into a fraction by writing part over whole and simplifying. For 15 white cars out of 50, the fraction that are white is $\frac{15}{50}=\frac{3}{10}$.

If one car is chosen at random, probability uses the same part-over-whole idea. Since 5 out of 50 cars are blue, the probability of not blue is $\frac{45}{50}=\frac{9}{10}$.

## Comparing two pie charts safely

Pie charts are excellent for comparing proportions, which means shares of the whole. A larger sector means a larger share in that chart, but not necessarily a larger number.

For example, School B might have a larger French sector than School A, but if School B has fewer students overall, it could still have fewer French students. You can only compare actual numbers when the totals are also known.

## Check yourself

1. What does the whole circle in a pie chart represent?

   - 100 items exactly

   - the whole data set, shown by $360^\circ$ ✔

   - the largest category only

   - only the categories with labels

   _Explanation:_ The full circle always represents the whole data set, and a full turn is $360^\circ$. A common wrong answer is 100, but 100 is for percentages, not the angle total in a pie chart.

2. A pie chart represents 60 pupils. A sector has angle $120^\circ$. What is its frequency?

   - 15

   - 20 ✔

   - 30

   - 40

   _Explanation:_ $$120^\circ$ is $\frac{120}{360}=\frac{1}{3}$ of the circle, and $\frac{1}{3}$ of 60 is 20. The common wrong answer 30 comes from treating $120^\circ$ as half a circle, but half a circle is $180^\circ$.

3. In a car park there are 18 black, 12 silver, 15 white and 5 blue cars. If one car is chosen at random, what is the probability that it is not blue?

   - $$\frac{1}{10}$

   - $$\frac{3}{10}$

   - $$\frac{9}{10}$ ✔

   - $$\frac{5}{9}$

   _Explanation:_ There are 50 cars in total and 45 are not blue, so the probability is $\frac{45}{50}=\frac{9}{10}$. The common wrong answer $\frac{1}{10}$ is the probability of blue, not not blue.

4. Stretch: School A has 48 students studying French. In School B, French is the largest sector on a pie chart, but the total number of students in School B is not given. What can you say?

   - School B definitely has more than 48 French students

   - School B definitely has fewer than 48 French students

   - You can only say French is the largest proportion in School B ✔

   - Both schools have the same number of French students

   _Explanation:_ The pie chart for School B only tells you the share of French within School B, not the actual number. A common wrong answer is A, but a large proportion of a small school could still be fewer than 48 students.
