## Listing without missing anything

Systematic listing means writing outcomes in a clear pattern so you do not miss any and do not repeat any. The complete list of outcomes is called the sample space.

The key rule is to keep one choice fixed, list everything that goes with it, then move to the next choice. This works for menus, coins, dice and number cards.

Example: sandwiches are Cheese or Tuna, and drinks are Tea, Juice or Water. Fix Cheese first to get Cheese and Tea, Cheese and Juice, Cheese and Water, then do the same with Tuna for 6 combinations altogether.

## Repeating an action and recording order

![alt="Tree diagram of three coin tosses branching to H or T each time, with outcomes HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH and TTT labelled"](../assets/001-3ea6c158-22d8-4562-879a-f6e2ab94cf32.jpeg)

When the same action happens more than once, each position must be recorded. For three coin tosses, HHT and HTH are different because the T happens on a different toss.

Build the list in stages. Start with two tosses, HH, HT, TH, TT, then attach H and T to each one to get HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT.

There are 8 outcomes altogether, so the sample space is complete when every two-toss start has been extended. A common mistake is to stop at HHH and TTT and miss the mixed outcomes.

## Number cards and repeated digits

When you make numbers from cards, each card can usually be used once unless the question says otherwise. So with cards $4$, $6$ and $9$, the number $44$ is not allowed because there is only one $4$ card.

To list all two-digit numbers, fix the tens digit first. Starting with $4$ gives $46$ and $49$, starting with $6$ gives $64$ and $69$, and starting with $9$ gives $94$ and $96$.

If some cards show the same digit, only keep different final numbers. From $2$, $2$ and $5$ the different three-digit numbers are $225$, $252$ and $522$.

## When order does not matter

Sometimes swapping the order does not create a new result. In a match list, Lions v Tigers is the same match as Tigers v Lions, so it should be written once.

For four clubs Lions, Tigers, Eagles and Sharks, start with Lions: Lions v Tigers, Lions v Eagles, and Lions v Sharks. Then move to Tigers and only add new matches: Tigers v Eagles and Tigers v Sharks.

Finally add Eagles v Sharks. The full list has 6 matches, and writing reverse copies would double-count.

## Using a list for probability

![alt="Sample space grid for two dice with the six outcomes that sum to 7 highlighted"](../assets/002-2abbf911-4447-4448-9a67-216471c6e468.jpeg)
A full list helps with probability because you can count favourable outcomes and total outcomes. For equally likely outcomes, $P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$.

In the image above, the grid represents the sample space for two dice. There are $6 \times 6 = 36$ ordered outcomes because results like $(1,6)$ and $(6,1)$ are considered distinct.

To find the probability of rolling a total of 7, we count the highlighted outcomes: $(1,6)$, $(2,5)$, $(3,4)$, $(4,3)$, $(5,2)$ and $(6,1)$. This counting process reveals exactly 6 favourable outcomes.

Therefore, $P(\text{total } 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$. A common mistake is to say $\frac{1}{11}$ because 7 is one of eleven possible totals (2 to 12), but these totals are not equally likely.

## Check yourself

1. A cafe has 2 sandwiches and 3 drinks. If you choose one sandwich and one drink, how many different lunches are possible?

   - 5

   - 6 ✔

   - 3

   - 9

   _Explanation:_ There are 2 sandwich choices and each can be paired with 3 drinks, so the total is $2 \times 3 = 6$. The common wrong answer 5 comes from adding 2 and 3 instead of matching one sandwich with one drink.

2. The cards are 1, 1 and 4. How many different three-digit numbers can you make?

   - 6

   - 3 ✔

   - 2

   - 4

   _Explanation:_ The different numbers are $114$, $141$ and $411$, so there are 3. The common wrong answer 6 counts swaps of the two 1 cards as different arrangements, but they do not create new final numbers.

3. Six teams each play every other team once. How many matches are there?

   - 30

   - 15 ✔

   - 12

   - 21

   _Explanation:_ A systematic list gives 5 matches from the first team, then 4 new ones from the second, then 3, 2 and 1, so $5+4+3+2+1=15$. The common wrong answer 30 counts each match twice, once as A v B and again as B v A.
