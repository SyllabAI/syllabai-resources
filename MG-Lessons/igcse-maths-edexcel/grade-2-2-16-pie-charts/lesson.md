## What a pie chart shows

![Pie chart with labelled sectors, centre, a 90 degree sector, a 120 degree sector, and the whole circle marked as 360 degrees](../assets/001-bdd6d6c2-6bc7-4c3b-8506-d68ed08fa0bb.jpeg)

A pie chart is a circle split into sectors, with each sector showing one category from the data. The whole circle represents the total, so all the sector angles must add to $360^{\circ}$.

A bigger sector means a bigger share of the total, but only within that same pie chart. Key words to know are sector, angle of a sector, and frequency.

To turn an angle into a share of the data, use this formula.

$$
\text{fraction of total} = \frac{\text{sector angle}}{360^{\circ}}
$$

This links pictures, fractions, and totals.

## Reading angles as fractions

When reading a pie chart, start by spotting the largest and smallest sectors. The largest sector shows the most common category, and the smallest sector shows the least common.

Some angle facts are quick shortcuts. $90^\circ$ is $\frac{1}{4}$, $180^\circ$ is $\frac{1}{2}$, and $120^\circ$ is $\frac{1}{3}$ of a full circle.

For example, if a snack sector is $120^\circ$, write the fraction of the whole. $$\frac{120}{360} = \frac{1}{3}$$ So one third of the people chose that snack, and the probability of choosing one at random is also $\frac{1}{3}$.

## Finding the total from one sector

Suppose the transport sector is a right angle and the person spent £300 on transport. A right angle is $90^\circ$, so transport is $\frac{90^\circ}{360^\circ} = \frac{1}{4}$ of the total spending.

If £300 is one quarter, multiply by 4 to scale up to the whole circle. $$300 \times 4 = 1200$$ So the total spending is £1200.

The common mistake is to treat £300 as the total. It is only one sector, so you must compare it with the whole $360^\circ$ first.

## Turning frequencies into angles

When you draw a pie chart, you usually start with a frequency table. First add the frequencies to get the total, then convert each frequency into a sector angle.

Use this formula to convert a frequency into an angle:

$$\text{sector angle} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total frequency}} \times 360^{\circ}$$

This tells you how much of the circle each category should take. Sometimes it is faster to find the angle for one item first.

For a total of 40 people, one person is worth $\frac{360^{\circ}}{40} = 9^{\circ}$, so a category with frequency 14 has angle $14 \times 9^{\circ} = 126^{\circ}$.

## Worked example: drawing the chart

![Protractor at the centre of a circle measuring a 126 degree sector from a starting radius](../assets/002-5759f43a-45e6-46c7-8796-edcccba62584.jpeg)
Suppose biscuit choices are Chocolate 10, Oat 14, and Shortbread 16. First find the total frequency.
$$10 + 14 + 16 = 40$$

Now find the angle for one person by dividing the full circle by the total frequency.
$$\frac{360^\circ}{40} = 9^\circ$$

Then multiply each frequency by $9^\circ$ to get the individual sector angles.
$$\begin{aligned} \text{Chocolate} &= 10 \times 9^\circ = 90^\circ \\ \text{Oat} &= 14 \times 9^\circ = 126^\circ \\ \text{Shortbread} &= 16 \times 9^\circ = 144^\circ \end{aligned}$$

Check the angles add to $360^\circ$ before drawing.
$$90^\circ + 126^\circ + 144^\circ = 360^\circ$$
Then draw the circle with a compass, measure each angle from the centre with a protractor, and label the sectors.

## Comparing pie charts carefully

Within one pie chart, a larger sector always represents a larger frequency. However, comparing sectors across two different pie charts is only safe if both charts represent the exact same total frequency.

For example, $25\%$ of 200 students is 50, whereas $20\%$ of 400 students is 80. Despite having a smaller percentage and sector size, the second group actually contains more students because the total is larger.

To avoid mistakes, always look for the total sample size or calculate the actual frequencies for each category. Never judge or compare quantities from different pie charts by sector size alone.

## Check yourself

1. In any pie chart, what must the angles of all the sectors add up to?

   - $$360^\circ$ ✔

   - $$180^\circ$

   - The total frequency

   - $$90^\circ$

   _Explanation:_ A pie chart uses a full circle, and a full turn is $360^\circ$. A common wrong answer is $180^\circ$, but that is only a half-turn, not a complete circle.

2. A $72^\circ$ sector represents 20 students. How many students are in the whole survey?

   - 100 ✔

   - 50

   - 92

   - 360

   _Explanation:_ $$72^\circ$ is $\frac{72}{360} = \frac{1}{5}$ of the full circle, so 20 students is one fifth of the total and the total is $20 \times 5 = 100$. A common wrong answer is 50, which comes from halving or guessing instead of scaling from one fifth to the whole.

3. Stretch: School A has $25\%$ walkers out of 200 students, and School B has $20\%$ walkers out of 400 students. Which school has more walkers?

   - School A, because $25\%$ is larger

   - School B, because $20\%$ of 400 is 80 ✔

   - They have the same number

   - It is impossible to tell

   _Explanation:_ School A has $0.25 \times 200 = 50$ walkers, while School B has $0.20 \times 400 = 80$ walkers, so School B has more. The common wrong answer is School A because $25\%$ looks bigger than $20\%$, but percentages must be compared with their totals.
