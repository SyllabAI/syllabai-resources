## Spotting the clues

![Composite figure showing a radius perpendicular to a tangent, a centre angle twice a matching circumference angle, opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral summing to 180 degrees, and the alternate segment theorem](../assets/001-75fff534-b479-42aa-95f2-0bd3733088f1.jpeg)

Circle theorems help you turn a picture into facts about angles and lengths. Most questions become easier once you label the centre, tangents, chords, and any equal radii.

A radius goes from the centre to the circumference, a chord joins two points on the circumference, and a tangent touches the circle once. If a triangle contains two radii, it is isosceles, so its base angles are equal.

The big angle facts are $90^\circ$ between radius and tangent, centre angle is $2 \times$ the circumference angle on the same chord, same segment angles equal, and cyclic opposite angles sum to $180^\circ$. Later, we will add the two length theorems for chords and secants.

## Radii, tangents, and hidden isosceles triangles

Whenever a triangle uses the centre and two points on the circumference, two sides are radii. That makes an isosceles triangle, so the base angles are equal. For instance, if $\angle ABO = 48^{\circ}$ in triangle $AOB$, then $\angle OAB = 48^{\circ}$ as well.

$$$\angle AOB = 180^{\circ} - 48^{\circ} - 48^{\circ} = 84^{\circ}$$

Now consider a tangent. If $AB$ is tangent at $B$ and $\angle BOA = 72^{\circ}$, then the radius $OB$ is perpendicular to the tangent, meaning $\angle ABO = 90^{\circ}$. We can then calculate the final angle in the triangle:

$$$\angle BAO = 180^{\circ} - 90^{\circ} - 72^{\circ} = 18^{\circ}$$

If two tangents are drawn from the same external point, the tangent lengths are equal. The radii to the points of contact also make two right angles, which often helps with angle chasing.

## Centre angles and same segments

When an angle at the centre and an angle at the circumference stand on the same chord, the centre angle is double the circumference angle. So if $\angle BOC = 66^\circ$, then

$$$\angle BAC = \frac{66^\circ}{2} = 33^\circ$$

Angles in the same segment are equal when they stand on the same chord on the same side of it. So if $\angle ADB = 51^\circ$, then $$\angle ACB = 51^\circ$$ because both angles stand on chord $AB$.

Be careful with obtuse angles on the circumference. The matching angle at the centre can be a reflex angle, so do not always use the smaller centre angle.

## Cyclic quadrilaterals and the alternate segment theorem

If four vertices lie on the circumference, the quadrilateral is cyclic. Opposite angles add to $180^\circ$, so if $\angle ADC = 83^\circ$, then: $$\angle ABC = 180^\circ - 83^\circ = 97^\circ$$

The alternate segment theorem links a tangent and a chord. The angle between the tangent at $C$ and chord $CB$ equals the angle in the opposite segment, so first find that angle inside the circle.

Example: if $\angle ABC = 61^\circ$ and $\angle ACB = 73^\circ$, then: $$\angle BAC = 180^\circ - 61^\circ - 73^\circ = 46^\circ$$ Therefore the angle between the tangent at $C$ and chord $CB$ is $46^\circ$.

## Length theorems for chords and secants

![Side-by-side diagrams of intersecting chords inside a circle and two secants from an external point with labelled lengths](../assets/002-19d5c4f3-46c6-482c-aedb-680be194af46.jpeg)

Length questions use product rules rather than angle facts. There are two common cases: intersecting chords inside the circle, and two secants meeting outside the circle.

For intersecting chords, multiply the two parts of one chord and set it equal to the product on the other chord. If $AE = 5$, $BE = 9$, $CE = 6$, and $DE = x$, then the calculation is:

$$
5 \times 9 = 6x
$$

Solving for $x$ gives $x = \frac{45}{6} = 7.5$.

For two secants from an external point, use the rule 'external part times whole length'. If the external parts are $4$ and $3$, and the inside parts are $x$ and $10$, then the whole lengths are $x+4$ and $13$:

$$
4(x+4) = 3 \times 13
$$

This equation simplifies to $4x + 16 = 39$, which gives $x = 5.75$.

A common error is to multiply only the inside pieces. Always trace each secant from the outside point to the far end to ensure you are using the whole length for the calculation.

## Check yourself

1. A radius $OP$ meets a tangent at point $P$. What is the angle between $OP$ and the tangent?

   - $$90^{\circ}$ ✔

   - $$45^{\circ}$

   - $$180^{\circ}$

   - It depends on the radius

   _Explanation:_ A radius to the point of contact is always perpendicular to the tangent, so the angle is $90^{\circ}$. The common wrong answer $180^{\circ}$ treats the two lines as if they formed a straight line, but a radius and a tangent meet at a right angle, not along the same line.

2. In a circle, $\angle BOC = 74^\circ$ and $\angle BAC$ stands on the same chord $BC$. What is $\angle BAC$?

   - $$37^\circ$ ✔

   - $$74^\circ$

   - $$148^\circ$

   - $$106^\circ$

   _Explanation:_ The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference on the same chord, so $\angle BAC = 74^\circ \div 2 = 37^\circ$. The common wrong answer $74^\circ$ assumes the two angles are equal, but this theorem gives a $2:1$ ratio, not equality.

3. From external point $P$, one secant has external part $2$ and internal part $7$. Another has external part $4$ and internal part $x$. Which equation is correct?

   - $$2 \times 7 = 4x$

   - $$2 \times 9 = 4(x+4)$ ✔

   - $$2 \times 9 = 4x$

   - $$2 \times 7 = 4(x+4)$

   _Explanation:_ For each secant, multiply the external part by the whole length from the outside point (external part + internal part). The common wrong answer $2 \times 7 = 4x$ uses only the inside pieces, but the secant theorem needs external $\times$ whole, not inside $\times$ inside. Here, the first whole length is $2+7=9$ and the second is $4+x$.
