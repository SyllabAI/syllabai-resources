## What gradient means

![Coordinate grid with line through A(1,2) and B(4,5), showing run +3 and rise +3](../assets/001-4f4771d4-9ed1-4806-89d9-59cd1bb1b3a2.jpeg)

The gradient of a straight line tells you how steep it is. It measures how much the $y$-value changes for each increase of 1 in $x$.

A point such as $(3,5)$ means 3 across and 5 up. Any two points on a straight line are enough to work out its gradient. We often describe gradient as "rise over run".

$$$\text{gradient}=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$$

The rise is the vertical change and the run is the horizontal change.

## Finding gradient from a graph

When you use a graph, choose two clear grid points where the line passes exactly through intersections. Avoid guessing from a point between squares.

Suppose the line passes through $(-2,-3)$ and $(0,5)$. The rise is $5-(-3)=8$ and the run is $0-(-2)=2$.

So the gradient is $\frac{8}{2}=4$. Because the line goes up as you move right, the positive sign makes sense.

## Signs and special cases

![Four coordinate grids showing positive gradient, negative gradient, a horizontal line, and a vertical line](../assets/002-1a11d8da-ef32-434b-9fe9-2ba208fa7b4d.jpeg)

Positive gradients slope upwards from left to right, and negative gradients slope downwards from left to right. A fractional gradient such as $\frac{1}{3}$ means the line goes up 1 for every 3 across.

A horizontal line has no change in $y$, so its gradient is 0. A vertical line has no change in $x$, so working out gradient would mean dividing by 0, which is not allowed.

This sign check catches many mistakes. If the line goes down as you move right, the gradient must be negative.

## Using two coordinates

If no graph is given, use the coordinate formula directly. For points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$,

$$$
\text{gradient}=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}
$$

This compares the change in $y$ with the change in $x$. For example, between $(2,3)$ and $(6,15)$, the change in $y$ is $15-3=12$ and the change in $x$ is $6-2=4$. So the gradient is $\frac{12}{4}=3$.

You may swap the order, but you must swap it in both the top and the bottom. Mixing the order gives the wrong sign.

## Finding a missing value

Sometimes the gradient is given and one coordinate contains an unknown. Substitute the values into the same formula, then solve the equation.

Example: the line through $A(3,-2)$ and $B(7,k)$ has gradient 4. $$4=\frac{k-(-2)}{7-3}=\frac{k+2}{4}$$

Multiply by 4 to get $16=k+2$. Then subtract 2 to get $k=14$. Check the answer against the direction of the line: moving right by 4 with gradient 4 means the line should go up by 16, and $-2+16=14$.

## Check yourself

1. A line goes up 6 squares as it goes right 2 squares. What is its gradient?

   - 3 ✔

   - $$\frac{1}{3}$

   - 8

   - -3

   _Explanation:_ The gradient is rise over run, so $\frac{6}{2}=3$. B is the common mistake because it reverses the fraction and does run over rise instead.

2. What is the gradient of the line through $(2,4)$ and $(2,-1)$?

   - 0

   - no gradient ✔

   - 5

   - -5

   _Explanation:_ Both points have the same $x$-coordinate, so the run is 0. That makes the line vertical, so its gradient is not defined; A is the common mistake because students confuse vertical and horizontal lines, but only horizontal lines have gradient 0.

3. Stretch: The line through $(-1,6)$ and $(k,18)$ has gradient 3. What is $k$?

   - 3 ✔

   - 5

   - -5

   - 1

   _Explanation:_ Use $3=\frac{18-6}{k-(-1)}=\frac{12}{k+1}$. Then $3(k+1)=12$, so $k+1=4$ and $k=3$; B is the common error from adding 1 instead of subtracting 1 at the final step.
