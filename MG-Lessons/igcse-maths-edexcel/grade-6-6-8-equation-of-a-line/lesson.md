## Reading gradient and intercept

![Coordinate graph showing the line y = 2x + 3, the y-intercept at (0,3), a gradient triangle with rise 2 and run 1, and a parallel line y = 2x - 1](../assets/001-e0b79d1f-2097-4e7f-aa97-936b45337d04.jpeg)

Straight lines are usually written as $y=mx+c$. Here $m$ is the gradient and $c$ is the y-intercept, which is where the line crosses the y-axis.

The gradient tells you how steep the line is and whether it rises or falls as $x$ increases. A positive gradient slopes up, a negative gradient slopes down, and lines with the same gradient are parallel.

On the graph, $y=2x+3$ crosses the y-axis at $(0,3)$. Its gradient is $2$ because moving $1$ to the right moves $2$ up.

## Writing an equation

To build a straight-line equation, place the gradient in front of $x$ and then add the y-intercept. If the gradient is 3 and the line crosses the y-axis at $(0,-4)$, then $m=3$, $c=-4$, and the equation is $y=3x-4$.

Sometimes the terms are written in a different order, such as $y=5-x$. Rewrite it as $y=-x+5$ so the hidden coefficient of $x$ is $-1$.

Always compare with $y=mx+c$ after rewriting. That stops you mixing up the gradient with the intercept.

## Rearranging to standard form

Some equations are not already in the form $y=mx+c$. Rearrange them until $y$ is on its own, then compare with the standard form.

Worked example: from $x+4y=12$, subtract $x$ from both sides to get $4y=12-x$. Then divide every term by 4, giving $y=3-\frac{1}{4}x$.

Write it in the usual order as $y=-\frac{1}{4}x+3$. Now the gradient is $-\frac{1}{4}$ and the y-intercept is 3. Do not forget to divide the $x$ term by 4 as well.

## From two points to a line

![Coordinate graph showing points A(0,2) and B(3,8) on the line y = 2x + 2 with a gradient triangle labelled change in x = 3 and change in y = 6](../assets/002-9c012cfe-2373-4169-bb50-e5af38d2570c.jpeg)

If you know two points on a line, use $m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$. This represents the change in $y$ divided by the change in $x$.

For the points $(0,2)$ and $(3,8)$, the change in $y$ is $8-2=6$ and the change in $x$ is $3-0=3$. So the gradient is calculated as $m=\frac{6}{3}=2$.

Because one point is on the y-axis, the y-intercept is 2 straight away. Substituting $m=2$ and $c=2$ into the general form $y=mx+c$ gives the equation $y=2x+2$.

## Parallel and horizontal lines

Parallel lines never meet and always have the same gradient. If one line is $y=-3x+4$, every parallel line must also have gradient $-3$.

So a parallel line crossing the y-axis at $(0,7)$ has equation $y=-3x+7$. Only the y-intercept changes.

A horizontal line has gradient $0$, so its equation is $y=c$. For example, $y=3$ stays level across the graph.

## Check yourself

1. For the line $y=4x-6$, which statement is correct?

   - Gradient 4, y-intercept $(0,-6)$ ✔

   - Gradient -6, y-intercept $(0,4)$

   - Gradient 4, y-intercept -6

   - Gradient -4, y-intercept $(0,6)$

   _Explanation:_ In $y=mx+c$, the coefficient of $x$ is the gradient, so $m=4$, and the line crosses the y-axis at $(0,-6)$. Option C is the most common mistake because -6 is the intercept value, but the intercept as a point must be written with $x=0$, so it is $(0,-6)$.

2. The equation $2y-6x=10$ is rearranged into $y=mx+c$. What is $m$?

   - 3 ✔

   - -3

   - -6

   - 5

   _Explanation:_ Add $6x$ to both sides and divide by 2 to get $y=3x+5$, so the gradient is 3. Option B is the most common mistake because students divide 6 by 2 but forget that moving $-6x$ to the other side changes its sign.

3. A line is parallel to $2y-8x=6$ and crosses the y-axis at $(0,-2)$. What is its equation?

   - $$y=4x-2$ ✔

   - $$y=-4x-2$

   - $$y=4x+3$

   - $$y=2x-2$

   _Explanation:_ First rearrange $2y-8x=6$ to $y=4x+3$, so any parallel line must have gradient 4. Using the new intercept -2 gives $y=4x-2$, and option C is the most common mistake because it keeps the original intercept 3 instead of using the new one.
