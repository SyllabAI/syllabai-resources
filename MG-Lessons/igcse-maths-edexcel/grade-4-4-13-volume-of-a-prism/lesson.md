## Prisms, cross-sections, and the main formula

![Generic prism with shaded front cross-section labelled cross-section area A and arrow showing prism length l](../assets/001-afc9d811-3886-4a45-b20a-9c7effb954d7.jpeg)
A prism is a 3D shape with the same 2D cross-section all the way through its length. You can think of it as a flat shape stretched straight backwards.

Volume measures the space inside a shape, so it is written in cubic units such as $\text{cm}^{3}$ or $\text{m}^{3}$. For any prism, the rule is

$$$V = A \times l$$

This works because the same area is repeated all the way along. Here, $A$ is the area of the cross-section and $l$ is the prism length.

## Worked example: triangular prism

A triangular prism has a right-angled triangular cross-section with base $6 \, \text{cm}$, height $5 \, \text{cm}$, and prism length $9 \, \text{cm}$. Start with the area of the triangle, not the volume.

The triangle area is $$A = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 5 = 15 \, \text{cm}^{2}$$ so the cross-section area is $15 \, \text{cm}^{2}$.

Now multiply by the prism length. $$V = 15 \times 9 = 135 \, \text{cm}^{3}$$ So the volume is $135 \, \text{cm}^{3}$. A common mistake is using the sloping side as the height, but the height must be perpendicular to the base.

## Finding the cross-section area

Before you can find the volume, you need the area formula that matches the cross-section. Rectangles use $A = lw$, triangles use $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$, and trapeziums use $A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$.

For a trapezium prism with parallel sides $5 \, \text{cm}$ and $11 \, \text{cm}$, height $4 \, \text{cm}$, and length $12 \, \text{cm}$, start with the trapezium area. $$A = \frac{1}{2}(5+11)\times4 = 32 \, \text{cm}^{2}$$ Now multiply that area by the prism length. $$V = 32 \times 12 = 384 \, \text{cm}^{3}$$

Forgetting the $\frac{1}{2}$ in the calculation would incorrectly double the answer. Always find the cross-section area before attempting to find the volume.

## Composite prisms

Some prisms have cross-sections made from more than one simple shape. Split the cross-section into rectangles or triangles, find the area of each part, then add them to get the total cross-section area.

For example, if an L-shaped cross-section is split into two rectangles with areas of 30 square centimetres and 20 square centimetres, the total cross-section area is 50 square centimetres. If the prism length is 8 cm, multiply 50 by 8.

$$$V = 50 \times 8 = 400$$

So the volume is 400 cubic centimetres. Add areas, not side lengths, when the cross-section is composite.

## Capacity and unit conversions

Volume questions about tanks and containers often finish in litres or millilitres. First find the volume in cubic centimetres, then convert at the end.

Useful facts: 1 litre equals 1000 cubic centimetres, and 1 millilitre equals 1 cubic centimetre.

For a prism, the volume is the cross-sectional area multiplied by the length. If a tank shaped like a prism has a cross-sectional area of 250 square centimetres and a length of 48 centimetres, multiply 250 by 48.

250 multiplied by 48 is 12,000, so the volume is 12,000 cubic centimetres. Since 1000 cubic centimetres equals 1 litre, the tank holds 12 litres.

In practical questions, keep the working in one set of units while finding the prism volume, then convert to litres or millilitres at the end.

## Check yourself

1. In the formula $V = A \times l$, what does $A$ represent?

   - The area of the cross-section ✔

   - The area of any one face

   - The total surface area

   - The perimeter of the cross-section

   _Explanation:_ The formula for a prism uses the repeated 2D cross-section, so $A$ must be the cross-section area. The common wrong answer is C because surface area also uses square units, but surface area measures the outside faces, not the space inside the prism.

2. A prism has a trapezium cross-section with parallel sides $7 \, \text{cm}$ and $9 \, \text{cm}$, height $3 \, \text{cm}$, and length $10 \, \text{cm}$. What is its volume?

   - $$160 \, \text{cm}^{3}$

   - $$240 \, \text{cm}^{3}$ ✔

   - $$320 \, \text{cm}^{3}$

   - $$480 \, \text{cm}^{3}$

   _Explanation:_ First find the trapezium area: $\frac{1}{2}(7+9)\times3 = 24 \, \text{cm}^{2}$. Then multiply by the length: $24 \times 10 = 240 \, \text{cm}^{3}$; the common wrong answer is D because it forgets the $\frac{1}{2}$ in the trapezium area formula.

3. A rectangular tank measures $80\,\text{cm} \times 45\,\text{cm} \times 50\,\text{cm}$. Each bucket holds $9\,\text{L}$. How many full buckets are needed to fill the tank?

   - 18

   - 20 ✔

   - 180

   - 200

   _Explanation:_ The tank volume is $80 \times 45 \times 50 = 180000\,\text{cm}^3 = 180\,\text{L}$, and $180 \div 9 = 20$. The common wrong answer is C because it stops after converting to litres and forgets to divide by the bucket size.
