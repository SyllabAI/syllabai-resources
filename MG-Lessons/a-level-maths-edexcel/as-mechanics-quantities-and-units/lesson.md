## Quantities, units and SI

![Flow diagram linking displacement, velocity, acceleration and force to their SI units: metre, metre per second, metre per second squared and newton](../assets/001-2d4b1c62-d578-4bd4-9621-5a31738aa4aa.jpeg)
In mechanics, a number without a unit is incomplete because $20\,\mathrm{m}$, $20\,\mathrm{s}$ and $20\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ describe different things. A quantity is something measurable, and a unit tells you the scale used.

An SI unit is the standard unit used internationally so calculations stay consistent. In AS Mechanics, the main SI base units are metre ($\mathrm{m}$), second ($\mathrm{s}$) and kilogram ($\mathrm{kg}$).

Many other units are built from these. For example, velocity uses $\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, acceleration uses $\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}$, and force uses $\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}$.

## Distance, displacement, speed and velocity

![Number line showing east as positive and west as negative, with a journey 80 m east then 30 m west ending at +50 m, labelled distance 110 m and displacement +50 m east](../assets/002-bc3495da-3961-4240-9f55-cf4c8abcc5bf.jpeg)

Distance is the total length travelled, and speed is distance divided by time, so both are scalars. Displacement is change in position, and velocity is displacement divided by time, so both are vectors.

In one-dimensional mechanics, choose a positive direction and keep it throughout the question. If east is positive, then west is negative, so turning back reduces displacement but still adds to distance.

Average speed uses distance divided by time, while average velocity uses displacement divided by time. In the pictured journey, these are different because the object turns back.

## Derived units and the newton

Derived units are built by combining simpler units. Velocity is displacement divided by time, so its unit is $\text{m s}^{-1}$, and acceleration is change in velocity divided by time, so its unit is $\text{m s}^{-2}$.

Worked example: a particle changes velocity from $3 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ to $15 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ in 4 s. Using $$a = \frac{v-u}{t} = \frac{15-3}{4} = 3$$ the numerical value of the acceleration is 3.

The unit check is $$\frac{\text{m s}^{-1}}{\text{s}} = \text{m s}^{-2}$$ so the full answer is $3 \, \text{m s}^{-2}$, not just 3. One newton is $1 \, \text{kg m s}^{-2}$, which links mass and acceleration to force.

## Convert to SI units before calculating

Before using a mechanics formula, convert lengths to metres, times to seconds and masses to kilograms unless the question says otherwise. Useful facts are $1 \, \text{km} = 1000 \, \text{m}$, $1 \, \text{minute} = 60 \, \text{s}$, and converting from $\text{km h}^{-1}$ to $\text{m s}^{-1}$ means multiplying by $5/18$.

Worked example: a runner covers $1.5 \, \text{km}$ in 5 minutes. First convert the distance and time to SI units: $$1.5 \times 1000 = 1500 \, \text{m}, \qquad 5 \times 60 = 300 \, \text{s}$$ After these calculations, both values are expressed in the correct standard units.

Now use the converted values to find the average speed: $$\text{speed} = \frac{1500}{300} = 5$$ The final answer is $5 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. Dividing $1.5$ by $300$ would give a mixed-unit answer, not a proper SI speed.

## Use units to check formulae

In $v = u + at$, the term $at$ must have the same unit as $u$ because only like units can be added. Since $a$ has unit $\text{m s}^{-2}$ and $t$ has unit $\text{s}$, the product $at$ has unit $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

Worked example: if $u = 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$, $a = 1.5 \, \text{m s}^{-2}$ and $t = 8 \, \text{s}$, then $$v = u + at = 4 + 1.5 \times 8 = 16$$ so the final velocity is $16 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$.

For displacement, $$\text{m s}^{-1} \times \text{s} = \text{m}, \qquad \text{m s}^{-2} \times \text{s}^{2} = \text{m}$$ so both terms in $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}$ are measured in metres. Read carefully: the symbol $s$ in the formula means displacement, but the unit symbol s means seconds.

## Check yourself

1. Which quantity is measured in $\text{m s}^{-2}$?

   - Acceleration ✔

   - Velocity

   - Displacement

   - Force

   _Explanation:_ Acceleration is change in velocity per second, so its unit is metres per second squared. The common wrong answer is velocity, but velocity is only metres per second, $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

2. Which statement correctly describes $1 \, \text{N}$?

   - It is the force that gives a $1 \, \text{kg}$ mass an acceleration of $1 \, \text{m s}^{-2}$ ✔

   - It is the force needed to give a $1 \, \text{kg}$ mass a speed of $1 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$

   - It has the same unit as acceleration

   - It is another name for kilogram

   _Explanation:_ By definition, $1 \, \text{N} = 1 \, \text{kg m s}^{-2}$, so it is the force needed for $1 \, \text{kg}$ to accelerate at $1 \, \text{m s}^{-2}$. The common wrong answer is B, which confuses speed with acceleration; force is linked to change in velocity, not just having a velocity.

3. Which expression has the unit of displacement?

   - $$at$

   - $$ut$ ✔

   - $$u+a$

   - $$\frac{u}{t}$

   _Explanation:_ $$u$ has unit $\text{m s}^{-1}$ and $t$ has unit $\text{s}$, so $ut$ has unit $\text{m}$. The common wrong answer is $at$, but $\text{m s}^{-2} \times \text{s} = \text{m s}^{-1}$, which is a velocity, not a displacement.
