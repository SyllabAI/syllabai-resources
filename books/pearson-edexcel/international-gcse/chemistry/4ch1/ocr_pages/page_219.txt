You have seen the thermal decomposition of metal carbonates before in
Chapter 13. These are examples of endothermic reactions. You have to heat a
carbonate constantly to make it decompose.

For example, copper(I) carbonate (green) decomposes on heating to produce
copper(I!) oxide (black).

CuCO,(s) + CuO(s) + CO.(g)
Similarly, zinc carbonate decomposes to form zinc oxide when heated.
ZnCO,(s) + ZnO(s) + CO.(g)

In an endothermic change, the products have more energy than the reactants
so we Say that the products are /ess stable than the reactants. That extra
energy has to come from somewhere, and it is taken from the surroundings.
In the case of the thermal decomposition of carbonates in the laboratory, it
comes from the Bunsen burner.

Because the reactants are gaining energy, the enthalpy change of the reaction
AH is given a positive sign.

For example:
CaCO,(s) + CaO(s) + CO,(g) AH = +178 kJ/mol

This means that 178 kJ of heat energy must be absorbed to convert 1 mole of
calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.
