# 1950s calculators

> Excerpt: This resource is designed to develop a sense of what logarithms are, how they can be used and the sort of values they take. A set of short video clips demonstrate the use of 'log tables' to perform a selection of multiplications and other calculations.

## Log tables

Here is a question from a 1952 O-level exam paper:

Calculate, correct to three significant figures, \[\frac{1{\cdot}152\times(3{\cdot}902)^3}{(5{\cdot}463)^2} .\]

This is relatively straightforward to do using a calculator.

But electronic calculators only became commonly available from the late 1970s, so how did students of the 1950s answer a question like this?

They used **logarithm tables** (usually called “log tables”) where we would now use calculators, and in this resource we will explore how logarithms can help us to do calculations.

We’ll start with something simpler, and look at the exam question in a later section.

Use logarithm tables to evaluate, correct to three significant figures, \[336\times6290 .\]

We will do this in a series of steps.

Step 1

Can you write the numbers in this calculation (\(336\) and \(6290\)) in standard form?

Answer

\[336=3{\cdot}36\times10^2\quad\text{and}\quad6290=6{\cdot}29\times10^3\]

Step 2 (video)

In this video, you can see someone working out \(\log 336\) using [log tables](https://undergroundmathematics.org/exp-and-log/calculators1950/assets/logtables.pdf).

Can you describe what they are doing and explain how it relates to standard form?

Explanation

The logarithm is found in two parts.

1. The table gives the digits of \(\log{3{\cdot}36}\) (namely \(5263\)) and these go after the decimal point.

---

1. To get from \(3{\cdot}36\) to \(336\) we have to multiply by \(10^2\), so the whole number part of the logarithm is \(2\).

You can think of it as

\[\begin{align*} 336&=3{\cdot}36\times10^2\\ \log336&=\log{(3{\cdot}36\times10^2)}\\ &=\log{3{\cdot}36}+2\\ &=2+0{\cdot}5263 \end{align*}\]

## Add the difference

In the previous section we used [log tables](https://undergroundmathematics.org/exp-and-log/calculators1950/assets/logtables.pdf) to find the logarithms of numbers given to three significant figures. **One final twist**

In this resource we have dealt only with the logarithms of numbers greater than \(1\). Can you see the difficulty with using log tables to do calculations with numbers such as \(0{\cdot}0283\) ?

Bar notation

First, we write the number in standard form: \[0{\cdot}0283=2{\cdot}83\times10^{-2}\] so \[\log 0{\cdot}0283=-2+\log 2{\cdot}83=-2+0{\cdot}4518 .\]

If we were to work this out as \(-2+0{\cdot}4518=-1{\cdot}5482\), we would lose the clarity that the power of \(10\) is \(-2\). So instead we write the number as \(\bar2{\cdot}4518\) (called *bar notation*). Arithmetic with such quantities is carried out separately on the whole number part (called the [characteristic](https://undergroundmathematics.org/glossary/characteristic)) and the decimal part (called the [mantissa](https://undergroundmathematics.org/glossary/mantissa)) as illustrated here. Use of bar notation usually makes calculations easier.
