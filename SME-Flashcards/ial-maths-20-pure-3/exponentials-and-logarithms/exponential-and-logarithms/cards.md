## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_4cwF7QpXW9hCKkfc`)

**FRONT**

Define **e**, Euler's number.


**BACK**

$\text{e}$ is an **irrational** number, roughly $2 . 718$, which serves as the base of natural logarithms.

Like $π$, it cannot be written exactly as a fraction or as a terminating decimal.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_S8SNjx3MBj6bbCH3`)

**FRONT**

How is the graph of $y = \text{e}^{-x}$ related to $y = \text{e}^{x}$?


**BACK**

It is the reflection of $y = \text{e}^{x}$ in the $y$-axis.

The pair are in the form `y = \text{f} \left(x\right)` and `y = \text{f} \left(- x\right)`, which is what produces that reflection.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_8jJkpydVyRFVS27R`)

**FRONT**

What does $\mathrm{ln} x$ mean?


**BACK**

It is the **natural logarithm**, the logarithm to base $\text{e}$: $\mathrm{ln} x \equiv \mathrm{log}_{\text{e}} x$.

Everything that is true of logarithms in general is therefore true of $\mathrm{ln}$ as well.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_hmT5Xw9nXzgvzvZr`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{ln}$ is a number, in the way that $\text{e}$ and $π$ are.


**BACK**

**False.**

$\mathrm{ln}$ is a **function**, so it needs something to act on before it means anything at all.

$\mathrm{ln} 5$ is a number, but $\mathrm{ln}$ by itself is not, any more than $\mathrm{sin}$ by itself is.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_XJGtT3Fbfj5697Kr`)

**FRONT**

What is the relationship between $\text{e}^{x}$ and $\mathrm{ln} x$?


**BACK**

They are **inverse functions**, so each one undoes the other.

That is why $\text{e}^{\mathrm{ln}x} = x$, and why applying one of them to both sides of an equation strips the other away.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_tbb9rSJqYWW6qM7H`)

**FRONT**

An equation reads `\text{e}^{\text{f} \left(x\right)} = \text{g} \left(x\right)`. How do you get at `\text{f} \left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

Take $\mathrm{ln}$ of both sides, which leaves `\text{f} \left(x\right) = \ln \text{g} \left(x\right)`.

It works the other way round too: `\ln \text{f} \left(x\right) = \text{g} \left(x\right)` becomes `\text{f} \left(x\right) = \text{e}^{\text{g} \left(x\right)}`.

$\mathrm{ln} x$ has no value when $x$ is zero or negative.


**BACK**

**True.**

If $y = \text{e}^{kx}$ then `\frac{\text{d} y}{\text{d} x} = \_\_\_\_\_\_`, and if $y = \text{e}^{-kx}$ then `\frac{\text{d} y}{\text{d} x} = \_\_\_\_\_\_`.

The constant from the power comes down as a multiplier, and $y = \text{e}^{x}$ is simply the case $k = 1$.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_XW46Pr2CFGXG6Ncr`)

**FRONT**

What stays the same when you differentiate $\text{e}^{kx}$?


**BACK**

The exponential part itself: $\text{e}^{kx}$ reappears in the derivative unchanged.

Only a constant multiplier is added in front, which is what makes exponential derivatives unusually simple.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_nrf69nfyNrTfjHyD`)

**FRONT**

What is the derivative of $y = \text{e}^{-3x}$?


**BACK**

$\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = - 3 \text{e}^{-3x}$.

The minus sign comes down with the $3$, so a decay curve has a negative gradient everywhere along it.

## Card 12 — true_or_false (`fl_CGQnymyqYw63v3pt`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The gradient of $y = \text{e}^{x}$ is never zero.


**BACK**

**True.**

The gradient equals $\text{e}^{x}$, which is positive for every value of $x$.

So the curve is always increasing and has no stationary points at all.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_c7bKPm3cffGxRtrC`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the gradient of $y = \text{e}^{2x}$ at $x = 0$?


**BACK**

Differentiate to get $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = 2 \text{e}^{2x}$, then substitute $x = 0$.

Since $\text{e}^{0} = 1$, the gradient there is $2$.
