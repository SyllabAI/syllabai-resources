**BACK**

Cosecant is sometimes written $\mathrm{csc} x$ instead.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_K9n5c9BTKcZBVyPG`)

**FRONT**

How can $\mathrm{cot} x$ be written in terms of $\mathrm{sin} x$ and $\mathrm{cos} x$?


**BACK**

$\mathrm{cot} x = \frac{\mathrm{cos}x}{\mathrm{sin}x}$.

It is $\mathrm{tan} x$ turned upside down, so the sine and the cosine simply swap places.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_Td3vgcFTtxGsX7Xr`)

**FRONT**

Where does $\mathrm{cot} x$ take the value zero?


**BACK**

Wherever $\mathrm{tan} x$ is **undefined**.

A reciprocal is zero only where the function it came from grows without limit, so cotangent's zeros sit exactly where tangent has its asymptotes.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_cJmy7KcxVg75m8Vv`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve an equation containing $\mathrm{sec}$, $\text{cosec}$ or $\mathrm{cot}$?


**BACK**

Convert each one into the ordinary trigonometric function it is the reciprocal of, then solve in the usual way.

So $\mathrm{sec} x = 2$ becomes $\mathrm{cos} x = \frac{1}{2}$, which is an equation you already know how to handle.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_Qjf9MQ5dpbBQYzwG`)

**FRONT**

Why is it often useful to rewrite an expression entirely in $\mathrm{sin}$ and $\mathrm{cos}$?


**BACK**

Because every one of the six trigonometric functions can be written that way, so a mixture of them stops being a problem.

Once everything is in $\mathrm{sin}$ and $\mathrm{cos}$, the parts can be combined and cancelled like ordinary algebraic fractions.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_XwqY6FRPBDMtfMKV`)

**FRONT**

Where does a reciprocal trigonometric graph have its vertical asymptotes?


**BACK**

Wherever the **original function is zero**, since you cannot divide by zero.

So $\mathrm{sec} x$ has them where $\mathrm{cos} x = 0$, and $\text{cosec}  x$ where $\mathrm{sin} x = 0$.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_ST93mjqVyNfBWrzy`)

**FRONT**

What are the periods of $\mathrm{sec} x$, $\text{cosec}  x$ and $\mathrm{cot} x$?


**BACK**

$\mathrm{sec} x$ and $\text{cosec}  x$ both repeat every $360 ^{\circ}$, or $2 π$ radians.

$\mathrm{cot} x$ repeats every $180 ^{\circ}$, or $π$ radians, just as $\mathrm{tan} x$ does.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_cdBrP2cKz3MCMDVp`)

**FRONT**

How do you sketch a reciprocal trigonometric graph?


**BACK**

Sketch the original function first, then take the reciprocal of every value on it.

Where the original is large the reciprocal is close to zero, and where the original reaches $\pm 1$ the two graphs touch.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_6DFthXpkd2BVsCSV`)

**FRONT**

How do you derive $\mathrm{tan}^{2} x + 1 \equiv \mathrm{sec}^{2} x$?


**BACK**

Divide every term of $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x + \mathrm{cos}^{2} x \equiv 1$ by $\mathrm{cos}^{2} x$.

That works because $\frac{\mathrm{sin}x}{\mathrm{cos}x} = \mathrm{tan} x$ and $\frac{1}{\mathrm{cos}x} = \mathrm{sec} x$.

**FRONT**

What do you divide $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x + \mathrm{cos}^{2} x \equiv 1$ by to reach the $\text{cosec}$ identity?

By $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x$.

That turns the first term into $1$, the second into $\mathrm{cot}^{2} x$ and the right-hand side into $\text{cosec}^{2} x$.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_zkc7TNdb7vXcGkFH`)

**FRONT**

When are the reciprocal trigonometric identities needed?


**BACK**

When an expression mixes $\mathrm{sec}$, $\text{cosec}$ or $\mathrm{cot}$ with $\mathrm{tan}$, or with each other.

Substituting one of them removes a squared reciprocal term, which often collapses the whole expression.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_pdmmzhJvc6773bHS`)

**FRONT**

Why must the domain of $\mathrm{sin} x$ be restricted before $\mathrm{arcsin} x$ can exist?


**BACK**

Because $\mathrm{sin} x$ is **many-to-one** over all real $x$, and only a one-to-one function has an inverse.

Restricting it to $- \frac{π}{2} \leq x \leq \frac{π}{2}$ makes it one-to-one while still producing every output from $- 1$ to $1$.

**FRONT**

What happens to the graph of $\mathrm{arctan} x$ for large values of $x$?


**BACK**

It never reaches them, because $\mathrm{tan} x$ never actually attains those angles.
