## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_p4Mz2P9cg3w8JtwH`)

**FRONT**

Define the **moment** of a force.


**BACK**

The **moment** of a force about a point is a measure of the force's ability to make a body **rotate** about that point.

That point is usually called the **pivot**. A force can turn a body **clockwise** or **anti-clockwise**.

## Card 2 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_ddrGGjcB6XSPMHsv`)

**FRONT**

Fill in the blanks to complete the rule for the moment of a force about a point:

moment $=$ magnitude of the force $\times$ the `\_\_\_\_\_\_` distance from the line of action of the force to the point

The unit of a moment is the `\_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

The completed rule is:

moment $=$ magnitude of the force $\times$ the **perpendicular** distance from the line of action of the force to the point

The unit of a moment is the **newton metre (N m)**.

The distance has to be measured **perpendicular** to the line of action, not along the force and not along the object.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_9Mdhvz4w2w6GkZ7T`)

**FRONT**

A force of magnitude $F$ acts at a point $d$ metres from a pivot, at an angle of $θ$ to the line joining that point to the pivot. What is the moment of the force about the pivot?


**BACK**

The moment is $F d \mathrm{sin} θ$.

The perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force is $d \mathrm{sin} θ$ rather than $d$, so the moment is the magnitude of the force multiplied by that distance.

When the force acts at right angles to the line joining the point to the pivot, $θ = 90 ^{\circ}$ and the moment is simply $F d$.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_GP3syvyK8psm97YX`)

**FRONT**

When is the moment of a force about a point zero, even though the force itself is not zero?


**BACK**

The moment of a force about a point is zero when the **line of action** of the force passes through that point.

The perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action is then zero. A force pointing straight at, or straight away from, a pivot cannot turn a body about it.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_Jj3XwWYXK8r3d3QW`)

**FRONT**

How can you tell whether a force turns a body clockwise or anti-clockwise about a pivot?


**BACK**

Picture the perpendicular line from the pivot to the force as the **radius of a circle** centred on the pivot.

The direction in which the force points then tells you which way round that circle the body would turn.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_DMM6nDxmTNSDFjQX`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the resultant moment of several forces acting on a body?


**BACK**

The resultant moment is found by first choosing one direction, clockwise or anti-clockwise, to count as **positive**.

Add together all the moments in that direction, then **subtract** all the moments in the opposite direction.

The **sign** of the answer tells you which way the body turns: positive means the direction you chose, negative means the opposite one.


Spec links: `spcpt_gwDNP8pgh2FJjrxv`


## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_KXXVtY4d4tnm4Sh7`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A resultant moment of $- 5$ N m means the body does not turn.


**BACK**

**False.**

A negative resultant moment means the body turns in the direction **opposite** to the one chosen as positive. Its size is $5$ N m, so it turns just as strongly as a resultant moment of $+ 5$ N m would.

It is a resultant moment of **zero** that means the body does not turn.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_xpdh3gZncWg5c3Mt`)

**FRONT**

Why do moments have to be considered for an object modelled as a rigid body, but not for one modelled as a particle?


**BACK**

All the forces on a particle act at the same single point, so a particle cannot **rotate** and only its motion in a straight line has to be considered.

A rigid body has size, so different forces act at different points on it, and that is what can turn it. A moment is the measure of exactly that turning effect.

## Card 9 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_Wd2q6367sgCJSGPs`)

**FRONT**

A rigid body is in equilibrium. Fill in the blanks:

The resultant force on it is `\_\_\_\_\_\_`.

The resultant moment about `\_\_\_\_\_\_` point is `\_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

The completed statements for a rigid body in equilibrium are:

The resultant force on it is **0 N**.

The resultant moment about **any** point is **0 N m**.

**Any** is the word that does the work here: you may take moments about whichever point makes the working easiest.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_mrZZsPrmNg7M93Gz`)

**FRONT**

For a body in equilibrium, how are the clockwise and anti-clockwise moments about a point related?


**BACK**

The sum of the sizes of the **clockwise** moments equals the sum of the sizes of the **anti-clockwise** moments.

This says the same thing as "the resultant moment is zero", written in the form that is usually easiest to turn into an equation.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_b4V3p6mjW6TGHhVD`)

**FRONT**

When you are forming a moment equation and one of the forces acting is unknown, how can you choose the pivot so that the unknown force does not appear?


**BACK**

Take moments about a point that lies **on the line of action** of the unknown force.

Its perpendicular distance from that point is then zero, so its moment about that point is zero and it drops out of the equation entirely.


Spec links: `spcpt_hZvhpjqm7TVXQPWM`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_sYQbBbT74mq8mq8K`)

**FRONT**

You take moments about a point for a body in equilibrium and the equation still contains two unknown forces. What can you do?


**BACK**

Take moments about a **different** point to get a second equation, then solve the two together as simultaneous equations.

Because the resultant moment is zero about **any** point, you are free to take moments about as many different points as you need.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

For a body in equilibrium, the resultant moment is zero about a pivot or a support, but not necessarily about other points.


**BACK**

**False.**

If a body is in equilibrium then the resultant moment about **any** point is zero, not only about a pivot or a support.

That is what lets you choose whichever point makes the algebra easiest, including a point where no force acts at all.

## Card 14 — keyword_definition (`fl_q4D8qNvTp9gC7Ms3`)

**FRONT**

Define the **centre of mass** of an object.


**BACK**

The **centre of mass** of an object is the point at which the **weight** of the object may be taken to act.

Treating the whole weight as a single force at this one point is what allows it to be used in a moment equation.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_Tnf5kZ8zkjcNVFXB`)

**FRONT**

Where is the centre of mass of a uniform rod, and where is it for a uniform rectangular lamina?


**BACK**

For a **uniform rod** the centre of mass is at the **midpoint** of the rod.

For a **uniform rectangular lamina** it is where the **diagonals** intersect, which is halfway along and halfway across.

In general, for a uniform object the centre of mass is where the lines of symmetry meet.


Spec links: `spcpt_n4CMFvkrTVx7QjVc`


## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_vMvc84cDBCZc7tV6`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can assume that the centre of mass of a non-uniform rod is at its midpoint.


**BACK**

**False.**

In a **non-uniform** object the mass is not spread evenly, so the centre of mass cannot be assumed to be at the centre. Its position has to be found, usually by forming a moment equation.

It is only for a **uniform** object that the weight may be placed at the centre without any working.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_39rBQZchMzMZkPTR`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the position of the centre of mass of a non-uniform rod that is resting in equilibrium?


**BACK**

Mark the weight of the rod acting at an unknown distance along it, call that distance $x$, and form a **moment equation** using the fact that the rod is in equilibrium.

Solving the equation gives $x$. The value of $x$ depends on the point it was measured from, so say what that point is when giving the answer.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_Wdqx3xYjdDWwD2P3`)

**FRONT**

A uniform rod rests on two supports that are not placed symmetrically about its centre. Are the two normal reactions equal?


**BACK**

No. The support **nearer the centre of mass** carries the larger reaction.

Taking moments about one support gives the reaction at the other, and the two come out equal only when the weight acts midway between them.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_p6HtZ5R2cfcgm9MQ`)

**FRONT**

A rod has its mass spread along its whole length. Why can its weight be drawn as a single downward force at one point?


**BACK**

Because the centre of mass is the point at which the whole weight may be taken to act.

Replacing the spread-out weight by one force there gives the same resultant force and the same moment about every point, so nothing is lost and the rod can be worked with as though it had a single weight.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_Q9WKbcNHF6cMdpDh`)

**FRONT**

What has to be true about the moments acting on a rod for it to tilt about a pivot?


**BACK**

The **resultant moment** about that pivot has to be **non-zero**.

While the resultant moment about the pivot is zero the rod stays as it is. As soon as it is not zero, the rod turns about that point.


Spec links: `spcpt_2XqMh2DGhXhDDWr2`


## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_ZGhKHP2pDPtgmnrf`)

**FRONT**

A rod resting on two supports is on the point of tilting about one of them. What does that tell you about the force at the other support?


**BACK**

The normal reaction at the support the rod is **not** tilting about is **zero**.

At the point of tilting the rod is just about to lift off that support, so it is no longer pressing on it. Setting that reaction to zero is what makes the problem solvable.


Spec links: `spcpt_2XqMh2DGhXhDDWr2`


## Card 22 — true_or_false (`fl_c6MqVpqzcPBCq5Nz`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A rod that is on the point of tilting is no longer in equilibrium.


**BACK**

**False.**

A rod on the point of tilting is still in **equilibrium**: it has not yet begun to turn, so the resultant force and the resultant moment are both still zero.

That is what allows the usual equations to be formed. What has changed is that the reaction at the support it is about to lift from is now zero.


Spec links: `spcpt_2XqMh2DGhXhDDWr2`


## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_73vyR2vwgggFmB5q`)

**FRONT**

In a tilting problem, why is the support that the rod is tilting about the most useful point to take moments about?


**BACK**

Because **both** of the unknown reactions disappear from the equation at once.

The reaction at the other support is zero, since the rod is about to lift off it.

The reaction at this support acts through the very point being taken moments about, so its moment is zero too, leaving only the weights and the distances.


Spec links: `spcpt_2XqMh2DGhXhDDWr2`


## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_PQfzcfWK54hp4qB8`)

**FRONT**

A person walks out along a plank that overhangs its support. What decides the furthest distance they can safely walk?


**BACK**

The furthest safe distance is the one at which the plank is on the **point of tilting**.

Walking further out increases the moment of the person's weight about the support, and the plank turns as soon as that moment is no longer balanced. The distance is found by setting the reaction at the other support to zero.


Spec links: `spcpt_2XqMh2DGhXhDDWr2`


## Card 25 — true_or_false (`fl_f83vcnr7M4CmGjq8`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A rod resting on two supports can be made to tilt by placing a heavy enough mass anywhere on it.


**BACK**

**False.**

A mass placed **between** the two supports can never tilt the rod, however heavy it is. Whichever support is considered, the mass lies on the same side as the other support and simply presses the rod down onto both.

The rod can only tilt if a mass is placed **beyond** one of the supports, on an overhanging part.
