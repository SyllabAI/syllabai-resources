## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_HvHCRHDWpTKKhRYr`)

**FRONT**

On a force diagram, what does each arrow show, and where is the size of the force written?


**BACK**

Each arrow shows a **force acting on the particle**, and the way the arrow points is the direction in which that force acts.

The **magnitude** of the force is written next to its arrow, in newtons.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_jvvsF3HFCzDJ8sCj`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A force diagram should show only the forces that the question mentions.


**BACK**

**False.**

A force diagram shows **every** force acting on the particle, whether or not the question names it.

Weight acts on anything with mass, a surface in contact always produces a normal reaction, and a rough surface produces friction. None of them has to be mentioned to be there.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_NX45hKY6ddpDdJCr`)

**FRONT**

Why are all the forces on a force diagram drawn acting at the same single point?


**BACK**

Forces are drawn at a single point because the object is **modelled as a particle**, which occupies a single point in space.

That is why the normal reaction on a block is drawn from the middle of the block rather than from the surface it is resting on.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_vyQY2SjhPJjrdyB9`)

**FRONT**

A force diagram also carries an arrow labelled $a \text{m s}^{-2}$. What does it show, and why must it be kept separate from the force arrows?


**BACK**

The arrow shows the **direction of the acceleration**, not a force.

An acceleration must not be included when the forces are added up, so it is drawn differently from the force arrows to keep the two apart.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_Zmc2CR77TXxKmMrT`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

For a block resting on a slope, the weight is drawn perpendicular to the slope.


**BACK**

**False.**

Weight always acts **vertically downwards**, whatever the surface underneath it is doing.

It is the **normal reaction** that is perpendicular to the slope. On a slope the two are therefore not in opposite directions, which is why they do not simply cancel.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_dzMK8QXkzNxY2dvc`)

**FRONT**

Define **resultant force**.


**BACK**

The **resultant force** is the sum of all the forces acting on a particle.

It is the single force that would have exactly the same effect as all of those forces combined.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_3x4WSvHrBvnqj8ZZ`)

**FRONT**

State Newton's first law of motion.


**BACK**

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object moving with constant velocity keeps moving with constant velocity, **unless an unbalanced force acts on it**.

An unbalanced force is one that is not cancelled by another force in the opposite direction, so it leaves a resultant force that is not zero, and the object accelerates.


Spec links: `spcpt_8FsF3KSbZ4GDtqcY`


## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_HnqNMvVKmNXnSX4d`)

**FRONT**

Define **equilibrium**, for a particle.


**BACK**

A particle is in **equilibrium** when the resultant force acting on it, the sum of all the forces, is **zero**.

Every force is then cancelled by others, so the particle does not accelerate.


Spec links: `spcpt_8FsF3KSbZ4GDtqcY`


## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_v9JhBTb8hCpxPRzD`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Forces on a particle can only balance if the same number of forces act in each direction.


**BACK**

**False.**

What matters is the **total** force in each direction, not how many forces there are.

For example, forces of $7 \text{N}$ and $5 \text{N}$ acting to the right are balanced by a single force of $12 \text{N}$ acting to the left.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_5Xpv2NSWZttrwCMF`)

**FRONT**

The resultant force on a particle is zero. What can you say about how it is moving?


**BACK**

The particle is either **at rest** or **moving with constant velocity**.

A zero resultant force does not mean the particle is stationary. It means the particle is not accelerating, so whatever it was doing, it carries on doing.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_SqtbxsYg693pCbMz`)

**FRONT**

Several forces acting on a particle in equilibrium are drawn one after another, nose to tail. What shape do they form, and why?


**BACK**

The forces form a **closed polygon**.

Drawing them nose to tail takes you from the start of the first arrow to the end of the last, and that overall journey represents the resultant. In equilibrium the resultant is zero, so the path has to finish where it started.

## Card 15 — true_or_false (`fl_9KPwNnbYqW55F4Bc`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The two perpendicular directions used in a two-dimensional problem must be horizontal and vertical.


**BACK**

**False.**

Any two perpendicular directions can be used, and they are chosen to make the problem easiest.

Horizontal and vertical are the usual choice, but for an object on a slope it is normally better to work **parallel** and **perpendicular** to the slope.

**FRONT**

Before writing an equation for the forces acting in one direction, what must you decide, and how do you record it?


**BACK**

You must decide **which direction you are taking as positive**, and show that choice beside the equation.

An arrow such as $( \rightarrow )$ or $( \uparrow )$ next to the working does it, and every force in that equation then takes a sign to match.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_xmBqSHqHYPJmYsB3`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the magnitude and direction of the force $F = x i + y j$ N?


**BACK**

The **magnitude** `\left| \mathbf{F} \right|` comes from Pythagoras' theorem applied to the two components.

The **direction** needs a diagram and trigonometry, because the components on their own do not show which way round the force points.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_RW9WFVNRK8zQZcWS`)

**FRONT**

A force is `\left( -8\mathbf{i} - 6\mathbf{j} \right)` N, so both of its components are negative. How do you find its direction?


**BACK**

Use $\mathrm{tan} θ ^{\circ} = \frac{y}{x}$ with the **sizes** of the components, ignoring the minus signs, which gives `\tan^{-1}\left( \frac{3}{4} \right) = 36.9^{\circ}`.

Then draw a diagram to see where the force actually points and adjust accordingly: this one points down and to the left, so its direction is $180 ^{\circ} + 36 . 9 ^{\circ} = 216 . 9 ^{\circ}$, measured anticlockwise from the positive horizontal.

## Card 21 — keyword_definition (`fl_m7mJhkXcFBS2QyY7`)

**FRONT**

Define the **zero vector**.


**BACK**

The **zero vector** is the vector both of whose components are zero.

For forces in two dimensions it is written `\left( 0\mathbf{i} + 0\mathbf{j} \right)` N, or simply as a bold $0$ N.

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_gYfrTwTgBNsWsydK`)

**FRONT**

Two forces act on a particle. How do you find the third force that would bring it into equilibrium?


**BACK**

Set the three forces added together equal to the zero vector, then equate the $i$ components to zero and the $j$ components to zero, which gives one equation for each unknown.

So if the first two have resultant `\left( -2\mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j} \right)` N, then $- 2 + x = 0$ and $- 3 + y = 0$, making the third force `\left( 2\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} \right)` N.


Spec links: `spcpt_Wdvfcz3xhySsxV57`


## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_fr3F3MmRhT24fGGX`)

**FRONT**

How is the magnitude of a force $F$ N written, and how does that notation differ from the force itself?


**BACK**

The magnitude is written `\left| \mathbf{F} \right|` N, or as an italic $F$ N.

The force itself is a **bold** upright $F$, so the bold letter is the vector and the italic letter is only its size.
