## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_h8kg4hRRxrBcPKbc`)

**FRONT**

Which of the five constant-acceleration formulae cannot be written in vector form, and what do you do instead?


**BACK**

The formula that cannot be written in vector form is $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2 a s$, because squaring a vector has no meaning here.

Instead, apply it to each direction **separately**: one ordinary equation in numbers using only the $i$ components, and one using only the $j$ components.

The other four formulae do work in vector form, because they only add vectors and multiply them by the scalar $t$.

## Card 2 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_NT4GMBQdzF5FfFCZ`)

**FRONT**

In two dimensions the constant-acceleration formulae are written with vectors. Fill in the blanks:

`\mathbf{s} = \_\_\_\_\_\_ t + \frac{1}{2} \_\_\_\_\_\_ t^{2}`


**BACK**

The completed formula is:

$s = u t + \frac{1}{2} a t^{2}$

The displacement, the initial velocity and the acceleration are all **vectors**, which is why they are written in bold. Time is a **scalar** and is not.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_cWWGMwSCnrbQZ68w`)

**FRONT**

In a two-dimensional constant-acceleration problem, what are the two ways of setting up the working?


**BACK**

Either work with **one vector equation**, keeping the vectors in component form all the way through, or **split it into two separate equations**, one for the $i$ components and one for the $j$ components, and solve those independently.

Both are valid because a vector equation holds exactly when it holds in each direction separately.

Time is a scalar, so it appears unchanged in either approach.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_yN3VXWPwCNsPJcZF`)

**FRONT**

A question says a particle is travelling **parallel to** the vector $i$. What does that tell you about its velocity?


**BACK**

Its velocity is a **scalar multiple** of $i$, so the $j$ component of the velocity is **zero**.

More generally, travelling parallel to a given vector means the velocity is that vector multiplied by some number. That is enough to write the whole velocity in terms of a single unknown, even when no components have been given.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_ztyhK6m3Pbddhz5n`)

**FRONT**

Why can "find the acceleration" be ambiguous in two dimensions, when "find the velocity" is not?


**BACK**

Velocity has a separate word for its magnitude, **speed**, and displacement has one too, **distance**, but acceleration has no such word: the same word is used for the vector and for its magnitude.

So "find the acceleration" may want the vector, in component form, or its magnitude, which is a single number.

The wording decides it: an answer asked for "in the form `\left(a \mathbf{i} + b \mathbf{j}\right)`" is the vector.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_BMWXZWX8gPPs2HYK`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can assume that the answer to a two-dimensional constant-acceleration question is a vector.


**BACK**

**False.**

It depends on what has been asked for. **Speed** and **distance** are scalars, so those answers are single numbers, found by taking the **magnitude** of the relevant vector.

A question asking for the distance of a particle from its starting point wants the magnitude of the displacement, not the displacement itself.
