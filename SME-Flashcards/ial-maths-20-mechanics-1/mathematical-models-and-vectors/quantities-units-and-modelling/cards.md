## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_gCWDYnvRJ2kFMwvv`)

**FRONT**

Define a **scalar** quantity.


**BACK**

A **scalar** is a quantity that has a **size (magnitude)** only, with no direction attached to it.

Distance, speed, time and mass are the scalars you meet in mechanics, and none of them can be negative.

## Card 2 — keyword_definition (`fl_p6PK2xYrDMkzJT2H`)

**FRONT**

Define a **vector** quantity.


**BACK**

A **vector** is a quantity that has a **direction** as well as a size (magnitude).

Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight and momentum are all vectors, and their components can be positive or negative.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_BFyymXFjnDsCk9PC`)

**FRONT**

A ball rolls **forwards** 60 cm before stopping. Is that a scalar or a vector quantity, and why?


**BACK**

It is a **vector**.

The number 60 cm on its own would be a **distance**, which is a scalar. The word **forwards** attaches a direction to it, and a quantity with both a size and a direction is a vector.

## Card 5 — true_or_false (`fl_72kyybDS84zKKNQR`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Two objects with velocities of $7 \text{m s}^{-1}$ and $- 7 \text{m s}^{-1}$ are travelling at the same speed.


**BACK**

**True.**

Velocity is a **vector**, so the minus sign records only the **direction** of travel: the two objects are moving in opposite directions.

Speed is the scalar that goes with velocity, so it keeps the size and drops the direction, and both objects have a speed of $7 \text{m s}^{-1}$.

**FRONT**

How are vector quantities distinguished from scalars in printed text, and how should you distinguish them in handwriting?


**BACK**

In print a vector is set in **bold**, non-italic type, as in $F$ or $a$.

You cannot write in bold, so in handwriting a vector is **underlined** instead.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_Jd2R4KfhVBQk28PR`)

**FRONT**

Define **fundamental (S.I.) units**.


**BACK**

**Fundamental units**, or **S.I. units**, are the internationally standardised units used to measure the basic quantities.

The three needed in mechanics are the **metre** for length, the **second** for time and the **kilogram** for mass, and every other unit is built from them.


Spec links: `spcpt_t9ZzPgXFZvYfvYT6`


## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_6BNWvJZmsrsnrbCt`)

**FRONT**

Convert 27.8 km and 3.8 hours into S.I. units.


**BACK**

Multiply each by the number of smaller units contained in one larger one:

$27 . 8 \times 1000 = 27800 \text{m}$

$3 . 8 \times 3600 = 13680 \text{s}$

There are 1000 metres in a kilometre, and $60 \times 60 = 3600$ seconds in an hour.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_9x2Ym3pR3gYsDFCz`)

**FRONT**

Why must every quantity be converted into S.I. units before it is substituted into a mechanics formula?


**BACK**

The mechanics formulae relate **metres**, **seconds** and **kilograms** to one another, so they are only correct when every quantity is measured in those units.

Substituting a distance in kilometres alongside a time in seconds gives an answer that is in no consistent unit at all, and so means nothing.


Spec links: `spcpt_t9ZzPgXFZvYfvYT6`


## Card 12 — keyword_definition (`fl_wy8TNxX7FVMtpKmM`)

**FRONT**

Define a **derived unit**.


**BACK**

A **derived unit** is a unit made by combining the fundamental S.I. units of length, time and mass.

A density measured in $\text{kg m}^{-3}$ is one example, since it combines the kilogram with the metre.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_3Pr9kVK24mcrBwNh`)

**FRONT**

Force $=$ mass $\times$ acceleration. What does that make the derived S.I. unit of force, and what is that unit normally called?


**BACK**

A mass in **kilograms** multiplied by an acceleration in $\text{m s}^{-2}$ gives a unit of $\text{kg m s}^{-2}$.

That combination is normally called the **newton**, written **N**, so $1 \text{N} = 1 \text{kg m s}^{-2}$.

**FRONT**

Convert $72 \text{km h}^{-2}$ into S.I. units.


**BACK**

Convert the kilometres and the hours separately, remembering that the hours are **squared**:

$72 \times 1000 \div 3600^{2} = 0 . 00556 \text{m s}^{-2}$

Because the unit is $\text{h}^{-2}$ rather than $\text{h}^{-1}$, the conversion factor for hours has to be squared as well.

## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_rXkfdXV5YtcJhnWt`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Speed and velocity are measured in the same derived unit.


**BACK**

**True.**

A direction is not something a unit can record, so attaching one to a speed leaves the unit unchanged: both are measured in $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

What tells you which of the two you are looking at is the sign or the vector notation, never the unit.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_dKXZjqqvSsnFKbgR`)

**FRONT**

When converting $14 \text{g cm}^{-3}$ into $\text{kg m}^{-3}$, why do you **multiply** by $100^{3}$ rather than divide?


**BACK**

Because the centimetres are in the **denominator**, so making the length unit larger makes the number larger.

There are $100^{3}$ cubic centimetres in a cubic metre, so a quantity measured *per cubic centimetre* becomes $100^{3}$ times as much *per cubic metre*:

$14 \div 1000 \times 100^{3} = 14000 \text{kg m}^{-3}$

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_NXndPKTfpZ4dBcpj`)

**FRONT**

A cyclist takes 15 minutes to travel 2.54 km. Why is it worth converting both values into S.I. units before dividing?


**BACK**

Dividing kilometres by minutes would give an answer in $\text{km min}^{-1}$, which is not an S.I. unit, so it would have to be converted afterwards anyway.

Converting first gives 2540 m and 900 s, and the division then lands straight in the right unit:

$\frac{2540}{900} = 2 . 82 \text{m s}^{-1}$

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_JvhGCttsDc4wYYpz`)

**FRONT**

Tension and thrust are both labelled $T$ N. What is the difference between them?


**BACK**

**Tension** is a **pulling** force, so it acts **away** from the object it is attached to, along the string or rod.

**Thrust**, also called compression, is a **pushing** force, so it acts **towards** the object.

## Card 21 — keyword_definition (`fl_Xbk8pNqKVHbJRkZ9`)

**FRONT**

Define the **weight** of an object.


**BACK**

An object's **weight** is the force on it produced by its mass and gravity, and it always acts **vertically downwards**.

It is found from $W = m g$ and, being a force, is measured in **newtons**.


Spec links: `spcpt_XMcYznXv3R7vqWcX`


## Card 22 — true_or_false (`fl_fjXvF59TxYq8rc48`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Friction acts on an object in the direction in which it is moving.


**BACK**

**False.**

Friction is a **resistive** force: it always acts to **oppose** the motion, so it points in the opposite direction to the way the object is moving.

Air resistance behaves the same way, which is why a falling object meets a resistive force acting upwards.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_rtZS8dgXkj9Zc2Sn`)

**FRONT**

An astronaut takes a 12 kg toolbox to the Moon. Which of its mass and its weight changes, and why?


**BACK**

Its **mass** does not change.

Mass is a scalar measure of the amount of matter in an object, in kilograms, and it is the same everywhere in the universe.

Its **weight** does change, because weight depends on $g$, the acceleration due to gravity, and $g$ is smaller on the Moon than on Earth.

## Card 24 — keyword_definition (`fl_fydnnrFhdgk7CfXW`)

**FRONT**

In mechanics, define a **particle**.


**BACK**

A **particle** is an object modelled as having **negligible dimensions**, so that it occupies a single point in space.

Because it has no size, **air resistance cannot act on it** and can be left out of the model.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_GNqVs4srh4PCSR9n`)

**FRONT**

A string is modelled as **light**. What does that mean, and what does it let you assume about the tension?


**BACK**

**Light** means the string is modelled as having **zero mass**.

There is then no weight anywhere along it to be supported, so the **tension is the same at every point** of the string.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_b2SvTKhJMcTVzCJg`)

**FRONT**

Two particles are joined by an **inextensible** string. What does inextensible mean, and what does it let you assume about the two particles?


**BACK**

**Inextensible** means the string cannot be **stretched**, so its length is fixed.

Whatever distance one particle moves, the other must move the same distance, so at every moment the two have the **same speed** and **accelerations of equal magnitude**.

## Card 28 — question_and_answer (`fl_CpCsN74gBF7gm3SG`)

**FRONT**

A pulley is modelled as **light and smooth**. What does the **smooth** part let you assume about the tension?


**BACK**

That the **tension is the same on both sides** of the pulley.

A smooth pulley exerts no friction on the string passing over it, so nothing acts to make the tension on one side differ from the tension on the other.

## Card 29 — question_and_answer (`fl_8VmPCtJ4znTBcHMh`)

**FRONT**

What does it mean to model a rod as **uniform**, and what does that let you assume about its weight?


**BACK**

**Uniform** means the rod's mass is distributed **evenly** along its length.

Its whole weight can therefore be taken to act at the **midpoint** of the rod, rather than being spread along it.

## Card 30 — question_and_answer (`fl_qSZTzRctyKMBWd8J`)

**FRONT**

A rod is modelled as **rigid**. What does that let you assume about it?


**BACK**

That it does not **bend** or change shape under the forces acting on it.

Its length stays fixed and the points at which the forces act stay in the same places, which is what makes it possible to work with distances measured along it.
