## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_RzvYjs8vNh6Qk73b`)

**FRONT**

Define **scalar**.


**BACK**

A **scalar** is a quantity that has only a **size (magnitude)** and no direction.

Distance, speed, mass and time are all scalar quantities.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_JVtKN25Zft9Zd46P`)

**FRONT**

Two cars are travelling along a straight road, with one having a velocity of $7 \text{m s}^{-1}$ and the other having a velocity of $- 7 \text{m s}^{-1}$. What does the difference in sign tell you about their motion?


**BACK**

The two cars are travelling at the **same speed** but in **opposite directions**.

Velocity is a vector, so the sign records the direction of travel. Which direction counts as positive is a choice made at the start of a problem.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A speed can be negative if an object is moving backwards.


**BACK**

**False.**

Speed is a **scalar**, so it has a size but no direction, and it cannot be negative.

It is the **velocity** that is negative when an object moves in the direction chosen as negative. Its speed is the size of that velocity.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_Q59TKFsxjNmJKY44`)

**FRONT**

Define **fundamental units**.


**BACK**

**Fundamental units**, also called **S.I. units**, are the internationally agreed standard units of measurement.

The three needed in mechanics are the **metre (m)** for length, the **second (s)** for time and the **kilogram (kg)** for mass.

Every other unit in mechanics is built from these.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_tTT7q39hKNHP2qMJ`)

**FRONT**

A time is given as 17 minutes and 42 seconds. Convert it into S.I. units.


**BACK**

The time is **1062 seconds**.

Each minute is 60 seconds, so $17 \times 60 = 1020$ seconds, and $1020 + 42 = 1062$ seconds.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_hNSH2t7CHNfJvYpS`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

To convert a time given in hours into seconds, you multiply by 60.


**BACK**

**False.**

Multiplying by 60 converts hours into **minutes**. Hours are converted into seconds by multiplying by 60 twice, which is multiplying by 3600.

For example, $3 . 8$ hours is $3 . 8 \times 3600 = 13680$ seconds.

## Card 12 — keyword_definition (`fl_PCzwyyzyc7TZjrQz`)

**FRONT**

Define **derived unit**.


**BACK**

A **derived unit** is a unit built by combining the fundamental units of length, time and mass.

For example, a velocity is a distance divided by a time, so it is measured in $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_5wR7d92Rf2tRfw7k`)

**FRONT**

When converting an acceleration from $\text{km h}^{-2}$ into $\text{m s}^{-2}$, why do you divide by $3600^{2}$ rather than by $3600$?


**BACK**

The unit of time is **squared** in $\text{km h}^{-2}$, so the conversion factor for time has to be squared as well.

One hour is 3600 seconds, so $1 \text{km h}^{-2} = 1000 \div 3600^{2} \text{m s}^{-2}$.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_WRR6R5SpJhcCSmbn`)

**FRONT**

For a density is given as $14 \text{g cm}^{-3}$, converting it into $\text{kg m}^{-3}$ means dividing by 1000 for the mass, but **multiplying** by $100^{3}$ for the volume. Why multiply?


**BACK**

In $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ the centimetres are in the **denominator**, so that part of the conversion works the opposite way round.

One metre is 100 cm, so one cubic metre holds $100^{3}$ cubic centimetres, and a quantity given per cubic centimetre is $100^{3}$ times as much per cubic metre.

Here $14 \div 1000 \times 100^{3} = 14000 \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_Tsrm6HbWMVxq9dFT`)

**FRONT**

Speed is measured in $\text{m s}^{-1}$. What does that unit tell you about the formula for speed?


**BACK**

The unit $\text{m s}^{-1}$ means metres divided by seconds, which is a distance divided by a time, so

$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}$

A forgotten formula can often be rebuilt this way, from the units the quantity is measured in.

## Card 19 — keyword_definition (`fl_72fjCNW6XCYrgWXw`)

**FRONT**

Define **weight**.


**BACK**

**Weight** is the force on an object caused by gravity acting on its mass, and it always acts vertically downwards.

For an object of mass $m$ kilograms the weight is $W = m g$ newtons, where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity.

## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_XPK3qVCMDqXjjgQt`)

**FRONT**

A block rests on a sloping surface. In which direction does the normal reaction from the slope act on the block?


**BACK**

The normal reaction acts **perpendicular to the surface**, pushing the block away from the slope.

It is therefore not vertical: every surface produces a reaction at right angles to itself, so tilting the surface tilts the reaction with it.

The normal reaction is usually labelled $R$ or $N$.


Spec links: `spcpt_5z55DhmSTdcR8BY8`


## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_yYSPW9QV85Z4W34Q`)

**FRONT**

Why does the direction of friction depend on which way the object is moving?


**BACK**

Because friction always acts to **oppose** motion.

It points in the opposite direction to the way the object is travelling, so if the object reverses, the friction reverses with it.

## Card 23 — true_or_false (`fl_BfnGpt7pJHjMmsWQ`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

An object taken from the Earth to the Moon keeps the same mass, but its weight changes.


**BACK**

**True.**

**Mass** measures the amount of matter in an object, and it is the same everywhere in the universe.

**Weight** depends on gravity as well as on mass, and gravity is weaker on the Moon, so the same object weighs less there.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_BVNjzBP8dbMrzVVC`)

**FRONT**

A book lies at rest on a horizontal table. Its weight and the normal reaction from the table are equal in size, so are they the same force?


**BACK**

No, they are two different forces with different sources: the weight is the pull of the **Earth** on the book, while the normal reaction is the push of the **table** on the book.

Being equal in size does not make two forces the same force, and here they are equal only because the book is at rest on a horizontal table.

## Card 26 — question_and_answer (`fl_MsFWtDfg9fnrQ3Hc`)

**FRONT**

Why are simplifying assumptions made when a real-life situation is turned into a mechanics problem?


**BACK**

Real situations are far too complicated to describe exactly, so assumptions strip out the details that matter least and leave something that can be written as equations or drawn as a graph.

The price is that the answer is only ever as good as the assumptions behind it.

## Card 27 — keyword_definition (`fl_yQjrHbKF2wb4m6y7`)

**FRONT**

Define **particle**.


**BACK**

A **particle** is an object modelled as having negligible dimensions, so that it occupies a single point in space.

Every force acting on it can then be taken to act at that same point, which is what makes a force diagram simple enough to work with.


Spec links: `spcpt_Nx33RFmnZdfbkQ9M`


## Card 28 — question_and_answer (`fl_vW273vBJ8ncJBVbv`)

**FRONT**

A light string passes over a smooth pulley. What does each of those two assumptions contribute to the tension being the same throughout the string?


**BACK**

**Light** means the string has zero mass, so none of the tension is used up carrying the string's own weight and the tension does not change along its length.

**Smooth** means nothing at the pulley resists the string sliding over it, so the tension is carried across the pulley unchanged.

## Card 30 — question_and_answer (`fl_4M69sBXTHh4M8gkv`)

**FRONT**

Two blocks are joined by a string modelled as **inextensible**. What does that tell you about their accelerations?


**BACK**

The two blocks have the **same acceleration**.

An inextensible string cannot stretch, so while it stays taut the blocks cannot move closer together or further apart, and they must speed up and slow down together.

## Card 31 — true_or_false (`fl_GXdCc8YmH2PJgtyh`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Describing an object as **uniform** means its whole mass is concentrated at a single point.


**BACK**

**False.**

**Uniform** means the object's mass is spread **evenly** throughout it, so no part of it is heavier than another part of the same size.

A uniform rod, for example, has the same mass in every centimetre of its length.

## Card 32 — question_and_answer (`fl_BYcqcMJ3pdrvDrpB`)

**FRONT**

A moving object is modelled as a particle, so it has negligible dimensions. Why does that also let you ignore air resistance?


**BACK**

Because an object with no size presents no surface for the air to push against, so there is no air resistance force to include.

This is an assumption rather than a fact: real objects do meet air resistance, and leaving it out is part of what makes the problem solvable.

## Card 33 — question_and_answer (`fl_2SFZNP4Nr4yJYfqf`)

**FRONT**

A thrown ball is modelled by $y = - 0 . 5 x^{2} + 1 . 8 x + 1 . 5$, where $y$ is the height in metres and $x$ is the horizontal distance in metres. What does the $1 . 5$ represent?


**BACK**

It is the height at which the ball leaves the thrower's hand.

Putting $x = 0 \text{m}$ makes the other two terms vanish and leaves $y = 1 . 5 \text{m}$, which is the height of the ball at the moment it is released.

## Card 34 — question_and_answer (`fl_GSBW3FQpg7y5PZDS`)

**FRONT**

A thrown ball is modelled by a curve, but the model is used only for $0 \leq x \leq 4$, where $x$ is the horizontal distance in metres. What does that restriction represent?


**BACK**

The ball is thrown at $x = 0 \text{m}$ and caught at $x = 4 \text{m}$, so those are the only distances over which it is actually in flight.

Outside that interval the curve still exists mathematically, but it no longer describes anything that happens.
