## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_MDkfjjhymd4yGGvD`)

**FRONT**

How do you differentiate or integrate a vector in kinematics?


**BACK**

Work on **each component separately**.

To differentiate, differentiate the $i$ component and the $j$ component; to integrate, integrate each of them.

The links between displacement, velocity and acceleration are the same as in one dimension, and only the displacement changes name: in two dimensions it is usually written $r$ rather than $s$.

## Card 2 — true_or_false (`fl_bWbNHH6pKQmFnfXh`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can assume that the constant of integration in a two-dimensional kinematics problem is a single number.


**BACK**

**False.**

Integrating a vector gives a **vector** constant of integration, with an $i$ component and a $j$ component.

Find it by substituting a known vector and then equating the $i$ components and the $j$ components separately, which gives one equation for each part of the constant.

So, for example, integrating a velocity might give $r = t^{2} i + 2 \text{e}^{0.5t} j + c$, and a starting position of `\left(3\mathbf{i} - 4\mathbf{j}\right)\text{ m}` then gives `\mathbf{c} = \left(3\mathbf{i} - 6\mathbf{j}\right)\text{ m}`.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_nDbBzmQgTbvRyM2H`)

**FRONT**

A particle's position vector is $r$ and its displacement from where it started is $s$. How are the two related, and why are they not the same thing?


**BACK**

They are related by $r = r_{0} + s$, where $r_{0}$ is the particle's **initial position** vector.

They differ because $s$ is measured from **wherever the particle started**, while $r$ is measured from the **origin**, and a particle need not start at the origin.

When you integrate a velocity to find the position, $r_{0}$ is exactly what the constant of integration turns out to be.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_m8MQByjpQxZ7SdDf`)

**FRONT**

A particle's velocity is `\left(3\mathbf{i} - 4\mathbf{j}\right)\text{ m s}^{-1}`. What is its speed?


**BACK**

Its speed is $5  \text{m s}^{-1}$.

Speed is the **magnitude** of the velocity, so it comes from Pythagoras' theorem on the two components:

`\left|\mathbf{v}\right| = \sqrt{3^{2} + \left(- 4\right)^{2}} = 5 \textrm{ }\text{m s}^{- 1}`

The same move applied to a displacement gives the **distance** from the starting point; both are single numbers and neither can be negative.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_mWfN3W5JFjMyxMz4`)

**FRONT**

A question asks which direction a particle is moving in. Which vector do you use, and why not the position vector?


**BACK**

Use the **velocity**: the position vector says where the particle **is**, measured from the origin.

The velocity says where it is **heading**, and the two need not point the same way: a particle can be north-east of the origin while travelling due south.

The direction of the velocity may then be wanted as an angle, as a bearing, or as a vector that the velocity is parallel to.
