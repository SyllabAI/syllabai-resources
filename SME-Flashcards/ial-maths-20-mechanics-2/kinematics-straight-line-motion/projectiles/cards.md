## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Nx2J9Z32F2YGBpBY`)

**FRONT**

Define a **projectile**.


**BACK**

A **projectile** is a particle moving freely under gravity in a two-dimensional plane.

A thrown ball, a javelin and an arrow are all modelled this way.

## Card 2 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_8ty9N5MYnxT85Mb5`)

**FRONT**

A projectile is launched with initial speed $U$ at an angle $θ$ to the horizontal. Fill in the blanks to complete the components of its initial velocity:

horizontal: `u_{x} = U \_\_\_\_\_\_`

vertical: `u_{y} = U \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed components are:

horizontal: $u_{x} = U \mathrm{cos} θ$

The **cosine** goes with the horizontal component because that component is the side **adjacent** to the angle in the right-angled triangle, with $U$ as the hypotenuse.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_6jtkCmP3f9f2RH7S`)

**FRONT**

How is the acceleration of a projectile written as a single two-dimensional vector?


**BACK**

Taking upwards as positive, it is `\left( 0\mathbf{i} - g\mathbf{j} \right)\text{ m s}^{-2}`.

Writing it this way makes the point of the whole topic explicit: only one of the two components is non-zero, so only the vertical motion is ever accelerated.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_Y2vVpZrqj38gFVSF`)

**FRONT**

A question gives a projectile's initial velocity as a vector rather than as a speed and an angle. How do you recover the speed and the angle of projection?


**BACK**

The initial **speed** is the **magnitude** of that vector, found from Pythagoras' theorem on its two components.

The **angle of projection** comes from a sketch and trigonometry, using the same two components as the sides of a right-angled triangle.

## Card 5 — true_or_false (`fl_C9JkM7dFJ6gsvpjM`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A projectile launched below the horizontal has a negative angle of projection.


**BACK**

**True.**

The angle is measured from the horizontal, so projecting downwards rather than upwards makes $θ$ negative.

That is exactly what is wanted, because it makes the vertical component of the initial velocity negative, and the projectile does start out moving downwards.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_QqgWy76Sm4v9cGrJ`)

**FRONT**

A projectile is launched at $25 ^{\circ}$ to the horizontal with speed $5 \text{m s}^{-1}$. Find its initial velocity as a vector.


**BACK**

Resolve the speed into its two components:

`\mathbf{u} = \left( 5\cos 25^{\circ}\mathbf{i} + 5\sin 25^{\circ}\mathbf{j} \right) = \left( 4.53\mathbf{i} + 2.11\mathbf{j} \right)\text{ m s}^{-1}`

Both components are positive, which says the projectile sets off upwards and to the right.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_rnFk9tXfkQ6PNNQN`)

**FRONT**

Which modelling assumptions about a projectile make its horizontal acceleration zero?


**BACK**

That there is **no air resistance** and no other horizontal force acting on it, and that its **spin and rotation** are ignored.

With nothing acting horizontally there is nothing to change the horizontal velocity, so it stays constant for the whole flight.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_QCnKHGz4wndm4H5g`)

**FRONT**

How are the constant-acceleration formulae applied to a projectile?


**BACK**

Apply them to the **horizontal and vertical directions separately**, as two one-dimensional problems.

The acceleration is different in each: it is **zero** horizontally, which reduces the horizontal formula to $s = u t$, and it is $- g$ vertically, taking upwards as positive.

Because each direction is treated on its own, $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2 a s$ can be used here, even though it has no vector form.


Spec links: `spcpt_6vPzRxtjQy8Kn7yf`


## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_wdvw3KbMPkKvPK2K`)

**FRONT**

What single quantity links the horizontal and vertical parts of a projectile problem?


**BACK**

The **time**, $t$.

The two directions are otherwise independent, but the projectile is in flight for one interval of time, so the same $t$ appears in both sets of equations.

That is what makes the problems solvable: a time found from the vertical motion can be substituted into the horizontal motion, and the other way round.


Spec links: `spcpt_6vPzRxtjQy8Kn7yf`


## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_4pwf2BMPw86Zj7hm`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can assume that a projectile is momentarily at rest at its highest point.


**BACK**

**False.**

Only the **vertical** component of the velocity is zero there; the **horizontal** component is unchanged, because there is no horizontal acceleration.

So at the highest point the projectile is still moving horizontally, with speed $U \mathrm{cos} θ$, which is the **smallest** speed it has at any point in its flight and is never zero.

An object thrown straight up is the special case that does come to rest, because it has no horizontal component to begin with.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_GfpqyxZBrbVhGXXs`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the greatest height a projectile reaches?


**BACK**

Use the fact that the **vertical** component of the velocity is **zero** at that point, and apply a vertical constant-acceleration formula.

With $v = 0$, $u = U \mathrm{sin} θ$ and $a = - g$, the formula $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2 a s$ gives the vertical displacement directly, without needing the time.

If the projectile was launched from above ground level, that displacement is measured **from the launch point**, so the launch height has to be added to get the height above the ground.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_gQWkr2bX2NW59mGC`)

**FRONT**

A stone is thrown from the top of a cliff rather than from ground level. Which of the two directions does the cliff height affect, and which does it not?


**BACK**

It affects the **vertical** direction only.

The horizontal motion is unchanged, because the horizontal acceleration is zero wherever the stone started from, so the horizontal equation is the same as it would be on level ground.

The cliff enters the problem only through the **vertical displacement** at the moment of landing, which is measured from the point of projection rather than from the ground.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_tysWtNB3fNrPtGws`)

**FRONT**

A projectile is launched and lands at the **same height**. What does the symmetry of its path let you say about the highest point?


**BACK**

The path is symmetrical about the highest point, so the projectile reaches it exactly **halfway** through the motion.

The time to the highest point is therefore **half** the total time of flight, and the horizontal distance to it is **half** the range.

Both of these depend on the launch and landing heights being equal: thrown from a cliff, the path is no longer symmetrical and neither shortcut holds.


Spec links: `spcpt_6vPzRxtjQy8Kn7yf`


## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_dqHC99BnVRs8zdXW`)

**FRONT**

The constant-acceleration formulae give the horizontal and vertical displacements of a projectile separately. How is the **equation of its trajectory** different?


**BACK**

The two suvat results are **parametric** equations: they give $x$ and $y$ each in terms of the **time**, which acts as the parameter.

The equation of the trajectory is a **Cartesian** equation, linking $x$ and $y$ to each other directly, in the form `y = \text{f} \left(x\right)`, with no time in it at all.

It is obtained by **eliminating the parameter** between the two, exactly as for any pair of parametric equations.


Spec links: `spcpt_scnXTjK798w4kzCC`


## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_QPwd9RqgZtzGP4Qw`)

**FRONT**

When deriving the equation of a trajectory, which of the two displacement equations do you rearrange, and why that one?


**BACK**

Rearrange the **horizontal** equation.

Because there is no horizontal acceleration it is simply `x = \left(U \cos \theta\right) t`, which rearranges to $t = \frac{x}{U\mathrm{cos}θ}$ in one step.

The vertical equation contains $t^{2}$ as well as $t$, so making $t$ its subject would mean solving a quadratic; substituting the horizontal result into the vertical one avoids that entirely.


Spec links: `spcpt_scnXTjK798w4kzCC`


## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_FsFdftgJzyjyd2cP`)

**FRONT**

How does the equation of a trajectory show that the path of a projectile is a **parabola**?


**BACK**

The equation is $y = x \mathrm{tan} θ - \frac{gx^{2}}{2U^{2}\mathrm{cos}^{2}θ}$.

For a fixed launch speed and angle, everything except $x$ and $y$ is constant, so this is a quadratic in $x$, and the graph of a quadratic is a parabola.

The coefficient of $x^{2}$ is **negative**, since $g$, $U^{2}$ and $\mathrm{cos}^{2} θ$ are all positive, so the parabola opens **downwards**, which is why the path has a greatest height rather than a least one.


Spec links: `spcpt_scnXTjK798w4kzCC`

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_fDbhXWM64SrH3BWy`)

**FRONT**

What can you do with the equation of a trajectory that you cannot do as directly with the two separate displacement equations?


**BACK**

Get straight from a horizontal distance to a height, or from a height to a horizontal distance, **without finding the time first**.

With the parametric pair you would have to find $t$ from one equation and substitute it into the other every time; the trajectory equation has already done that once and for all.

That is what makes it the natural tool for questions asking whether a projectile clears a wall or passes over a given point.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_dyffWrZF4sd52NfZ`)

**FRONT**

What three assumptions do all the standard projectile formulae depend on?


**BACK**

They assume that the **launch and landing points are at the same vertical level**, that the projectile travels **over horizontal ground**, and that **no force acts on it except gravity**.

The first is the one that is most often broken by a question. Anything launched from a cliff, a building or a window lands lower than it started, and the formulae no longer apply.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_xJSFxrgwRvPbSmSM`)

**FRONT**

How is the **time of flight** of a projectile derived, and why does the working produce two answers?


**BACK**

Set the vertical displacement to **zero**, since the projectile returns to the level it started from, and use $s = u t + \frac{1}{2} a t^{2}$ vertically:

`0 = \left(U \sin \theta\right) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^{2}`

Factorising gives `0 = t \left(U \sin \theta - \frac{1}{2} g t\right)`, so either $t = 0$ or $t = \frac{2U\mathrm{sin}θ}{g}$.

Two answers appear because the vertical displacement is zero at **two** moments, at the start and at landing; the $t = 0$ root is the launch itself and is rejected.


Spec links: `spcpt_YPdhNKDXcHJm8HKG`


## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_q9sThgZGjjfMJnXh`)

**FRONT**

At the maximum height the vertical component of velocity is zero. Which vertical formula gives the **time** to that point, and which gives the **height** itself?


**BACK**

For the **time**, use $v = u + a t$: with $v = 0$, $0 = U \mathrm{sin} θ - g t$, giving $t = \frac{U\mathrm{sin}θ}{g}$.

For the **height**, use $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2 a s$: with $v = 0$, $0 = U^{2} \mathrm{sin}^{2} θ - 2 g y$, giving $y = \frac{U^{2}\mathrm{sin}^{2}θ}{2g}$.

Choosing between them is the usual suvat choice: the first formula contains $t$ and not $s$, the second contains $s$ and not $t$.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_TMSRpjpnHWyjpxKh`)

**FRONT**

How is the **range** of a projectile derived, and where does the $\mathrm{sin} 2 θ$ come from?


**BACK**

The range is the horizontal distance covered in the whole time of flight, and horizontally there is no acceleration, so $s = u t$ applies:

`x = \left(U \cos \theta\right) \left(\frac{2 U \sin \theta}{g}\right) = \frac{2 U^{2} \sin \theta \cos \theta}{g}`

The double angle identity $2 \mathrm{sin} θ \mathrm{cos} θ = \mathrm{sin} 2 θ$ then turns that into $\frac{U^{2}\mathrm{sin}2θ}{g}$.

So the $\mathrm{sin} 2 θ$ is not a mechanics result at all: it is a trigonometric identity tidying up the answer at the very end.

## Card 24 — true_or_false (`fl_7rrsvmzJRmqQyPvz`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can assume the range formula gives the horizontal distance travelled by a projectile launched from the top of a cliff.


**BACK**

**False.**

Every one of the standard projectile formulae is derived on the assumption that the launch and landing points are at the **same level**, and a cliff breaks that.

A projectile launched from a cliff is in the air for longer than the formula allows for, so it travels further horizontally than the formula predicts.

For a launch and landing at different heights, go back to the constant-acceleration formulae applied to each direction separately.
