## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_8pVzX4mnxrNFt9sc`)

**FRONT**

Define **polynomial**.


**BACK**

A **polynomial** is an algebraic expression made up of a finite number of terms, in which every index is a **non-negative integer**.

So $3 x + 5$ and $2 x^{2} y - 4 y + 6$ are polynomials, while $5 x^{-3}$ and $\sqrt{x}$ are not.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_mcD8MCXJS2JWY6hW`)

**FRONT**

What does polynomial division do to a polynomial?


**BACK**

It splits it into a **factor pair**, two expressions that multiply together to give it.

For example `x^{3} + x^{2} - x - 1 = \left(x + 1\right) \left(x^{2} - 1\right)`, where `\left(x + 1\right)` is the divisor and `\left(x^{2} - 1\right)` is the result.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_2H4fbZhN4Kf3z6rK`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Polynomial division can only be carried out when the divisor is a factor of the polynomial.


**BACK**

**False.**

The division works either way, and when the divisor is not a factor it simply finishes with a **remainder**, exactly as dividing $17$ by $5$ does.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_K7NtTBGpXfSYP6yG`)

**FRONT**

When dividing a polynomial by `\left(x - p\right)`, how do you decide the first term of the answer?


**BACK**

Compare the **highest power term** of the polynomial with the highest power term of the divisor.

Dividing $x^{3} + 6 x^{2} - 9 x - 14$ by `\left(x - 2\right)` starts with $x^{2}$, because $x^{2} \times x = x^{3}$.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_qmpbx3NW32RpMzrn`)

**FRONT**

In a polynomial division you have just written the next term of the answer above the line. What are the two things you do with it?


**BACK**

**Multiply** the whole divisor by it, then **subtract** that product from what is left of the polynomial.

Anything the subtraction has not touched is carried straight down, ready for the next term to be found.

**FRONT**

Apart from factorising, what else is polynomial division used for?


**BACK**

The same long-division method applies to them unchanged.

## Card 8 — keyword_definition (`fl_c2JkNwm4S3XVH3cT`)

**FRONT**

Define the **factor theorem**.


**BACK**

For a polynomial `\text{f} \left(x\right)`, if `\text{f} \left(p\right) = 0` then `\left(x - p\right)` is a factor of `\text{f} \left(x\right)`, and if `\left(x - p\right)` is a factor then `\text{f} \left(p\right) = 0`.

It works in **both directions**, so it can be used either to find a factor or to deduce a value from one you are given.

## Card 9 — true_or_false (`fl_fNF8vdxrbGFBvY5Y`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If `\left(x + 2\right)` is a factor of `\text{h} \left(x\right)`, then `\text{h} \left(2\right) = 0`.


**BACK**

**False.**

The factor has to be read as `\left(x - \left(- 2\right)\right)`, so the value that matters is $- 2$ and it is `\text{h} \left(- 2\right)` that is zero.

A factor written as `\left(x + p\right)` always pairs with the value $- p$.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_mQVZ9TdPxPXVM8R3`)

**FRONT**

What does the **remainder theorem** tell you?


**BACK**

That dividing a polynomial `\text{f} \left(x\right)` by `\left(x - a\right)` leaves a remainder of `\text{f} \left(a\right)`.

Written formally, `\text{f} \left(x\right) = \left(x - a\right) Q \left(x\right) + \text{f} \left(a\right)`.


Spec links: `spcpt_5C5R39cdnm53Ktnh`

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_G7rFZYWHk6Zn4pPt`)

**FRONT**

How is the factor theorem a special case of the remainder theorem?


**BACK**

It is the case in which the remainder is **zero**, so the division comes out exactly.

Putting `\text{f} \left(a\right) = 0` leaves `\text{f} \left(x\right) = \left(x - a\right) Q \left(x\right)`, which says precisely that `\left(x - a\right)` is a factor.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_sZbVCKPGz7DHCCfk`)

**FRONT**

What is the remainder when $x^{2} - 2 x$ is divided by `\left(x - 3\right)`, and how do you get it without dividing?


**BACK**

Substitute $x = 3$ into the polynomial: $3^{2} - 2 \times 3 = 3$.

Whenever the remainder is all that is wanted, this replaces the entire division with a single substitution.

## Card 14 — true_or_false (`fl_kKrrJpRzjvsM4d5M`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

When a polynomial is divided by `\left(x - a\right)`, the remainder is always a number rather than an expression in $x$.


**BACK**

**True.**

The division is written with everything involving $x$ collected into the bracketed quotient, leaving a single value outside it.

So `x^{3} + 2 x^{2} + 3 x + 4 = \left(x - 1\right) \left(x^{2} + 3 x + 6\right) + 10`, and the remainder is simply $10$.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_ywJbXVtMsBWyX4K6`)

**FRONT**

The remainder when $x^{2} + p x$ is divided by `\left(x - 2\right)` is $8$. How do you find $p$?


**BACK**

Set the value of the polynomial at $x = 2$ equal to the remainder and solve: $2^{2} + 2 p = 8$.

That gives $p = 2$, and where a question carries more than one unknown the same move produces simultaneous equations.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_VQjX83Hd8CVmJ8nk`)

**FRONT**

Which two techniques does factorising a cubic combine?


**BACK**

The **factor theorem**, to find one linear factor, and **polynomial division**, to get the rest.

Neither is enough alone: the theorem gives a factor but not the quotient, and division needs a divisor before it can start.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_r5xX8FsmKKQvtTfT`)

**FRONT**

What is the goal when fully factorising a polynomial?


**BACK**

To write it as a **product of linear factors**, taken as far as it will go.

For example `x^{3} + 4 x^{2} - 11 x - 30 = \left(x + 2\right) \left(x - 3\right) \left(x + 5\right)`.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_FxKMBFc7QtBfrwK8`)

**FRONT**

What is the first move in factorising a cubic `\text{f}\left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

Find a value $p$ for which `\text{f}\left(p\right) = 0`.

Until you have one factor there is nothing to divide by, so this has to come first.
