## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_KDqj8mp4w8dnNphN`)

**FRONT**

Define **strictly increasing**.


**BACK**

A function is **strictly increasing** on an interval if `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) > 0` at every point of that interval.

Plain increasing is the weaker condition, since it permits the derivative to be zero here and there, so a curve that momentarily flattens still counts.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_hQf9PSMXcKhP9dtt`)

**FRONT**

What does the sign of `\text{f} ' \left(x\right)` tell you about the graph?


**BACK**

Positive means the curve is going **up** as $x$ increases, and negative means it is going **down**.

So the sign of the derivative, read across the domain, gives you the whole shape of the curve without plotting a single point.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_q386DpqFQFMtBw7k`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A function must be either increasing or decreasing over the whole of its domain.


**BACK**

**False.**

Most functions do both on different intervals: `\text{f} \left(x\right) = x^{3} + x^{2} - x + 2` increases, then decreases, then increases again.

That is why increasing and decreasing are always described over an **interval** rather than for a function as a whole.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_JmNJGn4KbpbZgCwj`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the intervals on which `\text{f} \left(x\right)` is increasing?


**BACK**

Differentiate to get `\text{f} ' \left(x\right)`, then solve the inequality `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) \ge 0`.

The solution set of that inequality is exactly the set of intervals being asked for.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_ThQkNSjMcKw8nmsF`)

**FRONT**

Solving `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) \ge 0` leaves you with a quadratic inequality. What is the quickest way to read off the intervals?


**BACK**

Sketch that quadratic and see where the curve lies **on or above** the $x$-axis.

For $3 x^{2} + 2 x - 1 \geq 0$, whose roots are $- 1$ and $\frac{1}{3}$, that is the two outer intervals rather than the middle one.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_RnHhxYhPpVHBvVpw`)

**FRONT**

Why are the ends of the increasing and decreasing intervals the solutions of `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) = 0`?


**BACK**

Because a smooth curve's gradient can only change from positive to negative, or back again, by passing through **zero**.

Those points are therefore exactly where one kind of interval finishes and the next begins.

## Card 8 — keyword_definition (`fl_S2V88nDNM2wkfmmC`)

**FRONT**

Define **stationary point**.


**BACK**

A point on a curve where the **gradient is zero**.

It may be a local minimum, a local maximum, or a point of inflection.


Spec links: `spcpt_kzV6TBRZ65SSjSBF`


## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_QGNNsYT7WdyT7JNt`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the stationary points of `\text{f}\left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

Differentiate, solve `\text{f}'\left(x\right) = 0` for the $x$-coordinates, then substitute those back into `\text{f}\left(x\right)` for the $y$-coordinates.

The derivative gives the positions; the original function gives the heights.


Spec links: `spcpt_kzV6TBRZ65SSjSBF`


## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_Nd9fg3MkZHfcScGq`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a stationary point and a turning point?


**BACK**

Every turning point is stationary, but a **point of inflection** is stationary without being a turning point.

A turning point is one where the curve actually changes direction, from rising to falling or the other way round.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_Nsb7g3ybpn6tWDCB`)

**FRONT**

How do you use the **first** derivative to determine the nature of a stationary point?


**BACK**

Check its **sign just to the left and just to the right** of the point.

Negative then positive is a minimum, positive then negative is a maximum, and the same sign on both sides is a point of inflection.

## Card 13 — true_or_false (`fl_3BxjbNBCVvm7sgXN`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If `\text{f}''\left(x\right) = 0` at a stationary point, the point is a point of inflection.


**BACK**

**False.**

A zero second derivative tells you **nothing**: the point could still be a maximum or a minimum.

The first-derivative test has to be used instead, and that one always works.

**FRONT**

Which test for the nature of a stationary point should you reach for first?


**BACK**

The **second derivative**, because it is usually much quicker.

Fall back on the first-derivative test only when the second derivative turns out to be zero.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_8qSTqdnhSy6shrw9`)

**FRONT**

How many stationary points does a quadratic have, and what kind?


**BACK**

Exactly one, and it is always a **turning point** rather than a point of inflection.

Its $y$-value is therefore the minimum or maximum value the whole quadratic can take.

What does a stationary point on `y = \text{f} \left(x\right)` become on the graph of `y = \text{f} ' \left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

## Card 18 — true_or_false (`fl_TB478djTxgSbYBFG`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Where `\text{f}\left(x\right)` cuts the $x$-axis, `\text{f}'\left(x\right)` does something notable.


**BACK**

**False.**

Where $\text{f}$ crosses the axis tells you nothing at all about $\text{f} '$.

Only the **gradient** of $\text{f}$ matters, and a curve can cross the axis at any gradient whatever.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_gWCdzm6RGpxtpGpR`)

**FRONT**

What can you **not** work out about `\text{f} ' \left(x\right)` from the graph of `\text{f} \left(x\right)` alone?


**BACK**

The exact coordinates of its $y$-intercept, or of its own stationary points.

A sketch of $\text{f}$ gives you the shape of $\text{f} '$, not its precise values.

## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_8Nfx6pGDjpY6gs9p`)

**FRONT**

Can you differentiate variables other than $y$ and $x$?


**BACK**

Yes: a derivative can be taken with respect to any variable.

$\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}r}$, for instance, gives the rate of change of a volume with respect to a radius.

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_HDjmXBxZnJPQ3Z66`)

**FRONT**

What has to happen before you can optimise a quantity by differentiating?


**BACK**

It has to be written as a formula in a **single** variable.

These problems usually give two variables plus a constraint linking them, and the constraint is what lets you eliminate one.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_GkZQKr7fHRqcqskr`)

**FRONT**

Why is the answer to an optimisation question often not just the value of $x$?


**BACK**

Because the question usually asks for the maximum or minimum **value itself**, not for where it occurs.

Substituting the stationary point's $x$ back into the original formula is what gives that value.

## Card 24 — true_or_false (`fl_bVrd8ByzDb4B5mK9`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A derivative in a modelling question always represents a gradient on a graph.


**BACK**

**False.**

It represents a **rate of change** of one quantity with respect to another, whatever those quantities happen to be.

The gradient of a graph is simply the case where they are $y$ and $x$.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_FrKmqmhRHtbmnzTn`)

**FRONT**

Why might a stationary point of a model not be a sensible answer?


**BACK**

Because the context may restrict the variable: a length cannot be negative, and a number of items has to be a whole number.

A mathematically valid stationary point can fall outside the range the situation allows.
