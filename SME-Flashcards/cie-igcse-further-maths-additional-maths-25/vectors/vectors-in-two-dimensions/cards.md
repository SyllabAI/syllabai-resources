## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_zM9Wg854zgDtjfBY`)

**FRONT**

Define a **vector**.


**BACK**

A **vector** is a quantity with both a **magnitude** (size) and a **direction**.

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are all vector quantities, and each has components along the $x$ and $y$ axes.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_fxFy9r6kxvdgm3kD`)

**FRONT**

How do you add or subtract two vectors?


**BACK**

Add or subtract the corresponding **components** separately.

In base vector form that means combining the $i$ terms with each other and the $j$ terms with each other, so $( 2 i + j ) - ( i + 4 j ) = i - 3 j$.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_Z9brJW7nkWdKCfBh`)

**FRONT**

What are the two ways of writing a vector?


**BACK**

As a **column vector**, such as `\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}`, with the $x$ component above the $y$ component.

Or in **base vector** form using the unit vectors, as $3 i - 2 j$.

## Card 6 — keyword_definition (`fl_dQQ7329zSbQJNckX`)

**FRONT**

Define the **resultant** vector.


**BACK**

The **resultant** is the single vector equivalent to two or more vectors added together.

Drawn nose to tail, it is the direct route from the start of the first vector to the end of the last.

## Card 7 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_7PQFjmcKPqNKP98c`)

**FRONT**

Complete the magnitude of the vector $v = v_{1} i + v_{2} j$:

`\left|\mathbf{v}\right| = \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed formula is:

`\left|\mathbf{v}\right| = \sqrt{v_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2}}`

The vertical bars mean magnitude, and the result is a **scalar**, since a length has no direction.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_YGytcxpNCWNr8Mp6`)

**FRONT**

What is subtracting a vector the same as?


**BACK**

**Adding the negative** of that vector, so $a - b$ means $a + ( - b )$.

The negative of a vector has the same length but points the opposite way, so you travel back along it.

## Card 9 — true_or_false (`fl_DTK3q45mH52kB9tz`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Finding the magnitude of a vector is the same calculation as finding the distance between two points.


**BACK**

**True.**

Both are Pythagoras' theorem applied to the horizontal and vertical parts, with the vector or the line as the hypotenuse.

So there is really only one formula here to learn, not two.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_vzKjP8FdNdDf7X5X`)

**FRONT**

When are two vectors parallel?


**BACK**

When one is a **scalar multiple** of the other, so $a = k b$ for some constant $k$.

Every component must have been multiplied by that same $k$, which is what you check when testing a pair.

## Card 11 — keyword_definition (`fl_VJfjVCzr6kMZT2Yx`)

**FRONT**

Define a **unit vector**.


**BACK**

A **unit vector** is a vector whose magnitude is exactly **1**.

The vectors $i$ and $j$ are the unit vectors along the positive $x$ and $y$ directions.

## Card 12 — true_or_false (`fl_v3Rt3rmnxDzWtWSw`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Multiplying a vector by a negative scalar changes only its magnitude.


**BACK**

**False.**

It changes the magnitude **and reverses the direction**, so the vector ends up pointing the opposite way.

Only a **positive** scalar leaves the direction alone.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_fhcpJdhBH7Q8kfwx`)

**FRONT**

How do you find a unit vector in the direction of $v$?


**BACK**

**Divide the vector by its own magnitude**, giving `\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left|\mathbf{v}\right|}`.

So for $- 12 i + 5 j$, whose magnitude is 13, the unit vector is $- \frac{12}{13} i + \frac{5}{13} j$.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_zchyNTFgcP79tfN9`)

**FRONT**

$O$ is the origin, with $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA} = a$ and $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB} = b$. Find $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$.


**BACK**

Travel from $A$ to $B$ by going back through the origin:

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AO} + \overset{\rightarrow}{OB} = - a + b$

So $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = b - a$, which is the **finishing** position vector minus the **starting** one.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_yYM7ZwvvrsyzjXJd`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a position vector and a displacement vector?


**BACK**

A **position vector** is measured from the **origin**, so $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA} = a$ fixes where the point $A$ is.

A **displacement vector** runs between any two points and describes the journey from one to the other.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_nJwGX78BNDXCjsWj`)

**FRONT**

What is the zero vector?


**BACK**

It is the vector with no magnitude and no direction, written as a bold $0$, or underlined by hand.

You get it by adding a vector to its own reverse, since $\overset{\rightarrow}{PQ} + \overset{\rightarrow}{QP}$ returns you to where you started.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_PZGYWT7Fgj56xC6y`)

**FRONT**

Where does the midpoint result $\frac{1}{2} ( a + b )$ come from?


**BACK**

Go from $O$ to $A$, then half way along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$:

$\overset{\rightarrow}{OM} = a + \frac{1}{2} ( b - a )$

Expanding gives $\frac{1}{2} a + \frac{1}{2} b$, which is the stated result.

## Card 21 — keyword_definition (`fl_5qRvsQRBhY8pzMKR`)

**FRONT**

Define **collinear**.


**BACK**

Points are **collinear** if they all lie on the **same straight line**.

For instance $( - 2 , - 2 )$, $( 3 , 3 )$ and $( 8 , 8 )$ are collinear, since all three sit on $y = x$.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_d97SyNjDG3KNRQ47`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a specific and a general vector?


**BACK**

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ is **specific**: it is the one arrow starting at $A$ and ending at $B$.

A labelled vector such as $a$ is **general**, so any arrow of the same length and direction anywhere on the diagram is also $a$.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_8H9dCN5xPPyDYBcn`)

**FRONT**

On a grid of identical parallelograms, how do you write the vector between two points in terms of $a$ and $b$?


**BACK**

Trace any **path** from one point to the other along the grid lines, and add the vectors as you go.

Each step is $a$ or $b$, or its negative if you travel against the arrow, and different routes all give the same answer.

## Card 25 — true_or_false (`fl_vBrJjD5Sr5hbY7HZ`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If two vectors are scalar multiples of each other, the points involved must be collinear.


**BACK**

**False.**

Scalar multiples are **parallel**, and parallel lines never meet, so the vectors could sit on two quite separate lines.

Collinearity needs the two vectors to share a **point** as well, which is why both tests start them from the same place.

## Card 26 — question_and_answer (`fl_TKZcpJzHKJtqz2QF`)

**FRONT**

What does a velocity vector such as `\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{ m s}^{-1}` tell you?


**BACK**

That the object moves at $3 \text{m s}^{-1}$ in the positive $x$ direction **and** $7 \text{m s}^{-1}$ in the positive $y$ direction.

The two components happen at once, so the object travels along the diagonal they produce.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_GvK4QzPmjPDVSMBS`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between speed and velocity?


**BACK**

**Velocity** is a vector, carrying both size and direction; **speed** is a scalar, carrying size alone.

Speed is the **magnitude** of the velocity, so it is found with Pythagoras and can never be negative.

## Card 28 — question_and_answer (`fl_35VGmNTKvT2bvbpV`)

**FRONT**

A particle has velocity `\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}\text{ m s}^{-1}`. Find its speed.


**BACK**

Speed is the magnitude of the velocity:

$\sqrt{6^{2}+8^{2}} = \sqrt{100} = 10$

So the speed is $10 \text{m s}^{-1}$, and the units stay the same because a magnitude is still a rate.

## Card 29 — true_or_false (`fl_8M8ZZbMwrzQSMmFb`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Two objects travelling in opposite directions can have exactly the same speed.


**BACK**

**True.**

Velocities of `\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}` and `\begin{pmatrix} -6 \\ -8 \end{pmatrix}` both have magnitude $10 \text{m s}^{-1}$.

Squaring the components destroys the signs, which is exactly why speed carries no direction.

## Card 31 — question_and_answer (`fl_PDSFSw2k5g83NTcs`)

**FRONT**

A particle starts at $( 2 , - 3 )$ and moves with constant velocity $( 4 i + 7 j ) \text{m s}^{-1}$. Where is it after 10 seconds?


**BACK**

Use $r = r_{0} + v t$ with $t = 10$:

$( 2 i - 3 j ) + 10 ( 4 i + 7 j )$

This gives $r = ( 42 i + 67 j ) \text{m}$, so the particle is at the point $( 42 , 67 )$.

## Card 32 — question_and_answer (`fl_4TJkpNq56BH9s8XF`)

**FRONT**

A boat's engine drives it at $( i + 2 j )$ while the current carries it at $( 1 . 5 i + 2 j )$. Find its resultant velocity.


**BACK**

**Add** the two velocity vectors, component by component:

$( i + 2 j ) + ( 1 . 5 i + 2 j ) = 2 . 5 i + 4 j$

So the resultant velocity is $( 2 . 5 i + 4 j ) \text{m s}^{-1}$, which is the velocity the boat actually travels at.

## Card 33 — question_and_answer (`fl_J68ByKPMkk9Dv3rv`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the time at which two particles collide?


**BACK**

Colliding means being in the **same place at the same time**, so set their position vectors equal to each other.

Two vectors are equal only when both components match, so equating either the $i$ or the $j$ components gives an equation to solve for $t$.
