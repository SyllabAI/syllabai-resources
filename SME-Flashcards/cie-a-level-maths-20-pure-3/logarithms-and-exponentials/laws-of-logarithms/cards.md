# Laws of Logarithms

## Card 1 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_2gMvpwKxnY4Yjh2s`)

**FRONT**

Complete the three laws of logarithms:

`\log_{a} x y = \_\_\_\_\_\_`

`\log_{a} \left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \_\_\_\_\_\_`

`\log_{a} x^{k} = \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed laws are:

$\mathrm{log}_{a} x y = \mathrm{log}_{a} x + \mathrm{log}_{a} y$

`\log_{a} \left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_{a} x - \log_{a} y`

$\mathrm{log}_{a} x^{k} = k \mathrm{log}_{a} x$

In words: multiplying becomes adding, dividing becomes subtracting, and a power becomes a multiplier out in front.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_Sbq6VNHhGpnzPHrC`)

**FRONT**

Where do the laws of logarithms come from?


**BACK**

They are the **laws of indices** in disguise, because a logarithm is itself a power.

Behind the product law sits $a^{x} \times a^{y} = a^{x+y}$, and behind the power law sits `\left(a^{x}\right)^{y} = a^{x y}`.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_wYDPdk9MYkfrWMZp`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

`\log \left(x + y\right) = \log x + \log y`


**BACK**

**False.**

There is no law that breaks up the logarithm of a **sum**: only a product, a quotient or a power can be split.

Taking $x = y = 10$ in base $10$ gives $\mathrm{log} 20 \approx 1 . 301$ on the left, against $1 + 1 = 2$ on the right.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_vfvcpPpZwQqJMXp6`)

**FRONT**

What are $\mathrm{log}_{a} a$ and $\mathrm{log}_{a} 1$ equal to?


**BACK**

The first is $1$ and the second is $0$.

Both come straight from reading a logarithm as a power: you raise $a$ to the power $1$ to get $a$, and to the power $0$ to get $1$.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_ygG6N34rVPf7bvfB`)

**FRONT**

Do the laws of logarithms apply to $\mathrm{ln}$?


**BACK**

Yes, because $\mathrm{ln} x$ is simply $\mathrm{log}_{\text{e}} x$, a logarithm whose base happens to be $\text{e}$.

Nothing in any of the laws depends on which base is used, so every one of them applies to $\mathrm{ln}$ unchanged.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_kf9jzD8pYDcxP2Sg`)

**FRONT**

What does `\log_{a} \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)` simplify to?


**BACK**

It simplifies to $- \mathrm{log}_{a} x$.

Writing $\frac{1}{x}$ as $x^{-1}$ makes it $\mathrm{log}_{a} x^{-1}$, and the power law then brings the $- 1$ out to the front.

## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_hRtJS7kz4VmWT9NG`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A logarithm can come out negative.


**BACK**

**True.**

For example $\mathrm{log}_{10} 0 . 01 = - 2$, because $10^{-2} = 0 . 01$.

It is the number you take the logarithm **of** that has to be positive; the logarithm itself can be any real number.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_S99qbSCMNTQxcrTC`)

**FRONT**

You have reduced a logarithmic equation to `\log_{2} \left(\frac{x^{2}}{x + 6}\right) = 3`. What is the next move?


**BACK**

Undo the logarithm by making each side a power of the base, giving $\frac{x^{2}}{x+6} = 2^{3} = 8$.

That removes the logarithm altogether and leaves an ordinary equation, here a quadratic once you multiply up by $x + 6$.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_PhBZJ9M4PrX3Q9Dq`)

**FRONT**

Why must you check the solutions of a logarithmic equation?


**BACK**

Because $\mathrm{log}_{a} x$ is only defined when $x > 0$, so any value making a logarithm in the original equation take a negative or zero argument must be rejected.

Solving `2 \log_{2} x = 3 + \log_{2} \left(x + 6\right)` gives $x = 12$ and $x = - 4$, and $- 4$ goes because `\log_{2} \left(- 4\right)` does not exist.

## Card 10 — keyword_definition (`fl_zCcWMHrf8BhVx7r6`)

**FRONT**

Define an **exponential equation**.


**BACK**

An equation in which the **unknown is a power**.

For example $3^{3x} - 4 = 9^{x} + 5$.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_KnCqfHfCcnKWSpHJ`)

**FRONT**

When can an exponential equation be solved without using logarithms at all?


**BACK**

When both sides can be written as powers of the **same base**, because then the powers themselves must be equal.

$5^{2x}=125\Rightarrow5^{2x}=5^{3}$, so $2 x = 3$ and $x=\frac{3}{2}$.


Spec links: `spcpt_cMmmKhpspFd2qwy4`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_c8Y5Q7rxtSB6TWHH`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve an exponential equation whose two sides **cannot** be written as powers of the same base?


**BACK**

Take **logarithms of both sides**, then use $\mathrm{log}_{a} x^{k} = k \mathrm{log}_{a} x$ to bring each power down as a multiplier, then rearrange for $x$.

Natural logarithms are usual, and exact answers are normally left in terms of $\mathrm{ln}$.

## Card 13 — true_or_false (`fl_pWXY86z4Ds3G4kNS`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\frac{\mathrm{ln}42}{\mathrm{ln}6} = \mathrm{ln} 7$


**BACK**

**False.**

Logarithms cannot be cancelled or divided like that. $\frac{\mathrm{ln}42}{\mathrm{ln}6}$ is one number divided by another and does not simplify.

It is **subtraction** that combines them: $\mathrm{ln} 42 - \mathrm{ln} 6 = \mathrm{ln} 7$.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_K7kMS8vCfBXXt2Ht`)

**FRONT**

How do you spot and handle a hidden quadratic in an exponential equation?


**BACK**

Look for one exponential term that is the **square** of another: `4^{x} = \left(2^{x}\right)^{2}` and `\text{e}^{2x} = \left(\text{e}^{x}\right)^{2}`.

Then substitute, for example, $y=\text{e}^{x}$, solve the resulting quadratic, then solve $\text{e}^{x}=y$ for each root.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_7YSKF93dWq9yhWyt`)

**FRONT**

An answer must be given as $x = \frac{\mathrm{ln}p}{\mathrm{ln}q}$ with $p$ and $q$ integers. How do you get there from $x = \frac{6\mathrm{ln}2+\mathrm{ln}3}{2\mathrm{ln}3+\mathrm{ln}2}$?


**BACK**

Use the laws of logarithms in **reverse** to collapse the top and bottom into single logarithms.

$6 \mathrm{ln} 2 = \mathrm{ln} 64$, so the top is $\mathrm{ln} 64 + \mathrm{ln} 3 = \mathrm{ln} 192$. The bottom is $\mathrm{ln} 9 + \mathrm{ln} 2 = \mathrm{ln} 18$, giving $x = \frac{\mathrm{ln}192}{\mathrm{ln}18}$.
