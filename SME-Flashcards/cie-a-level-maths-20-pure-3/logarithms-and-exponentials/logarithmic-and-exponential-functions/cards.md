## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_VhNn2hTPzxgc2Gwb`)

**FRONT**

Define **exponential function**.


**BACK**

A function of the form $y = a^{x}$, where the variable sits in the **power** and $a > 0$.

It is the variable's position that makes it exponential, not the size of the base.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_Dzqm6b52WwnpHFcJ`)

**FRONT**

What do all graphs of the form $y = a^{x}$ have in common?


**BACK**

They all pass through `\left(0 , 1\right)`, because $a^{0} = 1$ whatever $a$ is.

The $x$-axis is an asymptote, so the curve approaches it without ever reaching it.

## Card 3 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_QWZMFKYhHHygdk6c`)

$y = a^{x}$ shows exponential **growth** when `a \_\_\_\_\_\_`, and exponential **decay** when `\_\_\_\_\_\_ < a < \_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

It shows growth when $a > 1$, and decay when $0 < a < 1$.

A base bigger than $1$ multiplies up at every step, while a base between $0$ and $1$ multiplies down.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_TnN2ZsZw7dsSTMCS`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$y = 1^{x}$ is an exponential function.


**BACK**

**False.**

$1^{x} = 1$ for every value of $x$, so the graph is just the horizontal line $y = 1$.

That is precisely why $a = 1$ is excluded: nothing grows and nothing decays.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_yR6ZzdSv2M5wWTtX`)

**FRONT**

For $x > 0$, is $3^{x}$ above or below $2^{x}$, and what happens for $x < 0$?


**BACK**

Above for $x > 0$, and **below** for $x < 0$.

The two curves meet at `\left(0 , 1\right)`, which every exponential graph passes through, so that is where the ordering swaps.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_dCjkdDX2fWvT33B5`)

**FRONT**

Why must the base of an exponential function be positive?


**BACK**

Because a negative base gives no real value for fractional powers.

`\left(- 4\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}` does not exist as a real number, so the graph would have gaps instead of being a smooth curve.

## Card 7 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_DvzbxtTMXdTYnVnc`)

**FRONT**

$a^{x} = b$ and `\log_{a} b = \_\_\_\_\_\_` are equivalent statements, in which $a$ is called the `\_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

$a^{x} = b$ and $\mathrm{log}_{a} b = x$ are equivalent statements, in which $a$ is called the **base**.

The base must be positive, and the logarithm is simply the power.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_FRKkxfn63YvZ53x4`)

**FRONT**

How do you evaluate $\mathrm{log}_{2} 32$ without a calculator?


**BACK**

Ask what power of $2$ gives $32$: since $2^{5} = 32$, the answer is $5$.

Knowing the first few powers of $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$ and $10$ makes most such logarithms immediate.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_44RPSpCT5MS3CTcJ`)

**FRONT**

What does $\mathrm{log} x$ mean when no base is written?


**BACK**

Base $10$: $\mathrm{log} x$ means $\mathrm{log}_{10} x$, and it is sometimes written $\mathrm{lg} x$ instead.

A base written explicitly, such as $\mathrm{log}_{2}$, always overrides that convention.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_sTfK2HdvFF8d7WQX`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

`\left(\log x\right)^{2}` and $\mathrm{log} x^{2}$ mean the same thing.


**BACK**

**False.**

`\left(\log x\right)^{2}` means take the logarithm first and then square it.

$\mathrm{log} x^{2}$ means square $x$ first and then take the logarithm, which the power law turns into $2 \mathrm{log} x$.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_cYsJgWqHdnhZBgN9`)

**FRONT**

What does it mean to say a logarithm is the inverse of raising to a power?


**BACK**

Each undoes the other, so $a^{\mathrm{log}_{a}x} = x$ and `\log_{a}\left(a^{x}\right) = x`.

That is what makes a logarithm the tool for getting a variable down out of an exponent.

## Card 13 — keyword_definition (`fl_kDtY7rcYGn2xn5CV`)

**FRONT**

Define **e**.


**BACK**

**e** is an irrational number, approximately $2 . 718$, sometimes called Euler's number.

Like $π$, it is a **number**, not a variable and not a function.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_yPyd4Zwmw3yHZShr`)

**FRONT**

What is special about the graph of $y = \text{e}^{x}$?


**BACK**

Its gradient at every point is equal to its own value, so $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = \text{e}^{x}$.

No other base behaves that way, which is exactly why $\text{e}$ is singled out.

How is the graph of $y = \text{e}^{-x}$ related to $y = \text{e}^{x}$?

## Card 18 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_Q75B6kCjjqhc64Sy`)

**FRONT**

If $y = \text{e}^{kx}$ then `\frac{\text{d} y}{\text{d} x} = \_\_\_\_\_\_`, and if $y = \text{e}^{-kx}$ then `\frac{\text{d} y}{\text{d} x} = \_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

If $y = \text{e}^{kx}$ then $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = k \text{e}^{kx}$, and if $y = \text{e}^{-kx}$ then $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = - k \text{e}^{-kx}$.

The constant from the power comes down as a multiplier, and $y = \text{e}^{x}$ is simply the case $k = 1$.

**FRONT**

What stays the same when you differentiate $\text{e}^{kx}$?


**BACK**

The exponential part itself: $\text{e}^{kx}$ reappears in the derivative unchanged.

Only a constant multiplier is added in front, which is what makes exponential derivatives unusually simple.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_nrf69nfyNrTfjHyD`)

**FRONT**

What is the derivative of $y = \text{e}^{-3x}$?


**BACK**

$\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = - 3 \text{e}^{-3x}$.

The minus sign comes down with the $3$, so a decay curve has a negative gradient everywhere along it.

## Card 21 — true_or_false (`fl_CGQnymyqYw63v3pt`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The gradient of $y = \text{e}^{x}$ is never zero.


**BACK**

**True.**

The gradient equals $\text{e}^{x}$, which is positive for every value of $x$.

So the curve is always increasing and has no stationary points at all.

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_c7bKPm3cffGxRtrC`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the gradient of $y = \text{e}^{2x}$ at $x = 0$?


**BACK**

Differentiate to get $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = 2 \text{e}^{2x}$, then substitute $x = 0$.

Since $\text{e}^{0} = 1$, the gradient there is $2$.
