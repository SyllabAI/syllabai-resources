## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_BtWdkYkPBnJWKgpV`)

**FRONT**

Define an **Argand diagram**.


**BACK**

An **Argand diagram** is a way of representing complex numbers geometrically, in two dimensions.

It gives every complex number its own position in the plane, so that algebra with complex numbers can be seen as a picture.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_2RBzyMtCkgMh7m7X`)

**FRONT**

What do the two axes of an Argand diagram represent?


**BACK**

The horizontal axis is the **real axis**, labelled $\text{Re}$, and the vertical axis is the **imaginary axis**, labelled $\text{Im}$.

The complex number $x + y \text{i}$ is therefore placed at the point with coordinates `\left(x, y\right)`.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_XjMwydfVXxgDsStG`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The number $2$ cannot be shown on an Argand diagram, because it has no imaginary part.


**BACK**

**False.**

A real number is a complex number whose imaginary part is $0$, so $2$ is $2 + 0 \text{i}$ and is plotted at `\left(2, 0\right)`.

Every real number has a place on an Argand diagram, on the horizontal axis.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_rBF2YgjmyphFcvM8`)

**FRONT**

A complex number can be shown on an Argand diagram in two different ways. What are they?


**BACK**

As a **point**, marked at the coordinates given by its real and imaginary parts, or as a **position vector**, drawn as an arrow from the origin to that point.

Both represent the same complex number.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_bwkpzsmRPBnK9rC5`)

**FRONT**

Two complex numbers are drawn as position vectors and completed into a parallelogram. What does each diagonal show?


**BACK**

The diagonal starting at the **origin** gives their sum, $z_{1} + z_{2}$.

The other diagonal, joining the two plotted points, gives their difference, $z_{1} - z_{2}$.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_yGHPVkv5bxTQrnJC`)

**FRONT**

What is the geometric effect of adding a fixed complex number $w = a + b \text{i}$ to $z$?


**BACK**

It **translates** $z$ by the vector `\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}`.

Every point moves the same distance in the same direction, so nothing about the shape or size of a drawing changes.


Spec links: `spcpt_NXpDX6ztHhmdcPbD`


## Card 8 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_k3d9yR7Kd2xN2vd4`)

**FRONT**

Subtracting a fixed complex number $w = a + b \text{i}$ from $z$ also translates $z$. Complete the translation vector.

`\begin{bmatrix} \_\_\_\_\_\_ \\ \_\_\_\_\_\_ \end{bmatrix}`


**BACK**

The completed vector is:

`\begin{bmatrix} -a \\ -b \end{bmatrix}`

Subtracting $w$ moves a point exactly as far as adding $w$ would, but in the opposite direction.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_YxXnXyDsJjQJd6gQ`)

**FRONT**

Why can complex numbers be added using column vectors?


**BACK**

Because $x + y \text{i}$ can be represented by the position vector `\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}`, and each of the two parts is combined on its own.

Adding two column vectors does precisely that, entry by entry.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_dzGZ4zjxNSQsC9wT`)

**FRONT**

What is the geometric effect of taking the complex conjugate of $z$?


**BACK**

It **reflects** $z$ in the **real axis**.

The real part is unchanged while the imaginary part changes sign, so the point crosses the horizontal axis to an equal distance on the other side.

## Card 12 — true_or_false (`fl_3B4f3YhY3C7TZZMh`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Taking the conjugate moves every point on an Argand diagram.


**BACK**

**False.**

A point on the **real axis** stays exactly where it is, because its imaginary part is already $0$ and changing the sign of $0$ changes nothing.

Those are the only points left in place.

## Card 13 — keyword_definition (`fl_SV6QVvvwjTGmwzs7`)

**FRONT**

Define the **modulus** of a complex number.


**BACK**

The **modulus** of $z$, written `\left|z\right|`, is its distance from the origin on an Argand diagram.

For $z = x + y \text{i}$, Pythagoras gives `\left|z\right| = \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}`.


Spec links: `spcpt_CTzdQvyV74XgQhWs`


## Card 14 — true_or_false (`fl_K2bMtCjBkKWWn3K2`)

**True or False?**

For any two complex numbers, `\left|z_{1} + z_{2}\right| = \left|z_{1}\right| + \left|z_{2}\right|`.


**BACK**

**False.**

Both $3 + 4 \text{i}$ and $- 3 + 4 \text{i}$ have a modulus of $5$, but their sum is $8 \text{i}$, whose modulus is $8$ rather than $10$.

## Card 15 — keyword_definition (`fl_X4WrxKP5hqphNRfd`)

**FRONT**

Define the **argument** of a complex number.


**BACK**

The **argument** of $z$, written $\mathrm{arg} z$, is the angle measured **anticlockwise** in radians from the positive real axis to the line joining the origin to $z$.

The argument of $0$ is undefined, because no such line can be drawn.


Spec links: `spcpt_CTzdQvyV74XgQhWs`

Complete the range in which arguments are usually given.

`\_\_\_\_\_\_ < \arg z \le \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed range is:

$- π < \mathrm{arg} z \leq π$

Occasionally the range $0 \leq \mathrm{arg} z < 2 π$ is used instead.

## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_7xwkJKSMF5vk8RcM`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A complex number in the third or fourth quadrant has a negative argument.


**BACK**

**True.**

In the usual range the angle is measured from the positive real axis, and for a point below that axis the shorter turn is clockwise, which counts as negative.

Measuring anticlockwise instead would give an angle greater than $π$, outside the range.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_rbZvwDVRdvd6WjQt`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the argument of $- 1 - \sqrt{3} \text{i}$?


**BACK**

Sketch the point first to see which quadrant it lies in, then use $\mathrm{tan}$ on the two side lengths to get the acute angle to the real axis, here $\mathrm{tan}^{-1} \sqrt{3} = \frac{π}{3}$.

The point is in the third quadrant, so the argument is `-\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{2\pi}{3}`.


Spec links: `spcpt_CTzdQvyV74XgQhWs`


## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_RV3mG4ZGhMTXBn9z`)

**FRONT**

What is the modulus-argument (polar) form of a complex number?


**BACK**

`z = r\left(\cos\theta + \text{i}\sin\theta\right)`, where $r$ is the modulus and $θ$ is the argument.

It follows from substituting $x = r \mathrm{cos} θ$ and $y = r \mathrm{sin} θ$ into $z = x + y \text{i}$.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_qBcVGkwgRVzRjxqr`)

**FRONT**

A complex number is written as `3\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{5} - \text{i}\sin\frac{\pi}{5}\right)`. What is its argument?


**BACK**

$- \frac{π}{5}$, not $\frac{π}{5}$.

The minus sign means this is not yet in polar form. Using `\cos\left(-\theta\right) \equiv \cos\theta` and `\sin\left(-\theta\right) \equiv -\sin\theta` rewrites it with a plus sign and reveals the true argument.

## Card 21 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_9Kdq9Sk7BjC9yy7s`)

**FRONT**

When two complex numbers are multiplied, complete what happens to their moduli and their arguments.

`\left|z_{1}z_{2}\right| = \_\_\_\_\_\_, \arg\left(z_{1}z_{2}\right) = \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed results are:

`\left|z_{1}z_{2}\right| = \left|z_{1}\right|\left|z_{2}\right|, \arg\left(z_{1}z_{2}\right) = \arg z_{1} + \arg z_{2}`

Dividing works the same way in reverse: the moduli are divided and the arguments subtracted.

## Card 23 — keyword_definition (`fl_98Qchd2b4vd9PrMc`)

**FRONT**

Define a **locus** on an Argand diagram.


**BACK**

A **locus** is the set of all points representing complex numbers $z$ that satisfy a given equation.

On the diagram it appears as a line or a curve, made up of every point that fits.


Spec links: `spcpt_PX3rkYhHsTmCcGVs`


## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_SstmZS4zsxq6bYSr`)

**FRONT**

What loci do `\text{Re}\left(z\right) = k` and `\text{Im}\left(z\right) = k` give?


**BACK**

`\text{Re}\left(z\right) = k` is the **vertical** line $x = k$, and `\text{Im}\left(z\right) = k` is the **horizontal** line $y = k$.

Every point on the first has real part $k$, and every point on the second has imaginary part $k$.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_nnDz6mSPfMvvnDyG`)

**FRONT**

What locus does `\left|z - a\right| = k` give, and why?


**BACK**

A **circle** of radius $k$ centred at the point $a$.

This is because `\left|z - a\right|` is the distance between the points representing $z$ and $a$, so the equation says every such $z$ sits a fixed distance $k$ away from $a$.

## Card 29 — question_and_answer (`fl_NK4FVGpVjYrdD2y9`)

**FRONT**

What locus does `\left|z - a\right| = \left|z - b\right|` give?


**BACK**

The **perpendicular bisector** of the line joining the points $a$ and $b$.

The equation says $z$ is equally far from $a$ and from $b$, and that is exactly the set of points making up the perpendicular bisector.

## Card 30 — question_and_answer (`fl_qpNW8fY5cxxSCXZ2`)

**FRONT**

What locus does `\arg\left(z - a\right) = \alpha` give?


**BACK**

A **half-line** starting at the point $a$, making an angle $α$ with the positive real axis.

The point $a$ itself is marked with a small **open circle**, since taking $z = a$ would require $\mathrm{arg} 0$.

When is a boundary drawn dotted on an Argand diagram, and when solid?


**BACK**

**Dotted** for a strict inequality, $<$ or $>$, because points on the boundary itself are not included.

**Solid** for $\leq$ or $\geq$, where the boundary points do satisfy the inequality.

## Card 35 — question_and_answer (`fl_hxTDT28FcJRxktND`)

**FRONT**

Which region does `\alpha < \arg\left(z - a\right) < \beta` describe?


**BACK**

A **wedge**-shaped region with its vertex at $a$, lying between the two half-lines at angles $α$ and $β$ measured from that point.

Only the part between them satisfies both inequalities at once.

## Card 36 — question_and_answer (`fl_m3MrQCMjfNjQ5QVS`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the region satisfying two inequalities at once?


**BACK**

Shade each region lightly on the same diagram, using diagonal lines running in a different direction for each one.

Where the two sets of lines cross is the **intersection**, the only part satisfying both, and that is what gets shaded boldly.

## Card 37 — true_or_false (`fl_Q9DKgYXsTSpp9k33`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every region on an Argand diagram has a greatest value of `\left|z\right|`.


**BACK**

**False.**

An **unbounded** region, such as the outside of a circle, stretches away without limit, so there is no greatest value.

At the other end, the least value is $0$ whenever the region contains the origin.

## Card 38 — question_and_answer (`fl_7F6kwyGyKhb9TdvR`)

**FRONT**

A region is the disc `\left|z - 5\text{i}\right| \le 2`. How do you find the greatest value of `\left|z\right|`?


**BACK**

Find the distance from the origin to the centre, here $5$, then **add** the radius, giving $7$.

Subtracting the radius instead gives the least value, $3$, and both work because the origin lies outside this circle.

## Card 39 — question_and_answer (`fl_S5kpwwHYPZ8hC52D`)

**FRONT**

A region is the disc `\left|z - 5\text{i}\right| \le 2`. How do you find the least value of $\mathrm{arg} z$?


**BACK**

Draw the tangent from the origin to the circle on the side giving the smallest angle.

A tangent meets a radius at a right angle, so $\mathrm{sin} α = \frac{2}{5}$ gives the angle $α$ between that tangent and the line from the origin to the centre.

The least argument is then $\frac{π}{2} - α = 1 . 16$ radians.
