## Card 1 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_rhDXKkZ3CVpfcp8F`)

**FRONT**

Complete the three reciprocal trigonometric functions:

`\sec x = \_\_\_\_\_\_`

`\text{cosec} x = \_\_\_\_\_\_`

`\cot x = \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed definitions are:

$\mathrm{sec} x = \frac{1}{\mathrm{cos}x}$

$\text{cosec} x = \frac{1}{\mathrm{sin}x}$

$\mathrm{cot} x = \frac{1}{\mathrm{tan}x}$

Cosecant is sometimes written $\text{csc} x$ instead of $\text{cosec} x$.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_CbDDRK98zkzP8Pqp`)

**FRONT**

Which regular function does $\text{cosec}$ pair with?


**BACK**

$\text{cosec}$ is built from **sine**, and $\mathrm{sec}$ from **cosine**, which is the opposite of what the names suggest.

A reliable check is the third letter of each name: the **s** in cosec points to sine, and the **c** in sec points to cosine.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_s66z3pPF5f3rQQzj`)

**FRONT**

How can $\mathrm{cot} x$ be written in terms of sine and cosine?


**BACK**

It can be written as $\mathrm{cot} x = \frac{\mathrm{cos}x}{\mathrm{sin}x}$.

That form is often more useful than the reciprocal one, because it lets you combine $\mathrm{cot}$ with other sine and cosine terms over a common denominator.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_rPk6yvp3jzPrDst8`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{sec} x$ is another name for the inverse function $\mathrm{cos}^{-1} x$.


**BACK**

**False.**

$\mathrm{sec} x$ is the **reciprocal** of the cosine, whereas $\mathrm{cos}^{-1} x$ is the angle whose cosine is $x$.

The two are quite different: $\mathrm{sec} 60 ^{\circ} = 2$, while $\mathrm{cos}^{-1} 0 . 5 = 60 ^{\circ}$.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_vxczYX2d478HCDPj`)

**FRONT**

How do you start solving an equation containing $\mathrm{sec}$, $\text{cosec}$ or $\mathrm{cot}$?


**BACK**

Rewrite every reciprocal function in terms of $\mathrm{sin}$, $\mathrm{cos}$ or $\mathrm{tan}$, then solve it as an ordinary trigonometric equation.

For an expression such as $\frac{1+\mathrm{cot}x}{1+\mathrm{tan}x}$, putting everything over sine and cosine lets the whole thing cancel down.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_GQxmFnsKYc753Gxn`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The equation $\mathrm{cot} x = 10$ becomes $\mathrm{tan} x = 10$.


**BACK**

**False.**

Taking the reciprocal of both sides gives $\mathrm{tan} x = \frac{1}{10}$, not $10$.

Swapping a reciprocal function for its regular one always flips the number on the other side as well.

## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_mj92Y4N8tCQFqSpV`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{cot} x$ can be equal to zero.


**BACK**

**True.**

At $x = 90 ^{\circ}$ the cosine is $0$ and the sine is $1$, so $\mathrm{cot} x = 0$.

Those are exactly the values where $\mathrm{tan} x$ is undefined, which is why the reciprocal form is misleading here.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_XwqY6FRPBDMtfMKV`)

**FRONT**

Where does a reciprocal trigonometric graph have its vertical asymptotes?


**BACK**

Wherever the **original function is zero**, since you cannot divide by zero.

So $\mathrm{sec} x$ has them where $\mathrm{cos} x = 0$, and $\text{cosec}  x$ where $\mathrm{sin} x = 0$.

## Card 9 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_3sbpYJrMTc5hWq4W`)

**FRONT**

The range of both $\mathrm{sec} x$ and $\text{cosec}  x$ is `y \leq \_\_\_\_\_\_` or `y \geq \_\_\_\_\_\_`.


**BACK**

The range of both is $y \leq - 1$ or $y \geq 1$.

Since $\mathrm{sin}$ and $\mathrm{cos}$ never exceed $1$ in size, their reciprocals can never be smaller than $1$ in size.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_ST93mjqVyNfBWrzy`)

**FRONT**

What are the periods of $\mathrm{sec} x$, $\text{cosec}  x$ and $\mathrm{cot} x$?


**BACK**

$\mathrm{sec} x$ and $\text{cosec}  x$ both repeat every $360 ^{\circ}$, or $2 π$ radians.

$\mathrm{cot} x$ repeats every $180 ^{\circ}$, or $π$ radians, just as $\mathrm{tan} x$ does.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_2MrffV9FhKqv2YjF`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{cot} x$, like $\mathrm{sec} x$, can never take a value between $- 1$ and $1$.


**BACK**

**False.**

$\mathrm{cot} x$ takes **every** real value, because $\mathrm{tan} x$ does too.

It is $\mathrm{sec}$ and $\text{cosec}$ that are restricted, not all three.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_cdBrP2cKz3MCMDVp`)

**FRONT**

How do you sketch a reciprocal trigonometric graph?


**BACK**

Sketch the original function first, then take the reciprocal of every value on it.

Where the original is large the reciprocal is close to zero, and where the original reaches $\pm 1$ the two graphs touch.

**FRONT**

Which reciprocal trigonometric graph is symmetrical about the $y$-axis?


**BACK**

Taking reciprocals does not disturb a symmetry the original graph already has.

## Card 14 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_gDC5wRBZF9bc8zZp`)

**FRONT**

The two reciprocal identities are:

`\tan^{2} x + 1 \equiv \_\_\_\_\_\_` and `1 + \cot^{2} x \equiv \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

$\mathrm{tan}^{2} x + 1 \equiv \mathrm{sec}^{2} x$ and $1 + \mathrm{cot}^{2} x \equiv \text{cosec}^{2} x$

Both follow from $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x + \mathrm{cos}^{2} x \equiv 1$, so neither has to be memorised separately.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_6DFthXpkd2BVsCSV`)

**FRONT**

How do you derive $\mathrm{tan}^{2} x + 1 \equiv \mathrm{sec}^{2} x$?


**BACK**

Divide every term of $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x + \mathrm{cos}^{2} x \equiv 1$ by $\mathrm{cos}^{2} x$.

That works because $\frac{\mathrm{sin}x}{\mathrm{cos}x} = \mathrm{tan} x$ and $\frac{1}{\mathrm{cos}x} = \mathrm{sec} x$.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_B4QKDwN4qWN3FWbp`)

**FRONT**

What do you divide $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x + \mathrm{cos}^{2} x \equiv 1$ by to reach the $\text{cosec}$ identity?


**BACK**

By $\mathrm{sin}^{2} x$.

That turns the first term into $1$, the second into $\mathrm{cot}^{2} x$ and the right-hand side into $\text{cosec}^{2} x$.

## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_Jd4fbQQRKxSTFd5h`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{sec}^{2} x - \mathrm{tan}^{2} x = 1$ wherever both are defined.


**BACK**

**True.**

It is $\mathrm{tan}^{2} x + 1 \equiv \mathrm{sec}^{2} x$ with the $\mathrm{tan}^{2} x$ moved across.

Spotting the rearranged forms inside a longer expression is what the identity is actually for.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_zkc7TNdb7vXcGkFH`)

**FRONT**

When are the reciprocal trigonometric identities needed?


**BACK**

When an expression mixes $\mathrm{sec}$, $\text{cosec}$ or $\mathrm{cot}$ with $\mathrm{tan}$, or with each other.

Substituting one of them removes a squared reciprocal term, which often collapses the whole expression.
