## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_kRgcMNKvzSnqxqQQ`)

**FRONT**

Define **partial fractions**.


**BACK**

Splitting a single algebraic fraction into a **sum of simpler fractions**, each having one factor of the original denominator underneath it.

It is the reverse of adding fractions, where a common denominator is formed instead.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_NKFF4tx9qGqHtVxF`)

**FRONT**

What is the first step in writing an expression as partial fractions?


**BACK**

**Factorise the denominator**, so that each factor can be given its own fraction.

Until the denominator is written as a product there is nothing to split it into.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_NvvNrRBJHwttqVv9`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the unknown constants in a partial fraction split?


**BACK**

Multiply through by the original denominator to clear the fractions, then substitute values of $x$ that make one bracket zero.

Each substitution removes one unknown and leaves the other on its own.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_Zc6SZb8DyWRndf8x`)

**FRONT**

What is the alternative to substituting values when finding partial fractions?


**BACK**

**Comparing coefficients.** The number of $x^{2}$ terms on each side must match, and likewise for the $x$ terms and the constants.

That produces simultaneous equations to solve for the unknowns.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_MDjVF6yJpxvMkX3s`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Partial fractions can be used on $\frac{3x-2}{5x^{2}+3x-2}$.


**BACK**

**True.**

The denominator factorises to `\left(5 x - 2\right) \left(x + 1\right)`, so it can be split.

A non-linear denominator is perfectly acceptable as long as it can be written as a product of linear factors.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_rCkPgfyDhs6YsVCB`)

**FRONT**

Where are partial fractions used later in the course?


**BACK**

In **binomial expansions** and in **integration**.

Both are far easier to carry out on a sum of simple fractions than on a single complicated one.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_JrQGhWCnZdRzkbDf`)

**FRONT**

For `\frac{4 x + 1}{\left(x + 2\right) \left(x - 1\right) \left(x - 3\right)^{2}}`, how many partial fractions are needed?


**BACK**

**Four**, not three.

The squared bracket contributes **two** factors, `\left(x - 3\right)` and `\left(x - 3\right)^{2}`, and each of them needs its own fraction.

## Card 10 — keyword_definition (`fl_Q76wM83NdNRbMPQB`)

**FRONT**

Define **squared linear factor**.


**BACK**

A factor of the form `\left(a x + b\right)^{2}`, that is a linear factor repeated.

The repetition is what makes it behave differently from two distinct linear factors.


Spec links: `spcpt_f5fqHB9YCMFNPmSz`


## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_JwsdfNqJbt83xHFZ`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

An $x^{2}$ in a denominator counts as a squared linear factor.


**BACK**

**True.**

A linear factor is `\left(a x + b\right)` and $b$ is allowed to be zero, so $x$ is linear and $x^{2}$ is its square.

Such a denominator therefore needs fractions over both $x$ and $x^{2}$.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_bZbkDTBTqWwXbp9y`)

**FRONT**

Why is `\frac{A}{x + 1} + \frac{B}{\left(x - 4\right)^{2}}` not a complete split of `\frac{5}{\left(x + 1\right) \left(x - 4\right)^{2}}`?


**BACK**

Because the term over `\left(x - 4\right)` is missing.

That leaves only two constants to match a numerator which in general needs three, so the identity cannot be made to hold for every value of $x$.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_HVx3RngvkwMhhDfJ`)

**FRONT**

Why does a quadratic factor need a linear numerator rather than a constant?


**BACK**

Because the numerator over any factor has to be one degree lower than the factor itself, and one degree below a quadratic is linear.

The same rule is what gives a linear factor its single constant on top, so this is not a special case but the general pattern.


Spec links: `spcpt_4JTpBrxmkXcFSsXD`


## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_h9S7JQ62xFSb74R4`)

**FRONT**

Which quadratic factors need a linear numerator rather than being split further?


**BACK**

Only those that **do not factorise**, such as $x^{2} + 4$ or $2 x^{2} + 3$.

On this course the quadratic factor usually appears with no $x$ term at all, in the form $c x^{2} + d$.


Spec links: `spcpt_4JTpBrxmkXcFSsXD`


## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_7XbWwFZtg3jx3fzk`)

**FRONT**

With one linear and one quadratic factor, why does substituting roots not find every constant?


**BACK**

Because only the **linear** factor has a root you can substitute, and that single substitution removes only one of the unknowns.

The quadratic does not factorise, so it offers no convenient value of $x$ that makes it vanish, and the remaining constants have to be reached another way.


Spec links: `spcpt_4JTpBrxmkXcFSsXD`


## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_74GyVJSxZdVP5mPs`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If the linear factor is $x$ itself, you can still use $x = 0$ as one of your two extra values.


**BACK**

**False.**

Putting $x = 0$ is already the root of the factor $x$, so it does the job of the first substitution and cannot then serve again as a second, independent equation.

You have to choose two other values of $x$ and solve the resulting pair simultaneously.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_pvZTjDcy4sy3qz7K`)

**FRONT**

Which values of $x$ should you substitute to find all three constants?


**BACK**

Start with the **root of the linear factor**, then use $x = 0$, then any small convenient value such as $x = 1$.

The first removes one unknown outright, and the other two give equations that pin down the remaining pair.
