## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_9DJhHHJBDWybvy7g`)

**FRONT**

Define **surd**.


**BACK**

A **surd** is the square root of a **non-square integer**, such as $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{13}$ or $\sqrt{99}$.

Its exact value cannot be written as a whole number or a fraction, which is why $\sqrt{16}$ is not a surd: it is simply $4$.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_Gb4jQ8tRrcyR4Gbm`)

**FRONT**

Why leave an answer as $5 \sqrt{2}$ rather than $7 . 071067811 \dots$?


**BACK**

$5 \sqrt{2}$ is the **exact** value, while any decimal version has been rounded and is therefore slightly wrong.

Working in surds all the way through a calculation keeps every step exact, so small rounding errors cannot build up.

## Card 3 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_zbQHmHSV5gc9BJjC`)

**FRONT**

Complete the two rules for multiplying and dividing surds:

`\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{\_\_\_\_\_\_} \text{ and } \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\_\_\_\_\_\_}`


**BACK**

The completed rules are:

$\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab} \text{and} \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}$

Multiplication and division can be carried out under a single square root, so $\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{5} = \sqrt{15}$ and $\sqrt{21} \div \sqrt{7} = \sqrt{3}$.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_GqKyvFnZBn4tNKFv`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$ can always be simplified to $\sqrt{a+b}$.


**BACK**

**False.**

Numbers settle it at once: $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{4} = 3 + 2 = 5$, whereas $\sqrt{9+4} = \sqrt{13} = 3 . 6055 \dots$

Subtraction behaves the same way, so $\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}$ is **not** $\sqrt{a-b}$ either.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_dmdcmzfYnJ4VsYn4`)

**FRONT**

How do you write $\sqrt{48}$ in its simplest surd form?


**BACK**

Split off the **greatest square factor** and take its root: $\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16\times3} = \sqrt{16} \times \sqrt{3} = 4 \sqrt{3}$.

The number left under the root must have **no square factors** of its own.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_R9tpyHRZsyHvVKd5`)

**FRONT**

When simplifying a surd, what if you do not spot the greatest square factor first time?


**BACK**

Simply carry on: take out whatever square factor you did find, then check the number left underneath for square factors of its own.

Using $9$ gives $\sqrt{450} = 3 \sqrt{50}$, and $50$ still has the square factor $25$, so $3 \sqrt{50} = 15 \sqrt{2}$.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_gbnRcTmTd42nYPFH`)

**FRONT**

Why must $\sqrt{32}$ and $\sqrt{8}$ be simplified before they can be added?


**BACK**

Because only **like surds** can be collected, in just the way that like terms in algebra are collected.

Once simplified both turn out to be multiples of $\sqrt{2}$: $\sqrt{32} = 4 \sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8} = 2 \sqrt{2}$, so the sum is $6 \sqrt{2}$.

## Card 8 — keyword_definition (`fl_M7KCr63tRTwp8sYg`)

**FRONT**

Define **rationalising the denominator**.


**BACK**

**Rationalising the denominator** means rewriting a fraction as an equivalent one whose denominator is a **rational number** rather than a surd.

The value of the fraction is unchanged; only its form is different, with any surds now sitting in the **numerator**.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_dGDSwkrrWXTqFFPv`)

**FRONT**

How do you rationalise the denominator of $\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}}$?


**BACK**

Multiply the top and the bottom by $\sqrt{b}$, the surd that is in the denominator.

The denominator then becomes $\sqrt{b} \times \sqrt{b} = b$, which is rational, so $\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}$.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_6xZfYvWy2p4vKxzP`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}$ and $\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}$ are equal.


**BACK**

**True.**

Multiplying a fraction by something equal to $1$ leaves its **value unchanged**, however different the result is made to look.

Both expressions come to $0 . 4472 \dots$, so rationalising changes only the form of a number, never the number itself.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_SHQhGVNQ273tRCHT`)

**FRONT**

What do you multiply by to rationalise $\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{3}}$?


**BACK**

Multiply the top and the bottom by $1 - \sqrt{3}$, the same expression with the **sign in the middle changed**.

That expression is called the **conjugate** of the denominator.

## Card 12 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_WdfhkqZBhZHwGW5k`)

**FRONT**

Complete the result that makes the conjugate method work, filling in the missing **index** and **term**:

`\left(a + \sqrt{b}\right)\left(a - \sqrt{b}\right) = a^{\_\_\_\_\_\_} - \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed result is:

`\left(a + \sqrt{b}\right)\left(a - \sqrt{b}\right) = a^{2} - b`

This is the **difference of two squares**: the middle terms $- a \sqrt{b}$ and $+ a \sqrt{b}$ cancel each other, and that is what removes the surd.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_BQMsgKvC7RGzSF3S`)

**FRONT**

Write $\frac{4}{\sqrt{6}-2}$ in the form $p + q \sqrt{r}$.


**BACK**

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $\sqrt{6} + 2$:

`\frac{4\left(\sqrt{6} + 2\right)}{\left(\sqrt{6} - 2\right)\left(\sqrt{6} + 2\right)} = \frac{4\left(\sqrt{6} + 2\right)}{6 - 4} = 2\left(\sqrt{6} + 2\right)`

Expanding gives $4 + 2 \sqrt{6}$, so $p = 4$, $q = 2$ and $r = 6$.
