## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_9xfZTWhqHGvvqtvm`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a sketch of a graph and a drawing of one?


**BACK**

A **sketch** shows the correct general shape and the key points, but need not be to scale or plotted precisely.

A **drawing** is accurate: points are plotted exactly, straight lines are ruled, and the whole curve is to scale.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_RBYYy4CM645P3GvV`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the intercepts of `y = \text{f}\left(x\right)` with the two axes?


**BACK**

Put $x = 0$ to get the $y$-intercept, and set $y = 0$ and solve to get the $x$-intercepts.

The $x$-intercepts are also called the **roots** of the function, and there may be none, one or many of them.

## Card 4 — keyword_definition (`fl_VBSn9YzxykkXcTHb`)

**FRONT**

Define **local maximum** and **local minimum**.


**BACK**

A **local maximum** is a point higher than everything immediately around it, and a **local minimum** is a point lower than everything around it.

The word local matters: neither has to be the highest or lowest point on the whole graph.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_5HryD52yRWQFNfMr`)

**FRONT**

Where on the graph of `y = \text{f}\left(x\right)` do turning points occur?


**BACK**

Wherever the gradient is zero, that is where `\text{f}'\left(x\right) = 0`.

They are the peaks and valleys of the curve, and a graph may have several of them or none at all.

## Card 6 — keyword_definition (`fl_Wsd3TTy6qgbrDCzq`)

**FRONT**

Define **asymptote**.


**BACK**

An **asymptote** is a line that a graph gets steadily closer to without ever meeting it.

Asymptotes may be **horizontal** or **vertical**, and are usually shown as dashed lines on a sketch.


Spec links: `spcpt_KFsJJKMjjJYrfN4h`


## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_xjmBmtsVk5WwFM4Z`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every graph with a vertical asymptote also has a horizontal one.


**BACK**

**False.**

A logarithmic graph has a vertical asymptote at the $y$-axis but climbs without limit, so it has no horizontal asymptote at all.

The two kinds are independent: a curve may have one, the other, both, or neither.


Spec links: `spcpt_KFsJJKMjjJYrfN4h`


## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_H9knp66xvRQJkdVv`)

**FRONT**

Where is the axis of symmetry of $y = a x^{2} + b x + c$?


**BACK**

It is the vertical line $x = - \frac{b}{2a}$, which also passes through the vertex.

When the curve has two roots $p$ and $q$, that line sits exactly halfway between them, at $x = \frac{p+q}{2}$.


Spec links: `spcpt_3C6JG8MntWkWNfpJ`


## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_FT3DQwjMV9wnFVjS`)

**FRONT**

How does the sign of $a$ affect the graph of $y = a x^{2} + b x + c$?


**BACK**

A **positive** $a$ gives a U-shaped parabola opening upwards, and a **negative** $a$ turns it upside down.

The size of $a$ also stretches the curve vertically, making it narrower as $a$ gets larger.


Spec links: `spcpt_3C6JG8MntWkWNfpJ`


## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_dyCJkBT5PW2Scx9s`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The vertex of a quadratic graph is always its $y$-intercept.


**BACK**

**False.**

The $y$-intercept is at `\left(0 , c\right)` while the vertex sits on the line $x = - \frac{b}{2a}$, and the two agree only when $b = 0$.

In that special case the curve is symmetric about the $y$-axis, so the two points really do coincide.


Spec links: `spcpt_3C6JG8MntWkWNfpJ`


## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_HGq39ZK7fMn6j8Q4`)

**FRONT**

Which form of a quadratic would you use if you know its two roots?


**BACK**

The **factorised form** `y = a\left(x - p\right)\left(x - q\right)`, which shows the roots $p$ and $q$ directly.

One further point on the curve is still needed to pin down $a$, since every value of $a$ gives that same pair of roots.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_Csd5R4WKRtqdBrfQ`)

**FRONT**

How does the sign of $a$ affect the ends of the graph of $y = a x^{3} + b x^{2} + c x + d$?


**BACK**

A **positive** cubic comes up from the bottom left and goes off towards the top right.

A **negative** cubic does the reverse, starting at the top left and falling away to the bottom right.

## Card 13 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_PPWT4XtpsqsSRH7q`)

**FRONT**

For $y=ax^{3}+bx^{2}+cx+d$, complete the equation that locates the turning points:

`_ _ _ _ _ _ a x squared plus _ _ _ _ _ _ b x plus c equals 0`


**BACK**

The completed equation is:

$3 a x^{2} + 2 b x + c = 0$

It is the derivative set equal to zero, so a cubic has **two turning points when this quadratic has two unique solutions, and none otherwise** — a repeated solution gives a point of inflection, not a turning point.

**FRONT**

What is the first thing to find when sketching a cubic graph?


**BACK**

The $y$-intercept, at `\left(0 , d\right)`, because putting $x = 0$ leaves only the constant term.

Then find the roots by setting $y = 0$ and factorising, and settle the direction of the ends from the sign of $a$.

## Card 15 — keyword_definition (`fl_qBkggGq87pbT2p7h`)

**FRONT**

Define **linear rational function**.


**BACK**

A **linear rational function** has the form `\text{f}\left(x\right) = \frac{a x + b}{c x + d}`, one linear expression divided by another.

The reciprocal function `\text{f}\left(x\right) = \frac{1}{x}` is the simplest case of it.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_XRwY2MxsgkRKhdrd`)

**FRONT**

Where is the vertical asymptote of `\text{f}\left(x\right) = \frac{a x + b}{c x + d}`?


**BACK**

At $x = - \frac{d}{c}$, the value that makes the **denominator zero**.

That same value is the one number missing from the domain, since the function has no value there at all.


Spec links: `spcpt_wvcV6hScKCRRMSfH`


## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_hxXYBn28DNy5t8s9`)

**FRONT**

Where is the horizontal asymptote of `\text{f}\left(x\right) = \frac{a x + b}{c x + d}`?


**BACK**

At $y = \frac{a}{c}$, the ratio of the two coefficients of $x$.

Once $x$ is very large, positive or negative, the constants $b$ and $d$ barely matter and the function behaves like $\frac{ax}{cx}$.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_DJ7KSpb2Gz6mS2Ny`)

**FRONT**

Why can $\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ never equal $\frac{a}{c}$?


**BACK**

Because $y = \frac{a}{c}$ is the horizontal asymptote, which the curve approaches but never reaches.

So the **range** is every real number except $\frac{a}{c}$, just as the domain leaves out one value.

## Card 20 — true_or_false (`fl_jsKFTFNMY24yGTm7`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A horizontal line can cross a linear rational graph at most once.


**BACK**

**True.**

A linear rational function takes each of its values just once, so no horizontal line can meet the curve twice.

The single horizontal line that misses the graph entirely is its horizontal asymptote.

**FRONT**

Find both asymptotes of `\text{f}\left(x\right) = \frac{10 - 5 x}{x + 2}`.


**BACK**

The denominator is zero at $x = - 2$, so that is the **vertical** asymptote.

For large $x$ the function behaves like $\frac{-5x}{x} = - 5$, so the **horizontal** asymptote is $y = - 5$.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_5X5RMx3kdVCNqTfg`)

**FRONT**

When would you solve an equation graphically, and what is the cost?


**BACK**

When the equation cannot be rearranged into anything algebra can solve, which happens readily once logarithms and exponentials appear together.

The cost is accuracy: the answer is only an **estimate**, no better than the drawing and the reading of the scale.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_5q2z69ZkJCW7kyqm`)

**FRONT**

Given a graph of `y = \text{f}\left(x\right)`, how do you solve `\text{f}\left(x\right) = k`?


**BACK**

Draw the horizontal line $y = k$ and read off the $x$ coordinates wherever it meets the curve.

Finding **roots** is the special case $k = 0$, where that horizontal line is the $x$-axis itself.

## Card 25 — true_or_false (`fl_4tmFwgbhkCctqRyK`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A horizontal line is always the right line to draw when solving an equation from a graph.


**BACK**

**False.**

A horizontal line only works when the curve already plotted is of the whole expression you are setting equal to a number.

Otherwise the equation has to be rearranged first, and the line that comes out of that is usually a sloping one.

## Card 26 — question_and_answer (`fl_8HCCHmcgN6ZbM34t`)

**FRONT**

How do you work out which straight line to draw on a given graph?


**BACK**

Rearrange the equation you are solving until one whole side is **exactly** the function already plotted.

Whatever is left on the other side is the line to draw, and the crossing points then give the solutions you want.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_PKNgGxQFmS4GF6PN`)

**FRONT**

Which line should be drawn on the graph of `y = 2^{\left(\frac{x}{3} + 1\right)} - 1` to solve `\log_{2}\left(3 x - 1\right)^{2} - \frac{2}{3} x = 2`?


**BACK**

Rearranging gives `\log_{2}\left(3 x - 1\right) = \frac{x}{3} + 1`, then `3 x - 1 = 2^{\left(\frac{x}{3} + 1\right)}`, and finally `3 x - 2 = 2^{\left(\frac{x}{3} + 1\right)} - 1`.

The right-hand side is now the plotted curve, so the line to draw is $y = 3 x - 2$.
