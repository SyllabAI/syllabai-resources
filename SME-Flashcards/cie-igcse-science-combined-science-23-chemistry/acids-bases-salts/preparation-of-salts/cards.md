## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_5DDnh5VTTCNfgxMQ`)

**FRONT**

What is a **salt**?


**BACK**

A **salt** is a compound formed when the hydrogen atom(s) in an acid are replaced by a metal ion or ammonium ion. For example, replacing H in HCl with sodium gives sodium chloride (NaCl).

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_4rVn54H33YXnQpR6`)

**FRONT**

What two factors determine which method is used to prepare a soluble salt?


**BACK**

The method depends on the **solubility** of the salt and whether the base is **soluble (an alkali)** or **insoluble**. Soluble bases require titration; insoluble reactants use the excess solid method.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_HjZMdP6797TYS4RK`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Titration is used to prepare a soluble salt from a soluble alkali because excess alkali cannot be removed by filtration.


**BACK**

**True.**

Since alkalis are soluble, any excess would remain in the salt solution and could not be filtered off. **Titration** finds the exact volume of acid needed to neutralise the alkali, so no excess remains.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_pCWDkNdTXczM9cxz`)

**FRONT**

In a titration to prepare a pure soluble salt, why is the final titration repeated without indicator?


**BACK**

The indicator is omitted in the final titration so that it does not contaminate the **pure salt** solution. The volume of acid needed is already known from the first titration, so no indicator is required.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_jyD4y6tqZchX5Bcd`)

**FRONT**

How is the name of the salt produced in a reaction determined?


**BACK**

The first part of the salt name comes from the **metal** (or metal oxide/carbonate) and the second part comes from the **acid**. For example, hydrochloric acid always gives salts ending in **chloride**.

## Card 8 — true_or_false (`fl_9hGmHxTQtBmfhBCs`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

When using excess metal to prepare a salt, the excess metal is removed by evaporation.


**BACK**

**False.**

Excess metal is a solid and is removed by **filtration**. Evaporation is used afterwards to concentrate the salt solution before crystallisation.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_3fr9CpHPNyjGqzWV`)

**FRONT**

Describe how to check that a salt solution is saturated and ready to crystallise.


**BACK**

Dip a cold **glass rod** into the hot solution. If crystals form on the rod, the solution is saturated and ready to be left to **crystallise**.

## Card 10 — keyword_definition (`fl_FgYHH3gZQGfPwWgC`)

**FRONT**

What is a **precipitation reaction**?

**(Extended Tier Only)**


**BACK**

A **precipitation reaction** is a reaction in which two soluble solutions are mixed and an insoluble solid (the **precipitate**) forms. It is used to prepare insoluble salts.


Spec links: `spcpt_nYf8Wb3scdB8fBdt`


## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_bqSsm4j2r5jDKMDm`)

**FRONT**

Why must both reactants be soluble when preparing an insoluble salt by precipitation?

**(Extended Tier Only)**


**BACK**

Both reactants must be soluble so they can mix freely in solution, allowing the ions to collide and react. If either reactant were insoluble, the ions could not come into contact to form the **precipitate**.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_6fSVPZ4d2HKDCydv`)

**FRONT**

Why is the precipitation method used to prepare insoluble salts rather than the excess solid or titration method?

**(Extended Tier Only)**


**BACK**

The excess solid and titration methods produce salts in solution, which can only be recovered if the salt is **soluble**. An insoluble salt would simply settle out as a solid, so mixing two **soluble solutions** to form the insoluble precipitate directly is more practical.

**True or False?**

The solid formed in a precipitation reaction is called the filtrate.

**(Extended Tier Only)**


**BACK**

The insoluble solid formed is called the **precipitate**. The **filtrate** is the liquid that passes through the filter paper.
