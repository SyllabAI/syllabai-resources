## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_sKdXSfjQXxz3GqFB`)

**FRONT**

Define **bivariate** data.


**BACK**

Bivariate data records **two variables** for each item, such as the height and the arm span of every person in a group.

It is displayed on a **scatter diagram**, where each item contributes a single point plotted from its two values.

Every other diagram in this topic handles one variable at a time.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_TyYDQwxDWRhTpt8m`)

**FRONT**

Before choosing a diagram to display a set of data, what three things about the data or the task do you need to establish?


**BACK**

Three things decide it:

- whether the data is **raw and ungrouped** or has been **grouped** into classes
- whether it is **discrete** or **continuous**
- whether you are displaying a single set of data or **comparing** two

The third of these is the one most often forgotten, because a diagram that shows one set of data well is not always the one that lets two sets be read against each other.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_rG4Xwr7HvZDgFrXJ`)

**FRONT**

Two diagrams are drawn from the same data. One shows every individual value and the other shows only a handful of summary values. What does the second one gain by throwing information away?


**BACK**

It becomes far quicker to **read at a glance**, and far easier to set beside a second data set for comparison.

A diagram showing every value carries more information, but it takes longer to interpret and two of them side by side are awkward to compare.

Choosing a diagram is always this trade-off: how much detail it keeps against how quickly it can be read.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_M2k238ZBg8crq8h9`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Two bar charts drawn from exactly the same figures can give very different impressions of the data.


**BACK**

**True.**

The scale on the vertical axis is a **choice**: starting it well above zero, or stretching it, makes small differences between the bars look dramatic.

Nothing about the figures has changed, which is why the axes, their units and their labels have to be read before any conclusion is drawn from the picture.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_PYCzcsnQqpCSmskj`)

**FRONT**

A graph's vertical axis is labelled **Population (millions)**, and one of its bars reaches 60. What is the population?


**BACK**

**60 000 000**, because the axis label says the numbers along it have been given in millions, so each unit on the scale stands for 1 000 000.

## Card 6 — keyword_definition (`fl_93TTQf59HfR8FNcJ`)

**FRONT**

Define a **stem and leaf diagram**.


**BACK**

A stem and leaf diagram shows **every raw data value**, sorted into class intervals.

The **stem** is the leading digit or digits of a value and the **leaf** is its final digit, so a stem of 2 with a leaf of 3 might represent 23.

The leaves are written in order along each row, so the individual values and the shape of the distribution are both visible at once.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_crndqFsPTHJZgwK4`)

**FRONT**

Why must a stem and leaf diagram always carry a **key**?


**BACK**

Because the stems and the leaves are only digits, and nothing in the diagram itself says what **place value** they stand for.

A key saying that a stem of 2 with a leaf of 3 represents 2.3 settles it, since the same row would otherwise be read as 23, or as 230.

Leaves are always **single digits**, so the key also shows how each value is split between its stem and its leaf.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_N5ZCXtmW7RcHs4Zb`)

**FRONT**

Starting from an unordered list of data, why is a stem and leaf diagram usually drawn **twice**?


**BACK**

The first version simply gets every value into the right row, without worrying about order, and that is the stage where a value is most easily missed or written down twice.

The second version is then rewritten with the leaves in **order** within each row, and given its key.

Splitting the job in two makes it much less likely that a value is lost, and only the ordered version can be used to find the median and the quartiles.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 9 — true_or_false (`fl_yrd9FHM7ZyWGpHgH`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The numbers in brackets down the side of a stem and leaf diagram are the values in that row added together.


**BACK**

**False.**

They are the **frequency** of the row: how many leaves it contains.

They are not always included, but with a large amount of data they are worth having, both as a check that no value has been lost and as a quick way of counting along to the position of the median.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_YZqrdKgfGvqRvyYr`)

**FRONT**

On a back-to-back stem and leaf diagram the two sets of leaves share one central column of stems. How do you read the values on the **left-hand** side?


**BACK**

Outwards from the stem, so the left-hand leaves **increase from the centre towards the edge**, which is the opposite direction to ordinary reading.

The leaf nearest the stem is therefore the **smallest** value in that row, not the largest.

A single key covers both sides, saying what a stem and leaf mean for each of the two groups.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_ybC8fTsGThmKrMjf`)

**FRONT**

A stem and leaf diagram holds so much data that its rows have become very long. What is usually done, and what does each row then hold?


**BACK**

Every stem is **listed twice**, so its data is spread over two rows instead of one.

The first row for a stem holds the leaves **0 to 4** and the second holds the leaves **5 to 9**.

That halves the width of each class interval, which brings the shape of the distribution out more clearly.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_Kn9KDspmQ3JmvtsG`)

**FRONT**

A stem and leaf diagram has a row labelled LOW and a row labelled HIGH, each holding a value written out in full rather than as a leaf. What are those rows for?


**BACK**

They hold the **outliers**, kept outside the main body of the diagram.

Writing an outlier out in full avoids having to add a long run of empty stems just to reach one extreme value.

The stems in between then stay close together, so the shape of the rest of the data can still be seen.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_7x8Y69xCMNtJqvWj`)

**FRONT**

Why can the median and the quartiles be found **exactly** from a stem and leaf diagram, when a grouped frequency table only gives estimates of them?


**BACK**

Because a stem and leaf diagram keeps every raw value, and keeps them in order, so you can count along to the position you need and read off the value actually sitting there.

Grouping into a frequency table throws the individual values away and records only how many fell into each class, so anything worked out from it has to be estimated.

That is why a stem and leaf diagram is such a convenient starting point when a box plot is wanted.


Spec links: `spcpt_7T9ryhpxywvH3PVX`


## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_PgknXpMPGcCWVpxR`)

**FRONT**

Which values can be read from a box plot, and which cannot?


**BACK**

You can read the **minimum**, the **lower quartile**, the **median**, the **upper quartile** and the **maximum**, together with any **outliers**.

No other individual data value can be read from a box plot: it shows the shape of the distribution, not the data itself.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_PxmKrpFk2JhKj5KV`)

**FRONT**

What proportion of the data lies inside the box of a box plot, and what proportion lies along each whisker?


**BACK**

The box holds the middle **50%** of the data, between the lower and upper quartiles.

Each whisker holds **25%**: the lowest quarter of the data on one side and the highest quarter on the other.

A box plot drawn with a deeper box represents a larger set of data.

The box can be drawn to any depth, and the depth carries no information at all.

How is an outlier shown on a box plot, and where does the whisker end when there is one?


**BACK**

The outlier is marked with a **cross** beyond the end of the whisker.

The whisker then stops at the most extreme value that is **not** an outlier, so it no longer reaches the smallest or largest value in the data set.

Two sets of data are to be compared using box plots. How should the two box plots be presented?


**BACK**

One above the other, drawn against the **same scale** on the horizontal axis, with each one clearly labelled.

## Card 19 — keyword_definition (`fl_gchB6pYQkkJ562fh`)

**FRONT**

Define **cumulative frequency**.


**BACK**

The **running total** of the frequencies: the frequency of a class added to the frequencies of all the classes below it.


Spec links: `spcpt_scsQmXgJygJKWzhy`


## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_qCKRmRZpGK3SrJrC`)

**FRONT**

When a cumulative frequency graph is plotted, which value of each class is the cumulative frequency plotted against, and why?


**BACK**

The **upper boundary** of the class.

The cumulative frequency counts every value in that class as well as every value in the classes below it, so that total is only reached at the top of the class.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_kSpDZdBbR6PSYH6F`)

**FRONT**

What is plotted on the vertical axis of a histogram, and why is it not the frequency?


**BACK**

**Frequency density**.

The classes of a histogram may have **different widths**, so it is the **area** of each bar that represents the frequency, not its height. Plotting frequency density is what makes the areas come out in proportion to the frequencies.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

In a histogram the bars for adjacent classes always touch.


**BACK**

**True.**

A histogram displays **grouped continuous** data, so each class ends where the next begins and there is no gap between the bars.

A bar chart, which displays **discrete** or **qualitative** data, does have gaps between its bars.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_bWNpbQ8wNj4kwjv2`)

**FRONT**

A grouped frequency table gives the classes as 1 to 5, 6 to 10 and 11 to 15. What must you do before you can find the class widths for a histogram?


**BACK**

**Close the gaps** between the classes first, so that the upper boundary of one class is the lower boundary of the next.

Check whether the values were **rounded** or **truncated** before deciding where the boundaries lie.

For data rounded to the nearest whole number the boundaries become 0.5 to 5.5, 5.5 to 10.5 and 10.5 to 15.5, each of **class width 5**.

## Card 28 — question_and_answer (`fl_rrXYtQBVGyWCGVWx`)

**FRONT**

A histogram question tells you that the area of each bar is **proportional** to the frequency rather than equal to it. How do you find the frequencies?


**BACK**

Use a class whose frequency you are given to find the **scale factor**: work out the area of that bar and compare it with the frequency it represents.

That tells you how many data values one unit of area stands for, and every other bar can then be converted in the same way.

A question of this kind will always give you enough information to find the scale factor.

**FRONT**

Define an **outlier**.


**BACK**

An outlier is an extreme data value that does not fit the general pattern of the rest of the data.

## Card 33 — question_and_answer (`fl_NWCBYHMNKtFchBgp`)

**FRONT**

Outliers are not always defined using the quartiles. What is the other definition you may be given, and what is $k$ typically in it?


**BACK**

A value counts as an outlier if it lies more than $k$ **standard deviations** from the mean, so below $\overline{x} - k σ$ or above $\overline{x} + k σ$.

Here $k$ is commonly 2, where for the interquartile-range definition it is commonly 1.5.

The question tells you which definition to use and what $k$ is, and the two need not flag the same values in the same data set.

## Card 34 — question_and_answer (`fl_3CdM7trJgJV8hZf8`)

**FRONT**

The ages of children at a party include a value of 29, which the outlier test flags. Should it be removed from the data set?


**BACK**

**Yes**, because 29 cannot be the age of a child, so it is almost certainly an **error** in the data.

The general rule is that an outlier is kept if it is a valid piece of data and removed if it is likely to be a mistake, so the decision comes from the **context** rather than from the test.

A flagged value of 13 at the same party would be investigated rather than deleted, because a 13-year-old there is unusual rather than impossible.

## Card 35 — question_and_answer (`fl_7XRGjmG9DWhDtCSN`)

**FRONT**

A single outlier is added to a data set. What happens to the **range**, and what happens to the **interquartile range**?


**BACK**

The range is changed **completely**, because it is the largest value minus the smallest and the outlier immediately becomes one of those two.

The interquartile range is essentially **unaffected**, because it depends only on the two quartiles, which sit well inside the data.

That is what makes the interquartile range the more reliable measure of spread whenever a data set may contain extreme values.

## Card 37 — keyword_definition (`fl_VyjYYgvWyKjJNWFd`)

**FRONT**

Define a **measure of spread**.


**BACK**

A measure of spread gives information about how **varied** a set of data is, rather than about where it sits.

The range, the interquartile range, the standard deviation and the variance are all measures of spread.

A smaller value means the data is more **consistent**, and a larger one means it is more varied.

When comparing two data sets, what **two** kinds of statistic must your comparison mention, and which ones pair together?

A measure of **location** and a measure of **spread**, one of each.

## Card 39 — question_and_answer (`fl_S8qFfK9GyqmxhZCh`)

**FRONT**

A data set contains extreme values. Which pairing of location and spread should be used to describe it, and why?


**BACK**

The **median with the interquartile range**, because both are resistant to extreme values while the mean and the standard deviation are not.

The mean and the standard deviation are each worked out from every value in the set, so a single extreme one pulls both of them away from where the bulk of the data actually sits.

Use the mean and the standard deviation instead when the data is roughly symmetrical and free of extreme values.

## Card 41 — question_and_answer (`fl_vP2pcVyzczv8CpxJ`)

**FRONT**

A value is added to a data set. What decides whether the mean goes up or down?


**BACK**

Whether the new value is above or below the **current mean**: one above it pulls the mean up, and one below it pulls the mean down.

Removing a value does the opposite, so taking out a value from above the mean pulls the mean down.

A value exactly equal to the mean leaves it unchanged.

## Card 42 — true_or_false (`fl_RsDn8qWk8QJTnz7S`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Adding a very extreme value to a data set moves the median by about as much as adding an ordinary value would.


**BACK**

**True.**

The median is decided by **position**, so a new value counts once wherever it lands, and a value of 500 shifts the middle position by exactly as much as a value of 5 landing on the same side of it.

It is the **mean** that behaves differently, because it is built from the sizes of the values rather than their positions, so an extreme one moves it a long way.

## Card 43 — question_and_answer (`fl_bJQmYxRJsZmJj8Gk`)

**FRONT**

A value is added to a data set. Why can you not say in advance whether the median will change?


**BACK**

Because the median is decided by **position**, and adding a value shifts every position above where it lands while leaving the ones below it alone.

Whether the middle position ends up on a different value depends on where in the ordered data the new one falls, so each case has to be checked individually.

The same is true of the quartiles.

## Card 44 — keyword_definition (`fl_m3hGHG5p4MQMfHp9`)

**FRONT**

Define **skewness**.


**BACK**

Skewness describes the way a **non-symmetrical** distribution leans.

A distribution whose tail stretches out to the **right** has **positive** skew, and one whose tail stretches out to the **left** has **negative** skew.

Any distribution is either symmetrical or it has skewness of one of these two kinds.


Spec links: `spcpt_3pFxhzpdSvQ8YGSD`


## Card 45 — true_or_false (`fl_C8QpXppPsqqtQbD3`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A positively skewed distribution has most of its data concentrated at the high end.


**BACK**

**False.**

The skew is named after the direction of the **tail**, not of the bulk of the data.

In a positively skewed distribution most of the data is bunched at the **low** end, and it is the long thin tail that stretches away to the right.


Spec links: `spcpt_3pFxhzpdSvQ8YGSD`


## Card 46 — question_and_answer (`fl_rNRszqpbpywpH9DD`)

**FRONT**

How do the gaps between the three quartiles tell you which way a distribution is skewed?


**BACK**

Compare $Q_{2} - Q_{1}$ with $Q_{3} - Q_{2}$, which on a box plot are the widths of the two halves of the box.

If the median sits closer to the **lower** quartile, so that $Q_{2} - Q_{1} < Q_{3} - Q_{2}$, the skew is **positive**.

If it sits closer to the **upper** quartile, so that $Q_{2} - Q_{1} > Q_{3} - Q_{2}$, the skew is **negative**.


Spec links: `spcpt_3pFxhzpdSvQ8YGSD`


## Card 47 — question_and_answer (`fl_dB3VhdHSp9gkxsxz`)

**FRONT**

In a positively skewed distribution, how do the mean, the median and the mode compare in size, and why do they come out in that order?


**BACK**

They come out as $\text{mode} < \text{median} < \text{mean}$.

The **mode** sits at the peak, which is down at the low end, while the **mean** is dragged out towards the long tail because it is worked out from every value including the extreme ones lying in that tail.

The **median** settles between the two, since it moves with position rather than with size; for negative skew the whole order reverses, giving $\text{mean} < \text{median} < \text{mode}$.


Spec links: `spcpt_3pFxhzpdSvQ8YGSD`


## Card 48 — question_and_answer (`fl_J3YNfmKWXptvQQrN`)

**FRONT**

A distribution is **symmetrical**. What does that tell you about its quartiles, and about its three averages?


**BACK**

The median sits exactly midway between the quartiles, so $Q_{2} - Q_{1} = Q_{3} - Q_{2}$ and the two halves of the box on a box plot come out equal.

The mean, the median and the mode are all equal, or very nearly so, because there is no tail on either side to drag the mean away from the peak.

Symmetry is the case that skewness is measured against, which is why the quartile test and the ordering of the averages are both written as comparisons rather than as formulae.


Spec links: `spcpt_3pFxhzpdSvQ8YGSD`
