## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_QnPyWdSVNmrNbWyM`)

**FRONT**

Define a **local maximum** of a graph.


**BACK**

A **local maximum** is a **turning point** where the curve stops rising and begins to fall, so it is the highest point in its own neighbourhood.

It need not be the highest point on the whole graph, which is exactly what the word **local** is there to signal.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_4GYx3JvWdDQj22cz`)

**FRONT**

What key features can the graph analysis menu on a calculator find for you?


**BACK**

It finds the **zeros** (also called roots), the **local maximum and minimum points**, and the **intersection** of two graphs.

On some models this menu is called **analyze graph**, and on others it is reached with the **G-Solv** button.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_v4S8jgyRdCNtcJwQ`)

**FRONT**

Why must you click either side of a point before the calculator will find it?


**BACK**

The calculator searches only inside the interval you have marked, so the two clicks tell it **where to look**.

A graph can have several zeros or several turning points, and this is how you say **which one** you want.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_j7RdTj4rRGftmgHR`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

To sketch a graph from your calculator you should first make a table of values and plot the points.


**BACK**

**False.**

A **sketch** needs no plotted points and no table of values at all.

You copy the shape as a **single smooth freehand curve** and mark the key features on it, and you do not use a ruler for a curve.

Why is a calculator especially useful for a function you have never seen before?


**BACK**

That is how you would handle something like $y = x^{\frac{1}{2}}$ without ever having met that curve.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_9tKG6YWj7k5mFRym`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` using a graphical method?


**BACK**

Draw `y = \text{f}_{1} \left(x\right)` and `y = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` on the same axes, then find their **points of intersection**.

The solutions are the $x$-coordinates of those points, and not the full coordinate pairs.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_8y7HHqpvyYfDKFVP`)

**FRONT**

How do you put a second graph onto the same axes on a calculator?


**BACK**

Enter it on the next function line down, `\text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)`, underneath the first one.

The **tab** key is often a shortcut for opening that second line, and both curves are then drawn together.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_gSWsXrnsyV7BbRdN`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Solving `\text{f} \left(x\right) = 0` on a graph means finding where the curve crosses the $x$-axis.


**BACK**

**True.**

The right-hand side is $y = 0$, which is the equation of the $x$-axis itself.

The intersections you are looking for are therefore the curve's $x$-intercepts, which the calculator calls its **zeros**.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_nXybyH3RPBqNKhG7`)

**FRONT**

To solve $x^{2} + 3 x + 1 = 1$ on a calculator, what second graph do you draw?


**BACK**

You draw $y = 1$, which is a **horizontal line** cutting the $y$-axis at 1.

A constant on one side of an equation is always a horizontal line, however complicated the other side happens to be.

## Card 13 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_kQFsN2HSYXGtMxWm`)

A calculator offers two different routes to the solutions of an equation. Complete the sentence naming them:

Finding where two graphs cross is a `\_\_\_\_\_\_` method, while bringing every term to one side and using the equation solver is an `\_\_\_\_\_\_` method instead.


**BACK**

The completed sentence is:

Finding where two graphs cross is a **graphical** method, while bringing every term to one side and using the equation solver is an **algebraic** method instead.

Both routes give the same answers, because the points where the two graphs cross are exactly the roots of the single rearranged equation.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_Sgy9DHHK7qWG5SPP`)

**FRONT**

You already have $y = x^{2} + 3 x + 1$ drawn. How can you use it to solve $x^{2} + 3 x - 4 = 0$?


**BACK**

Rearrange the new equation so that the graph you already have appears on one side, by adding 5 to both sides.

That turns it into $x^{2} + 3 x + 1 = 5$, so you need only add the line $y = 5$ rather than redraw anything.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_dVcD66Tv7qFTSDnx`)

**FRONT**

How can you be sure you have found **all** the solutions of an equation from a graph?


**BACK**

Count **every** place the two graphs cross, checking that the viewing window is wide enough to show them all.

A curve and a line can meet more than once: a cubic and a straight line may cross at **three** points, giving three solutions.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_b5YyBGSCHDfSZgTJ`)

**FRONT**

What is the first thing to do when solving an inequality with a calculator?


**BACK**

Replace the inequality sign with an **equals sign** and solve `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` first.

Those solutions are the **boundaries** of the answer, being the values of $x$ at which the two graphs swap over.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_bV6Ngnv9XQhThxDG`)

**FRONT**

How do you decide which parts of the graph satisfy `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) > \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

Look for the stretches of $x$ where `y = \text{f}_{1} \left(x\right)` is the **top curve** and `y = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` is the **bottom curve**.

Being higher up the page is what being **greater** means, so those stretches are exactly where the inequality holds.

## Card 18 — true_or_false (`fl_8T8HRzN4mTtf8V2n`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Solving an inequality graphically gives you a list of $x$-values, just as solving an equation does.


**BACK**

**False.**

An inequality is satisfied over whole **ranges** of $x$, rather than at isolated points.

An answer therefore looks like $x < 2$ or $- 1 \leq x \leq 3$, describing every value in a stretch and not just a few of them.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_mG5vf5BSDHs2gb8g`)

**FRONT**

The graphs of $y = x^{2} + 3 x + 1$ and $y = 2 x + 1$ cross at $x = - 1$ and $x = 0$. Solve $x^{2} + 3 x + 1 > 2 x + 1$.


**BACK**

The quadratic is the **top curve** outside the two crossing points, because a u-shaped curve dips below a straight line only between them.

The solution is therefore $x < - 1$ or $x > 0$.

## Card 20 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_pwVCvRyRcygdwNDt`)

**FRONT**

The inequality sign in your answer has to match the one in the question. Complete the rule:

A question written with $<$ or $>$ has an answer written with the same `\_\_\_\_\_\_` signs, so the boundary values are `\_\_\_\_\_\_` from the set of solutions.


**BACK**

The completed rule is:

A question written with $<$ or $>$ has an answer written with the same **strict** signs, so the boundary values are **excluded** from the set of solutions.

With $\leq$ or $\geq$ the boundary values are included instead, and the answer is written with those signs.

## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_GcjkJKPbf4V6Z5rW`)

**FRONT**

Why can the answer to a graphical inequality need two separate ranges?


**BACK**

The two graphs can swap over more than once, so the curve you want on top can be on top in more than one place.

A cubic and a straight line can cross **three** times, which leaves the cubic below the line in two separate stretches.

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_2D4H3DZqScQC54b5`)

**FRONT**

Two graphs cross at $x = 1$ and $x = 4$, and `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) > \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` has solution $1 < x < 4$. Solve `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) < \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)`.


**BACK**

The solution is everything that is left over, $x < 1$ or $x > 4$.

The graphs change places only where they cross, so wherever one of them is not on top the other one must be.
