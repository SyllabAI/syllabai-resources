## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_BtxzMNJT5KCGYPHv`)

**FRONT**

Define the term **parabola**.


**BACK**

A **parabola** is the smooth, symmetrical curve that a **quadratic graph** makes.

It has a **vertical line of symmetry**, and a single turning point sitting on that line called the **vertex**.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_5rS8Jdnh672DcFWY`)

**FRONT**

How does the sign of $a$ in $y = a x^{2} + b x + c$ change the shape of the graph?


**BACK**

A **positive** value of $a$ gives a **u-shape**, whose vertex is a **minimum point**.

A **negative** value of $a$ gives an **n-shape**, whose vertex is a **maximum point**.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_CMQBT6rDxDjkBty2`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every quadratic graph crosses the $y$-axis, but it may not cross the $x$-axis at all.


**BACK**

**True.**

Substituting $x = 0$ always gives a value, so there is always a $y$-intercept, and it sits at `\left(0 , c\right)`.

Whether the curve ever reaches the $x$-axis depends on where its **vertex** sits.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_CT59ydt3qxYX8GzX`)

**FRONT**

On a quadratic graph, what are the **roots** and how many can there be?


**BACK**

The **roots** are the values of $x$ where the curve **crosses** the $x$-axis, so they are the solutions of $a x^{2} + b x + c = 0$.

A quadratic can have **two** roots, **one** root, or **none at all**.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_fQwj53gzShZFV9Np`)

**FRONT**

A quadratic graph has its **minimum** point at `\left(3 , 4\right)` and opens upwards. How can you tell it has no roots?


**BACK**

Its **minimum** is the lowest point on the whole curve, and it sits **above** the $x$-axis.

The curve can only rise from there, so it never reaches the axis and therefore has **no roots**.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_P2HVS3q2Qf6R72gC`)

**FRONT**

Why is knowing the **vertex** of a quadratic not enough to find its equation?


**BACK**

Knowing the vertex fixes **where** the curve sits, but not the value of $a$.

Many different parabolas share the same vertex, because $a$ also sets how **wide or narrow** the curve is, so **one further point** on the curve is needed.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_M7xPYkGmpGZqbrzF`)

**FRONT**

A quadratic graph passes through `\left(0 , 24\right)` and has **roots** at 2 and 3. Find its equation.


**BACK**

Build the factorised form from the roots, `y = a \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right)`, then substitute the known point.

That gives `24 = a \times \left(- 2\right) \times \left(- 3\right) = 6 a`, so $a = 4$:

`y = 4 \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right)`

## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_Jfjkv85DgV7ySwqk`)

**FRONT**

Define a **cubic** function.


**BACK**

A **cubic** is a function of the form $a x^{3} + b x^{2} + c x + d$, where $a$ cannot be zero.

It is a polynomial of **degree** (or **order**) **3**, and $b$, $c$ and $d$ are all allowed to be zero.


Spec links: `spcpt_PZ2gr6CddzRWzXWZ`


## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_pzbX88gnmhFZWYxJ`)

**FRONT**

How can you tell a **positive** cubic from a **negative** cubic on a graph?


**BACK**

A **positive** cubic, where $a > 0$, comes up from the **bottom left** and leaves at the **top right**.

A **negative** cubic, where $a < 0$, does the opposite: it starts at the **top left** and falls away to the **bottom right**.


Spec links: `spcpt_PZ2gr6CddzRWzXWZ`


## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_Zyz9TxBpRnqzzdXN`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every cubic graph has two turning points, a maximum and a minimum.


**BACK**

**False.**

A cubic has **either two turning points or none at all**.

The graphs of $y = x^{3}$ and $y = - x^{3}$ have none: each one levels off briefly and then carries straight on in the same direction.


Spec links: `spcpt_PZ2gr6CddzRWzXWZ`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_txKYcBq5RfwMHnBZ`)

**FRONT**

What are the roots of `y = \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right) \left(x + 5\right)` and why?


**BACK**

The roots are $x = 2$, $x = 3$ and $x = - 5$, one coming from each bracket.

A cubic in the form `y = \left(x - p\right) \left(x - q\right) \left(x - r\right)` has its roots at $p$, $q$ and $r$, so three brackets give up to three roots.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_bQHkJFYmG5wzKP4f`)

**FRONT**

What does a **repeated root** look like on a cubic graph?


**BACK**

At a **repeated root** the curve **touches** the $x$-axis and turns back, instead of crossing through it.

For `y = \left(x - 2\right)^{2} \left(x + 1\right)` the curve touches at $x = 2$, where the bracket is repeated, and crosses at $x = - 1$.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_4nPCfWCB8Zkj2gc3`)

**FRONT**

Why must every cubic graph cross the $x$-axis at least once?


**BACK**

A cubic starts on one side of the $x$-axis and ends on the other, because one end runs away to the **bottom** and the other to the **top**.

It is a **smooth, unbroken curve**, so it has to pass through the axis somewhere, which means a cubic always has at least one root.

## Card 16 — keyword_definition (`fl_DDr3zMv7YyXYRqKP`)

**FRONT**

Define the term **asymptote**.


**BACK**

An **asymptote** is a line that a curve gets **closer and closer to** but never touches.

The ones you meet are **parallel to the axes**, so an asymptote has an equation of the form $x = k$ or $y = k$.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_NjgHkjVkfBK26QMm`)

**FRONT**

How can you recognise a **reciprocal** function from its equation?


**BACK**

The variable sits in the **denominator** of a fraction, as in $y = \frac{a}{x}$, so a number is being divided by $x$.

That is what separates $y = \frac{6}{x}$, which is reciprocal, from $y = 5 x^{3}$, where $x$ is raised to a power instead.


Spec links: `spcpt_xVgvkMgFJmrrTsM9`


## Card 18 — true_or_false (`fl_qYzybRfZkcqP48N8`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The graph of $y = \frac{a}{x} + b$ has exactly the same shape as the graph of $y = \frac{a}{x}$.


**BACK**

**True.**

Adding $b$ slides the whole curve **up or down** without stretching it or bending it.

Only its **position** changes, so it still has the same two branches and the same overall shape.

**FRONT**

What are the equations of the two asymptotes of $y = \frac{a}{x}$?


**BACK**

The **vertical** asymptote is the $y$-axis, which has equation $x = 0$.

The **horizontal** asymptote is the $x$-axis, which has equation $y = 0$.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_TH7jx4WYYXWJy9D8`)

**FRONT**

Why does the graph of $y = \frac{a}{x}$ come in two separate parts?


**BACK**

There is no value of $y$ at all where $x$ is zero, because you cannot divide by zero.

The curve is therefore **broken** at the $y$-axis, which leaves one branch sitting on each side of it.

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_b2z38ht7nG88HRFP`)

**FRONT**

Why does the graph of $y = \frac{a}{x}$ never cross the $x$-axis?


**BACK**

Crossing it would mean the fraction $\frac{a}{x}$ was equal to zero, and dividing a fixed **non-zero** number can never give exactly zero.

The value gets **as close to zero as you like** for large $x$, so the curve flattens towards the axis without ever arriving.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_JgTyYqQFPNJBhXTS`)

**FRONT**

How can you recognise an **exponential** function from its equation?


**BACK**

The **variable is in the power**, as in $y = a^{x}$, rather than in the base.

That is what separates $y = 3^{x}$, which is exponential, from $y = x^{3}$, which is a cubic.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_VMZpJFhn3fPxDSvg`)

**FRONT**

At what point does every graph of $y = a^{x}$ cross the $y$-axis?


**BACK**

Every one of them crosses at `\left(0 , 1\right)`, whatever the value of $a$.

Substituting $x = 0$ leaves $a^{0}$, and **any non-zero number raised to the power 0 is equal to 1**.

**FRONT**

What asymptotes does the graph of $y = a^{x}$ have?


**BACK**

It has one **horizontal asymptote**, the $x$-axis, with equation $y = 0$.

There is **no vertical asymptote**, because $a^{x}$ can be worked out for every single value of $x$.

## Card 26 — true_or_false (`fl_fNmP9VK9D9PpJRtn`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

For negative values of $x$, the graph of $y = 3^{x}$ lies **below** the graph of $y = 2^{x}$.


**BACK**

**True.**

When the power is negative, the bigger base gives the **smaller** result.

At $x = - 1$, for example, $3^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}$, which is less than $2^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}$.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_54DnD692v6548nxk`)

**FRONT**

The graphs of $y = 2^{x}$ and $y = 4^{x}$ are drawn on the same axes. Which one is higher when $x$ is positive, and why?


**BACK**

The graph of $y = 4^{x}$ is higher, because repeatedly multiplying by 4 outruns repeatedly multiplying by 2.

At $x = 2$, for example, $4^{2} = 16$ against $2^{2} = 4$.

**FRONT**

Why is $y$ always **positive** on the graph of $y = a^{x}$?


**BACK**

A **positive base** raised to any power stays positive, whether that power is positive, negative or zero.

The curve therefore lies **entirely above** the $x$-axis, never dropping onto it or below it.
