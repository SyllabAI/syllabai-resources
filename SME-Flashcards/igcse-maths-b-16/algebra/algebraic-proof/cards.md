## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_p6pvypFDt3FMndFB`)

**FRONT**

Define **algebraic proof**, and say how it differs from testing values.


**BACK**

Algebraic proof means proving a result **using algebra**, so that it is shown to hold for **every** value at once.

Testing individual values only shows the result works for the ones you happened to try, which is not a proof.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_6G7dZZhfP4HPnV2p`)

**FRONT**

In an algebraic proof, what do letters such as $n$ and $k$ stand for?


**BACK**

They stand for **any positive integer**, rather than for any number at all.

That is what lets one piece of algebra prove a result for every whole number at the same time.

In these forms $k$ stands for a whole number. Fill in the two missing parts.

`\text{even means } 2 \times \_\_\_\_\_\_`

`\text{odd means } 2 k \pm \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed forms are:

$\text{even means} 2 \times k$

$\text{odd means} 2 k \pm 1$

Both are reached by **rewriting and factorising** the expression until it takes that shape, and $k$ has to be a whole number for the argument to work.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_2YgSBn7cgTRjTnsZ`)

**FRONT**

How do you prove that an expression is a multiple of 7?


**BACK**

Rewrite it until it can be **factorised** into the form $7 \times k$, where $k$ is a whole number.

For example `7 n^{2} + 14 n = 7 \left(n^{2} + 2 n\right)`, and $n^{2} + 2 n$ is a whole number whenever $n$ is.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_FxRZDVqsnK3WDkZJ`)

**FRONT**

How do you prove that an expression is a square number?


**BACK**

Rewrite it until it can be put in the form $k^{2}$, where $k$ is a whole number, which usually means **factorising** it.

For example `n^{2} + 6 n + 9 = \left(n + 3\right)^{2}`.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_f58GHg6n63vv7GPX`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

`2 \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)` is even, because it is 2 multiplied by something.


**BACK**

**False.**

For `2 \times \left(\text{something}\right)` to be even, that something has to be an **integer**, and $n + \frac{1}{2}$ is not.

In fact `2 \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2 n + 1`, which is **odd**.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_vxk65vgZy3m35zNw`)

**FRONT**

A proof involves `\left(6 n + 5\right)^{2} - \left(6 n - 5\right)^{2}`. Why is the difference of two squares quicker here than expanding both brackets?


**BACK**

It rewrites the expression as `\left(\left(6 n + 5\right) + \left(6 n - 5\right)\right) \left(\left(6 n + 5\right) - \left(6 n - 5\right)\right)`, and each bracket collapses immediately to $12 n$ and $10$.

That reaches $120 n$ without either square ever being expanded.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_Kk3wpn6Ms2cNSgRM`)

**FRONT**

An expression simplifies to $\frac{2n+1}{2}$. Why can it never be an integer?


**BACK**

The numerator $2 n + 1$ is **odd**, and an odd number divided by 2 is never a whole number.

Splitting the fraction shows the same thing: $\frac{2n+1}{2} = n + \frac{1}{2}$, an integer plus a half.
