## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Xh8jQqzysqswhzGt`)

**FRONT**

State the **factor theorem**.


**BACK**

For a function `\text{f} \left(x\right)`, if `\text{f} \left(a\right) = 0` then `\left(x - a\right)` is a factor of `\text{f} \left(x\right)`.

The theorem works **both ways**, so if `\left(x - a\right)` is a factor then `\text{f} \left(a\right) = 0`.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_JM94hhyRk5xxKkbz`)

**FRONT**

Testing whether `\left(x + 1\right)` is a factor of `\text{g} \left(x\right)` gives `\text{g} \left(- 1\right) = 7`. What does that tell you?


**BACK**

It tells you that `\left(x + 1\right)` is **not** a factor of `\text{g} \left(x\right)`.

The factor theorem needs the substitution to give exactly **zero**, so any other value rules the factor out.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_c3DW2HdhR3g6c5Jd`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

To test whether `\left(2 x - 3\right)` is a factor of `\text{h} \left(x\right)`, you work out `\text{h} \left(3\right)`.


**BACK**

**False.**

When the linear factor has a coefficient of $x$, take that coefficient out first: `\left(2 x - 3\right) = 2 \left(x - \frac{3}{2}\right)`.

The factor to test is therefore `\left(x - \frac{3}{2}\right)`, so it is `\text{h} \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)` that must equal zero.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_VK6NQTFDpm7hmfCb`)

**FRONT**

If `\left(x - a\right)` is a factor of `\text{f} \left(x\right)`, what does dividing `\text{f} \left(x\right)` by it give?


**BACK**

It gives the **quotient** `\text{Q} \left(x\right)`, so that `\text{f} \left(x\right) = \left(x - a\right) \times \text{Q} \left(x\right)`.

The quotient is itself a factor of `\text{f} \left(x\right)`, so factorising it further gives any remaining factors.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_KxXWV7GXVpnfkFXt`)

**FRONT**

Where should you look first for integer solutions of a polynomial equation?


**BACK**

Among the **factors of the constant term**, because an integer solution of a polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients must divide that constant.

For $x^{3} - 2 x^{2} - x + 2$ the constant is $2$, so the only integer solutions possible are $\pm 1$ and $\pm 2$.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_wqjzRhBjqWgHvyJN`)

**FRONT**

Define **algebraic division**, and say what it is used for.


**BACK**

Algebraic division, also called **polynomial division**, splits a polynomial into a pair of factors, with or without a **remainder** term.

It is used to **factorise** polynomials and to **simplify** algebraic fractions.

## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_HDXjdmxq5XCdNtRY`)

**FRONT**

Define a **polynomial**.


**BACK**

A polynomial is an algebraic expression made up of a **finite** number of terms whose powers are all **non-negative whole numbers**.

So $2 x^{2} y - 4 y + 6$ is a polynomial, and so is a plain number such as $8$, while $\sqrt{x}$ and $\frac{3}{x}$ are not.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_85hzgd7wtQyc9fs7`)

**FRONT**

In algebraic long division, what happens after each term of the answer is written?


**BACK**

That term is **multiplied** by the divisor, and the result is **subtracted** from what is left.

The answer is built up term by term, working **downwards** in powers of $x$ and starting from the highest power.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_Z7y967KzDm4Hkdpk`)

**FRONT**

When does an algebraic long division stop, and what is left at that point?


**BACK**

It stops once what is left is just a **number**, with no $x$ terms in it.

That number is the **remainder**.

## Card 12 — true_or_false (`fl_9sfg7zTvbY9k8r59`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A remainder of zero in an algebraic division means the divisor is a factor of the polynomial.


**BACK**

**True.**

A zero remainder means the polynomial splits exactly into the divisor multiplied by the quotient, with nothing left over.

That is precisely what it means for the divisor to be a **factor**.
