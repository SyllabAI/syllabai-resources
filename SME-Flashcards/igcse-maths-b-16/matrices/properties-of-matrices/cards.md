## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Tz3wTBR8cdNKB4Fh`)

**FRONT**

Define a **matrix**, and say what its **order** means.


**BACK**

A matrix is a rectangular grid of **elements**, which may be numbers or letters, arranged in rows and columns.

Its **order** is its number of rows followed by its number of columns, so a matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns has order $2 \times 3$.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_5GMXwzXb9sV8VvHp`)

**FRONT**

What is the order of `\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}`, and what is such a matrix called?


**BACK**

Its order is $2 \times 2$, because it has two rows and two columns.

A matrix with the same number of rows as columns is called a **square** matrix.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_qYjPtR6cYgrjwQ8q`)

**FRONT**

What can a $2 \times 3$ matrix be used to represent geometrically?


**BACK**

The three vertices of a triangle, with each vertex written as one **column** of the matrix.

A single column of two numbers holds one point, so three columns hold three points.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_JDc45mm8ptb4HPdW`)

**FRONT**

What is the **null matrix**, and how does it differ from the identity matrix?


**BACK**

The null matrix, also called the **zero matrix**, has **every element equal to 0**, and it plays the part that 0 plays for ordinary numbers.

Unlike the identity matrix, it does **not** have to be square.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_MJr6wPR2Z3KfsQ7s`)

**FRONT**

When can two matrices be added or subtracted?


**BACK**

Only when they have the **same order**, meaning the same number of rows and the same number of columns.

You then add or subtract the corresponding elements, position by position.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_nrFmMckrDnssmvwW`)

**FRONT**

How do you multiply a matrix by a scalar?


**BACK**

Multiply **every element** of the matrix by that scalar.

A scalar is simply a number, and a lower-case letter is usually used for one, so $k A$ means every element of $A$ multiplied by $k$.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_wXJkKGbMCbh8vtJg`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Two matrices are equal only when every pair of corresponding elements is equal.


**BACK**

**True.**

Matrix equality is checked **element by element**, so all of the pairs have to match.

That is what lets you form an ordinary equation from any one position when a matrix equation contains an unknown.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_yxCFxqxMtrkzvtnx`)

**FRONT**

Given that `2 \mathbf{A} - n \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 1 \\ - 13 & 2 \end{pmatrix}`, how do you find $n$?


**BACK**

Work out $2 A$ and $n B$ separately, subtract them element by element, then equate any **one** position with the matching element on the right.

Solving that single equation gives $n$, and the other three positions are worth checking to make sure no slip has been made.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_SWVnpXV8QZFxNCrM`)

**FRONT**

When can two matrices be multiplied together?


**BACK**

Only when the number of **columns in the first** equals the number of **rows in the second**.

So a $2 \times 2$ matrix can multiply a $2 \times 3$ matrix, but a $2 \times 3$ cannot multiply a $2 \times 2$.

## Card 13 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_r3XfrZvH76htmx7N`)

`\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \_\_\_\_\_\_ \\ \_\_\_\_\_\_ \end{pmatrix}`


**BACK**

The completed product is:

`\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a x + b y \\ c x + d y \end{pmatrix}`

Each entry pairs a **row of the first** matrix with the **column of the second**, multiplying corresponding elements and adding the results.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_B8ZvhSrSkNTWCYKK`)

**FRONT**

What is the order of the product of a $2 \times 2$ matrix and a $2 \times 3$ matrix?


**BACK**

It is $2 \times 3$, taking the **rows of the first** and the **columns of the second**.

## Card 15 — true_or_false (`fl_RdR97kqbhtkv5qCG`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

For matrices, $\mathrm{AB}$ always gives the same result as $\mathrm{BA}$.


**BACK**

**False.**

Matrix multiplication is **not** commutative, so in general $\mathrm{AB} \neq \mathrm{BA}$.

For instance `\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 3 \\ 20 & 7 \end{pmatrix}`, while the other order gives `\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 8 & 14 \end{pmatrix}`.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_8yhdqbFQk5MfjzMH`)

**FRONT**

How do you square a matrix?


**BACK**

Write it out as the matrix multiplied by **itself**, then carry out the multiplication in the usual way.

Do **not** square each element separately, and note that only square matrices can be squared at all.

## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_bd3dskqNPdbwYCMy`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

For three matrices, `\left(\mathbf{AB}\right) \mathbf{C}` gives the same result as `\mathbf{A} \left(\mathbf{BC}\right)`.


**BACK**

**True.**

Matrix multiplication is **associative**, so which pair you multiply first makes no difference.

What must not change is the **order of the letters**, since `\mathbf{A} \left(\mathbf{BC}\right)` is not the same as `\left(\mathbf{BC}\right) \mathbf{A}`.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_hBkqqwgnnKp9PrFF`)

**FRONT**

What happens when you multiply a matrix by the identity matrix?


**BACK**

Nothing changes: $\mathrm{AI} = \mathrm{IA} = A$, whichever side the identity is on.

That makes the identity matrix behave like the number 1, and it is one of the few matrix products where the order genuinely does not matter.

## Card 19 — keyword_definition (`fl_twhfDxy4g6TntsHQ`)

**FRONT**

Define the **determinant** of a matrix.


**BACK**

The determinant is a single **number**, positive or negative, worked out from the elements of a square matrix.

Only square matrices have one, and its main use is in finding the **inverse**.

The completed formula is:

**FRONT**

Define the **inverse** of a matrix.


**BACK**

The inverse of a square matrix $A$, written $A^{-1}$, is the matrix that multiplies with it to give the **identity matrix** $I$.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_RndRvjs9zvvz6S79`)

**FRONT**

Why can a matrix with determinant zero have no inverse?


**BACK**

The formula divides by the determinant, and dividing by zero is not defined.

Such a matrix therefore has no inverse at all.

## Card 30 — question_and_answer (`fl_ZvYjzK4Cdd97SNM7`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve $\mathrm{AB} = C$ for $B$?


**BACK**

Multiply both sides **from the left** by $A^{-1}$, which gives $A^{-1} \mathrm{AB} = A^{-1} C$.

Since $A^{-1} A$ is the identity and the identity leaves $B$ alone, this leaves $B = A^{-1} C$.

## Card 31 — question_and_answer (`fl_BM94fzQyRCzxS3ZC`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve $\mathrm{BA} = C$ for $B$?


**BACK**

Multiply both sides **from the right** by $A^{-1}$, which gives $\mathrm{BAA}^{-1} = \mathrm{CA}^{-1}$.

That leaves $B = \mathrm{CA}^{-1}$, with the inverse written on the **right** of $C$.

**True or False?**

When solving a matrix equation, it does not matter which side you multiply by the inverse.


**BACK**

**False.**

Multiplying $\mathrm{BA} = C$ from the left gives $A^{-1} \mathrm{BA} = A^{-1} C$, which does not simplify any further.

## Card 33 — question_and_answer (`fl_vtC68YVqkxKX8MMn`)

**FRONT**

Why does $A^{-1} \mathrm{BA}$ not simplify?


**BACK**

A matrix and its inverse only collapse to the identity when they are **next to each other** in the multiplication.

Here $B$ sits between them, so neither pairing can be made and nothing cancels.
