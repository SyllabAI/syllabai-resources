## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_zq5TSrRNss68Zxw5`)

**FRONT**

What is a quadratic graph's shape called, and what is its turning point called?


**BACK**

The shape is a **parabola**, a smooth curve with a **vertical line of symmetry**.

Its turning point is called the **vertex**, which is a minimum on a u-shape and a maximum on an n-shape.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A quadratic with a negative number in front of $x^{2}$ gives a u-shaped curve.


**BACK**

**False.**

It is a **positive** number in front of $x^{2}$ that gives the u-shape, with a minimum point.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_5PGpVR5QMYYb7G5j`)

**FRONT**

How many times can a quadratic graph cross each axis?


**BACK**

It crosses the $y$-axis **exactly once**, whatever the quadratic is.

It crosses the $x$-axis **twice, once, or not at all**, and those crossing points are the **roots**.

**True or False?**

The curves `y = \left(x - 3\right)^{2} + 2` and `y = 5 \left(x - 3\right)^{2} + 2` have the same vertex.


**BACK**

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_nVcWNfzR89YbKn23`)

**FRONT**

A quadratic graph has roots at $x = 2$ and $x = 3$ and passes through `\left(0 , 24\right)`. How do you find its equation?


**BACK**

Use the form `y = a \left(x - x_{1}\right) \left(x - x_{2}\right)` to write `y = a \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right)`, then substitute the other point into it.

That gives $24 = 6 a$, so $a = 4$ and the equation is `y = 4 \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right)`.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_QMTf8269XWX8hF8x`)

**FRONT**

A quadratic graph has vertex `\left(9 , - 16\right)` and passes through `\left(2 , 82\right)`. How do you find its equation?


**BACK**

Use the form `y = a \left(x - p\right)^{2} + q` to write `y = a \left(x - 9\right)^{2} - 16`, then substitute the other point into it.

That gives $82 = 49 a - 16$, so $a = 2$ and the equation is `y = 2 \left(x - 9\right)^{2} - 16`.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_TKxWNVTXGBZpv2RX`)

**FRONT**

When is the vertex alone enough to find a quadratic's equation?


**BACK**

Only when you already know that $a = 1$, so the equation is simply `y = \left(x - p\right)^{2} + q`.

Otherwise a second point on the curve is needed as well, to pin down the value of $a$.

## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_ns2rRnvvBMg7xbW3`)

**FRONT**

Define a **cubic**.


**BACK**

A cubic is a function of the form $a x^{3} + b x^{2} + c x + d$, which is a polynomial of **degree 3**.

The constants $b$, $c$ and $d$ may each be zero, but $a$ **cannot** be zero.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_7JCpdsv5mbJPS3d9`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A cubic whose $x^{3}$ coefficient is negative starts in the bottom left and ends in the top right.


**BACK**

**False.**

That describes a **positive** cubic, one where the coefficient of $x^{3}$ is greater than zero.

A **negative** cubic runs the other way, starting in the top left and ending in the bottom right.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_vfGjK8d2N4ZmckNr`)

**FRONT**

How many turning points can a cubic graph have?


**BACK**

Up to **two**, one maximum and one minimum.

Some cubics have none at all: $y = x^{3}$ and $y = - x^{3}$ have no maximum or minimum, and each meets the $x$-axis only once, at $x = 0$.


Spec links: `spcpt_tyrnCXQW2hfdwnyq`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_TWRfkjBdzQS2C2nH`)

**FRONT**

Why does a cubic need to be in factorised form before you can sketch it?


**BACK**

Because the factors give you the **roots** directly, and the roots fix where the curve meets the $x$-axis.

Simple cubics factorise at once, such as `x^{3} - 4 x = x \left(x - 2\right) \left(x + 2\right)`, while harder ones need the factor theorem and algebraic division.


Spec links: `spcpt_tyrnCXQW2hfdwnyq`


## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_NWFqCdrYpdtpqzgy`)

**FRONT**

What are the roots of `y = \left(x - 2\right) \left(x - 3\right) \left(x + 5\right)`?


**BACK**

The roots are $x = 2$, $x = 3$ and $x = - 5$.

A cubic written `\left(x - p\right) \left(x - q\right) \left(x - r\right)` has roots at $p$, $q$ and $r$, so each one is read off by changing the sign inside its own bracket.

## Card 14 — true_or_false (`fl_6jfvYG5zcRfzG5F7`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The graph of `y = \left(x - 2\right)^{2} \left(x + 1\right)` crosses the $x$-axis at $x = 2$.


**BACK**

**False.**

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_xrZXHKhtjkjjCM2Y`)

**FRONT**

A positive cubic touches the $x$-axis at $x = 3$ and crosses it at $x = \frac{1}{2}$. Where must its two turning points lie?


**BACK**

One is a **minimum** at $x = 3$ itself, because the curve touches the axis there and turns back upwards.

The other is a **maximum** somewhere between the two roots, so between $x = \frac{1}{2}$ and $x = 3$.

## Card 16 — keyword_definition (`fl_STGTQ3grX6fRhrgP`)

**FRONT**

What form does a **reciprocal graph** take?


**BACK**

A reciprocal graph has the form $y = \frac{a}{x}$ or $y = \frac{a}{x^{2}}$.

The two simplest are $y = \frac{1}{x}$ and $y = \frac{1}{x^{2}}$, where $a = 1$.

## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_DTnH8nPhXtQvm6wx`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The graph of $y = \frac{3}{x}$ never crosses either axis.


**BACK**

**True.**

It has **no roots** and no $y$-intercept, so it meets neither axis anywhere.

Instead it approaches both axes without ever reaching them, which is what its two asymptotes describe.

## Card 18 — keyword_definition (`fl_yDMs592TzDxCJZqk`)

**FRONT**

Define an **asymptote**.


**BACK**

An asymptote is a line that a curve gets closer and closer to but never touches.

Asymptotes may be **horizontal** or **vertical** lines.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_ZtCKJH8sT6cQ27xK`)

**FRONT**

Why does $y = \frac{1}{x}$ have a vertical asymptote at $x = 0$?


**BACK**

Because $x = 0$ would mean **dividing by zero**, which has no value, so the curve can never reach that line.

The horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ arises the other way round: as $x$ grows large in either direction, $\frac{1}{x}$ shrinks towards zero without ever getting there.


Spec links: `spcpt_TvHwnSVsSstxssKh`


## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_xCmPgQJYNKSzQfnX`)

**FRONT**

Why do both branches of $y = \frac{1}{x^{2}}$ lie above the $x$-axis?


**BACK**

Because $x^{2}$ is **positive** for every non-zero value of $x$, whether $x$ itself is positive or negative.

That is what makes it differ from $y = \frac{1}{x}$, whose two branches sit on opposite sides of the axis.


Spec links: `spcpt_TvHwnSVsSstxssKh`


## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_GrCcBQs2nmsS2YXk`)

**FRONT**

In $y = \frac{a}{x}$, what do the sign and the size of $a$ each change?


**BACK**

The **sign** of $a$ decides where the two branches sit.

A positive $a$ puts them in the top-right and bottom-left, and a negative $a$ puts them in the top-left and bottom-right.

The **size** of $a$ decides how steep they are, and the closer $a$ is to zero the more **L-shaped** they become.
