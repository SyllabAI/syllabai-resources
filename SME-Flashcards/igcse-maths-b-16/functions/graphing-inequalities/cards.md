## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_wkmvXfzq9BrW8B2q`)

**FRONT**

What does the solution to a 2D inequality such as $x + y \geq 8$ look like?


**BACK**

It is a **region** in the $x y$ plane, rather than a set of separate values.

An inequality in one variable such as $y \geq 2$ also gives a region, namely every point on or above the line $y = 2$.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_55xMNkkPxTnkfKbT`)

**FRONT**

For $y < - 2 x + 5$, which side of the line is the wanted region?


**BACK**

**Below** the line, because the inequality reads $y$ **less than** something.

If you are unsure, substitute a point from one side into the inequality: a true statement means that side is the wanted one.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_PRy3DGYCyt6wrPX2`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

When showing a region $R$ defined by several inequalities, you shade the side of each line that you want.


**BACK**

**False.**

Unless the question says otherwise, shade the **unwanted** side of each line.

Shading away everything you do not want leaves the wanted region clear and unshaded, which is far easier to read than hunting for the one area covered by every layer of shading.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_rGVMhT9tNcqwWNgm`)

**FRONT**

A region is defined by $y \geq x$, $x + y \leq 8$ and $y \geq 2$. Does the point `\left(3 , 4\right)` lie in it?


**BACK**

Yes, because it satisfies **all three**: $4 \geq 3$ is true, $3 + 4 = 7 \leq 8$ is true, and $4 \geq 2$ is true.

A point has to satisfy **every** inequality to lie in the region, so a single failure is enough to rule it out.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_8zbgtFW5S45xgbR4`)

**FRONT**

Why rearrange $3 x + 2 y \geq 12$ before drawing its boundary line?


**BACK**

Rearranging it to $y \geq - \frac{3}{2} x + 6$ puts it in the form $y = m x + c$, so the gradient and the $y$-intercept can be read straight off.

It also makes the wanted side obvious, because the inequality now reads $y$ **greater than or equal to** something.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_xgPHDCvZxz2yJXrx`)

**FRONT**

What is the first thing to do when reading inequalities off a shaded region?


**BACK**

Find the **equation of each line** on the graph, ignoring the inequality signs for the moment.

Sloping lines need $y = m x + c$ from the gradient and $y$-intercept, vertical lines have the form $x = k$, and horizontal lines the form $y = k$.

## Card 9 — true_or_false (`fl_z7ybgv7gBJqzHcC4`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A horizontal boundary line on a region diagram has an equation of the form $y = k$.


**BACK**

**True.**

Every point on a horizontal line has the same $y$-coordinate, so its equation sets $y$ equal to a constant.

It is the **vertical** line that has equation $x = k$, and these two are the pair most often swapped.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_ygdqRrSRx6VwKYCW`)

**FRONT**

A shaded region lies above the line $y = x$, so which inequality does it give?


**BACK**

It gives $y > x$ if the line is dotted, or $y \geq x$ if the line is solid.

A region **above** a line gives $\geq$ or $>$, and a region **below** it gives $\leq$ or $<$.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_gvNhdx2MMvJK8FFV`)

**FRONT**

A shaded region lies to the right of the solid line $x = 1$. Which inequality is that?


**BACK**

It is $x \geq 1$, because a region to the **right** of a vertical line gives $\geq$ or $>$.

A region to the **left** of $x = k$ gives $\leq$ or $<$ instead.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_kbMnnyqx7N9gpStJ`)

**FRONT**

Why must you check whether a diagram shades the wanted or the unwanted region?


**BACK**

Because the two conventions give **opposite** inequality signs for the same picture.

If the shading marks the unwanted side, the region being described is the **unshaded** one, so every sign flips compared with reading the shaded side.
