## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_8PQNZfPVyWxsHX67`)

**FRONT**

Define **simultaneous equations**.


**BACK**

**Simultaneous equations** are two or more equations in more than one unknown, solved together.

Solving them means finding the **pairs of values** that make every equation true at the same time.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_BfWb75PVMrBxVqyd`)

**FRONT**

What does it mean for an equation in two unknowns to be **linear**?


**BACK**

None of the unknowns is raised to any power other than one.

Its graph is a **straight line**, which is where the name comes from.

The coordinates of that point are the pair of values you are solving for.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_6JkvDxsbFwPTrtXD`)

**FRONT**

What must you do before you can eliminate an unknown?


**BACK**

**Make the coefficients of that unknown match**, by multiplying one or both equations by a constant.

For $- 2 x + 4 y = 5$ and $4 x - 5 y = - 7$, doubling the first gives $- 4 x + 8 y = 10$, which matches the $4 x$.

## Card 6 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_c7YcN2FjppZ9SQtS`)

**FRONT**

In elimination, if the matching coefficients have the **same** sign you `\_\_\_\_\_\_` the equations, and if they have **different** signs you `\_\_\_\_\_\_` them.


**BACK**

In elimination, if the matching coefficients have the **same** sign you **subtract** the equations, and if they have **different** signs you **add** them.

Getting this the wrong way round leaves both unknowns still in the equation.

**FRONT**

Once you have found both unknowns, how should you check the answer to a pair of simultaneous equations?


**BACK**

Substitute both values into the equation you did **not** use to find the second unknown.

Using the same equation again would only confirm your arithmetic rather than the solution.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_PxNtbTWqNr8fxRpD`)

**FRONT**

Why must the rearranged expression be substituted into the **other** equation, rather than the one it came from?


**BACK**

Because putting it back into its own equation gives a statement that is always true, such as $0 = 0$, which tells you nothing.

Only the second equation brings in new information about the unknowns.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Elimination will solve any pair of linear simultaneous equations.


**BACK**

**True.**

Elimination always works on a linear pair.

Substitution is often quicker, particularly when one equation already has an unknown as its subject, so which you use is a matter of choice.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_7pwrN4VM9QwhYgct`)

**FRONT**

Substitution has given you the value of one unknown. How do you find the other?


**BACK**

Substitute it into the **rearranged** equation from the first step, which already has an unknown as its subject.

With $y = 1 - 9 x$ and $x = \frac{1}{3}$, this gives $y = 1 - 3 = - 2$.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_8Fzs2DvTfDzTXKxC`)

**FRONT**

What makes an equation **quadratic**?


**BACK**

It contains terms of **degree two** and no higher, with no unknowns raised to negative or fractional powers.

So $y = 5 x^{2} - 2 x + 3$ is quadratic, but $y = \sqrt{x} - 5$ is not, because $\sqrt{x}$ is a fractional power.


Spec links: `spcpt_82NtJq6t3CwTsvB7`


## Card 14 — true_or_false (`fl_Rq7hn5nbVzxQKDp9`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$y^{2} + 4 x y - x^{2} = - 7$ is a quadratic equation.


**BACK**

**True.**

The term $4 x y$ has degree **two**, because the powers of $x$ and $y$ add to two.

Nothing in the equation goes above degree two, so it is quadratic.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_nHrCS5VcbSXXBZd8`)

**FRONT**

For $2 x - 3 y = 23$ and $3 y^{2} = 4 x^{2} + 11$, why is it easier to rearrange to $2 x = 3 y + 23$ than to $x = \frac{3y+23}{2}$?

## Card 20 — keyword_definition (`fl_MbBgjNmPsh7Vx9cR`)

**FRONT**

Define **linear inequality**.


**BACK**

A **linear inequality** is an inequality in which every term has degree $1$ or $0$, so there are no squared or higher powers, and no fractional or negative powers.

Unlike an equation, it is satisfied by a **range** of values rather than a single one.


Spec links: `spcpt_N3twfd2vQRbVyNy7`


## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_HZSf3kXQxq5nmVXx`)

**FRONT**

On a number line, what does a filled circle mean, and an open one?


**BACK**

A **filled** circle means the endpoint is **included**, so it goes with $\leq$ and $\geq$.

An **open** circle means the endpoint is **excluded**, so it goes with $<$ and $>$.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_ssRB7PkPx4K8M6gm`)

**FRONT**

Why is $\infty$ always written with a round bracket in interval notation?


**BACK**

Because a square bracket would mean the endpoint is **included**, and $\infty$ is not a value that $x$ can take.

So $x \leq 2$ is written `\left(- \infty , 2\right]`, open at the infinite end and closed at $2$.


Spec links: `spcpt_N3twfd2vQRbVyNy7`


## Card 24 — true_or_false (`fl_YF9wZqX2BMXSRkGB`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Multiplying both sides of an inequality by $- 1$ reverses the inequality sign.


**BACK**

**True.**

Multiplying or dividing by any **negative** number reverses the sign, so $- x > 3$ becomes $x < - 3$.

Adding or subtracting a negative number does **not** reverse it, which is why rearranging is usually the safer route.


Spec links: `spcpt_N3twfd2vQRbVyNy7`


## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_ZKRkDzhn6mVRTyNn`)

**FRONT**

When solving $8 - 3 x \geq 5 x - 4$, why collect the $x$ terms on the right rather than the left?


**BACK**

It keeps the coefficient of $x$ **positive**, so the inequality sign never has to be reversed.

Collecting on the left would give $- 8 x \geq - 12$, and dividing by $- 8$ would then flip $\geq$ to $\leq$.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_Bf7hJvqWfCDKpTdP`)

**FRONT**

Why is sketching the graph the key step in solving a quadratic inequality?


**BACK**

The roots alone do not say which values of $x$ satisfy the inequality, only where the curve meets the axis.

The sketch shows where the curve is **above** the axis and where it is **below**, which is what the inequality is asking about.

## Card 28 — true_or_false (`fl_jgZr4csfCSnPtJZp`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

You can multiply both sides of an inequality by $x$, provided you reverse the inequality sign.


**BACK**

**False.**

You do not know whether $x$ is positive or negative, so you cannot know whether the sign should be reversed at all.

Multiplying by $x^{2}$ avoids that, since a square is never negative, but it can introduce extra values that do not satisfy the original inequality.

**FRONT**

When solving $5 - 5 x^{2} \leq 7 + 4 x - 8 x^{2}$, why rearrange it to $3 x^{2} - 4 x - 2 \leq 0$?


**BACK**

So that the $x^{2}$ term is **positive**, which makes the sketch a $\cup$ shape every time.

Working from one standard shape means the region above or below the axis can be read off without re-deriving it.

## Card 31 — question_and_answer (`fl_FMXHSwrCcSyRcZcT`)

**FRONT**

A quadratic with a positive $x^{2}$ term has roots $x_{1} < x_{2}$. Which values of $x$ make it negative, and which make it positive?


**BACK**

It is **negative between** the roots, $x_{1} < x < x_{2}$, where the curve dips below the axis.

It is **positive outside** them, $x < x_{1}$ or $x > x_{2}$, where the two arms rise above the axis.

## Card 32 — question_and_answer (`fl_93sPxXPvnbXqPkpY`)

**FRONT**

Why is it worth dividing $2 x^{2} - 2 x - 4 > 0$ through by $2$ before solving it?


**BACK**

The numbers become smaller and factorise more easily, giving `\left(x - 2\right)\left(x + 1\right) > 0`.

Dividing by a **positive** number leaves the inequality sign unchanged, so nothing is lost.

**FRONT**

How do you decide which side of a line satisfies an inequality?


**BACK**
