## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_4Rc8CZFXYWpDN5mf`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the gradient of a curve at a particular point?


**BACK**

Differentiate to get `\text{f}'\left(x\right)`, then substitute the $x$-coordinate of the point into it.

The derivative is a formula for the gradient, so it has to be evaluated at the point you actually want.

## Card 2 — keyword_definition (`fl_k6GjPkdxHCzR8JP3`)

**FRONT**

Define the **normal** to a curve.


**BACK**

The **normal** at a point is the line through that point which is **perpendicular** to the tangent there.

Where there is no tangent, as at a sharp corner, there is no normal either.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_cT8NhWJFt96x7hy9`)

**FRONT**

If the tangent at a point has gradient `\text{f}'\left(a\right)`, what is the gradient of the normal there?


**BACK**

`- \frac{1}{\text{f}'\left(a\right)}`.

The normal is perpendicular to the tangent, so the two gradients must multiply to $- 1$.

**FRONT**

You are told only the $x$-coordinate of the point where a tangent touches. What else do you need, and how do you get it?

The $y$-coordinate, found by substituting that $x$ into the **original** function.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_KFK6fkmG3qKqbhnr`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Where the tangent to a curve is horizontal, the normal is vertical.


**BACK**

**True.**

If `\text{f}'\left(a\right) = 0` the tangent is horizontal, so the normal must be vertical.

Its equation is then $x = a$, because `- \frac{1}{\text{f}'\left(a\right)}` has no value when the derivative is zero.

## Card 7 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_sWsTxTQ7YSjb3FWZ`)

**FRONT**

A function is **increasing** on an interval where `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) \_\_\_\_\_\_ 0`, and **decreasing** where `\text{f} ' \left(x\right) \_\_\_\_\_\_ 0`.


**BACK**

It is increasing where `\text{f}'\left(x\right) \geq 0`, and decreasing where `\text{f}'\left(x\right) \leq 0`.

With the strict inequalities $> 0$ and $< 0$ instead, the function is called **strictly** increasing or decreasing.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_4zTnKm23FkHbn7kf`)

**FRONT**

Why does the sign of `\text{f}'\left(x\right)` tell you whether a function is increasing?


**BACK**

Because `\text{f}'\left(x\right)` **is** the gradient, and a positive gradient means the curve rises as $x$ increases.

A negative gradient means it falls.

## Card 12 — keyword_definition (`fl_wMJsvWB8HHMGZRSW`)

**FRONT**

Define **second derivative**.


**BACK**

The result of differentiating a function **twice**, written `\text{f}''\left(x\right)` or $\frac{\text{d}^{2}y}{\text{d}x^{2}}$.

It does **not** mean squaring the first derivative: the superscripts are part of the notation, not powers.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_cQCqN2bdJPtZRPNm`)

**FRONT**

What does the second derivative measure?


**BACK**

The **rate of change of the gradient**.

The first derivative says how fast $y$ is changing; the second says how fast that rate is itself changing.

**FRONT**

What is the second derivative mainly used for?


**BACK**

Determining the **nature of a stationary point**, that is whether it is a maximum or a minimum.

Its sign at that point is what distinguishes the two.

## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_DdxyKxJgDgp4Fqnr`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The second derivative of a straight line is zero.


**BACK**

**True.**

A straight line has a **constant** gradient, so the rate at which that gradient changes is zero.

Differentiating $y = m x + c$ gives $m$, and differentiating again gives $0$.

## Card 17 — keyword_definition (`fl_S2V88nDNM2wkfmmC`)

**FRONT**

Define **stationary point**.


**BACK**

A point on a curve where the **gradient is zero**.

It may be a local minimum, a local maximum, or a point of inflection.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_QGNNsYT7WdyT7JNt`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the stationary points of `\text{f}\left(x\right)`?


**BACK**

Differentiate, solve `\text{f}'\left(x\right) = 0` for the $x$-coordinates, then substitute those back into `\text{f}\left(x\right)` for the $y$-coordinates.

The derivative gives the positions; the original function gives the heights.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_Nd9fg3MkZHfcScGq`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a stationary point and a turning point?


**BACK**

Every turning point is stationary, but a **point of inflection** is stationary without being a turning point.

A turning point is one where the curve actually changes direction, from rising to falling or the other way round.

**FRONT**

How do you use the **first** derivative to determine the nature of a stationary point?

## Card 35 — question_and_answer (`fl_XKvT98Q37JVVWMfv`)

**FRONT**

In a problem with three variables, how does the chain rule connect the rate you know to the rate you want?


**BACK**

It joins them through a third derivative that you can work out. For a volume, a side length and time:

$\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}t} = \frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}s} \times \frac{\text{d}s}{\text{d}t}$

Write down the rate you are given and the rate you want, then pick the chain that joins them.

## Card 40 — question_and_answer (`fl_2YYCMhpD6YzMcnB6`)

**FRONT**

A sphere's volume grows at a constant rate. Why can you not find the rate of change of its surface area $A$ without first linking $V$ and $A$?


**BACK**

Because the chain rule needs $\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}A}$, and that only exists once $V$ has been written in terms of $A$.

From $V = \frac{4}{3} π r^{3}$ and $A = 4 π r^{2}$, eliminating the radius gives $V = \frac{A^{\frac{3}{2}}}{6\sqrt{π}}$.
