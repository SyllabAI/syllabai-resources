## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_3tDprwptyTbZ3RnW`)

**FRONT**

What is meant by the **gradient of a curve** at a particular point?


**BACK**

It is the gradient of the **tangent** to the curve at that point.

The gradient of a curve is not fixed: it changes from point to point, which is why a gradient can only be given **at a particular point**.

## Card 2 — keyword_definition (`fl_n9B5R7vYxGpMJmTP`)

**FRONT**

Define **tangent** to a curve.


**BACK**

A straight line that **touches** a curve at a given point, running in the same direction as the curve at that point.

At any given point of a **smooth** curve there is exactly **one** tangent.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_dVq4zCybBBzp27Wd`)

**FRONT**

A tangent has been drawn to a curve at the point $A$ on an accurately drawn graph. Which two points should you use to work out the gradient of the curve at $A$?


**BACK**

Any two points that lie **on the tangent line**, chosen where it passes through easily read coordinates; points on the **curve** itself must not be used.

The gradient is then the change in $y$ divided by the change in $x$ between those two points. So, **for example**, a tangent through `\left(1 , - 2\right)` and `\left(3 , 4\right)` has gradient:

`\frac{4 - \left(- 2\right)}{3 - 1} = \frac{6}{2} = 3`

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_Rjj3zqKd3JVsYthJ`)

**FRONT**

Are there points on a graph where a curve has no gradient?


**BACK**

Yes. A tangent can only be drawn where the curve is **smooth**.

At a sharp corner, such as the vertex of the graph of `y = |\text{f}(x)|`, no tangent can be drawn, so the gradient there is **undefined**.

## Card 6 — keyword_definition (`fl_YNQZnpNZ46479dM5`)

**FRONT**

Define **derivative**.


**BACK**

The **derivative** of a function is its **rate of change** with respect to a variable.

It is also the **gradient function** of the graph `y = \text{f} \left(x\right)`, giving the gradient of the curve at any value of $x$.


Spec links: `spcpt_W3hwHXtqPNktSS4H`


## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_JfWkYJ3GnzD6WyXM`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The derivative of a function is itself a function, rather than a single number.


**BACK**

**True.**

The gradient of a curve changes from one value of $x$ to the next, so the derivative has to give a **rule** rather than one answer.

For $y = x^{2}$ it is close to zero near the origin and grows as $x$ increases, while for a straight line it happens to be the same everywhere.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_cP6r3ZrXJsCpP2qB`)

**FRONT**

What does the notation `\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d} x} \left(\text{f} \left(x\right)\right)` tell you to do?


**BACK**

**Differentiate** `\text{f} \left(x\right)` with respect to $x$.

Written this way it is an **instruction**, with the function it acts on placed in the brackets after it, and what it produces is the derivative.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_jhJS7QcQYGckyxhP`)

**FRONT**

Why can the gradient of a **chord** be found exactly, when the gradient of a tangent cannot be read off directly?


**BACK**

Because a chord joins **two known points** on the curve, so both of their coordinates can be worked out from the equation.

A tangent touches at only one point, so there is no second point to work with and drawing one gives only an estimate.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_n3dsb6FgBjP9XCpm`)

**FRONT**

In the chord construction for a derivative, why do you let $h$ tend to zero?


**BACK**

Because as the second point slides towards the first, the chord turns until it lies along the **tangent**.

The gradient of the tangent is therefore the **limit** the chord gradients approach, and that limit is what the derivative is defined to be.

## Card 13 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_G25mWHXfhq5RxMWm`)

**FRONT**

Complete the rule for differentiating a power of $x$, filling in the missing **coefficient** and **index**:

`\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}\left(x^{n}\right) = \_\_\_\_\_\_ x^{\_\_\_\_\_\_}`


**BACK**

The completed rule is:

`\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}\left(x^{n}\right) = n x^{n-1}`

Multiply by the **index**, then reduce the index by 1.

This works for any constant $n$, including negative and fractional values.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_5yD6JsRBwjQBkcyj`)

**FRONT**

How do you differentiate a term such as $- 4 x^{5}$, where a power of $x$ has a constant multiplier?


**BACK**

Differentiate the power of $x$ and keep the multiplier:

`\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}\left(ax^{n}\right) = anx^{n-1}`

So, **for example**, $- 4 x^{5}$ differentiates to $- 4 \times 5 x^{4} = - 20 x^{4}$.

## Card 15 — true_or_false (`fl_7VCT7XrDnnkmcMnV`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Differentiating $x^{-4}$ gives $- 4 x^{-3}$.


**BACK**

**False.**

The index is always **reduced** by 1, even when it is already negative: $- 4 - 1 = - 5$, so the derivative is $- 4 x^{-5}$.

Negative and fractional indices are a common place to lose marks for exactly this reason.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_y88kTBDGg9GQnKRM`)

**FRONT**

What must you do to terms such as $\sqrt{x}$ and $\frac{4}{x}$ before you can differentiate them?


**BACK**

Rewrite each one as a **power** of $x$, so that the rule for differentiating $x^{n}$ can be applied:

$\sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{1}{2}}$

$\frac{4}{x} = 4 x^{-1}$

The derivative is often converted back into root or fraction form at the end.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_WSPDgxnDsGDvh9Tz`)

**FRONT**

How do you differentiate an expression that is a sum or difference of several terms?


**BACK**

Differentiate the terms **one at a time**, keeping them in the same order.

So, **for example**, $y = x^{4} - \sqrt{x} + \frac{4}{x}$ gives:

$\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = 4 x^{3} - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} - \frac{4}{x^{2}}$

Notice that the last term changes sign, because differentiating $4 x^{-1}$ gives $- 4 x^{-2}$.

## Card 18 — true_or_false (`fl_7xsFTmPvHhkDwCxS`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The derivative of $y=\sqrt{7}$ is $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{7}}$.


**BACK**

**False.**

$\sqrt{7}$ is a **constant**: there is no $x$ in it. The derivative of any constant is **zero**, so $\frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}x} = 0$.

The graph of a constant is a horizontal line, which has gradient zero everywhere.

**FRONT**

Define the **normal** to a curve.


**BACK**

The **normal** at a point is the line through that point which is **perpendicular** to the tangent there.

Where there is no tangent, as at a sharp corner, there is no normal either.

## Card 27 — keyword_definition (`fl_wMJsvWB8HHMGZRSW`)

**FRONT**

Define **second derivative**.


**BACK**

The result of differentiating a function **twice**, written `\text{f}''\left(x\right)` or $\frac{\text{d}^{2}y}{\text{d}x^{2}}$.

It does **not** mean squaring the first derivative: the superscripts are part of the notation, not powers.

The second derivative of a straight line is zero.
