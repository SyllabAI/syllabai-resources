## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Mv6bwN2WFX5NgYYx`)

**FRONT**

Define **index**.


**BACK**

In $a^{n}$, the **index** is $n$, the number that the **base** $a$ is raised to.

It is also called the **power** or the **exponent**.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_5WfHGq9cgHzHvtj4`)

**FRONT**

How do you simplify $a^{m} \times a^{n}$ and $a^{m} \div a^{n}$?


**BACK**

Multiplying **adds** the indices: $a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m+n}$

Dividing **subtracts** them: $a^{m} \div a^{n} = a^{m-n}$

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_PgcRbfgdzy9MNfQd`)

**FRONT**

How can you simplify an expression involving powers whose terms have different bases?


**BACK**

Rewrite the terms so that they share a base, then use the index laws.

For example `9^{4} = \left(3^{2}\right)^{4} = 3^{8}`, so $9^{4} \div 3^{7} = 3^{8-7} = 3$

## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_739tKNWWwgpgX3YY`)

**FRONT**

Define **surd**.


**BACK**

If $n$ is a positive integer that is **not a square number**, then $\sqrt{n}$ is a **surd**. Any **rational multiple**, such as $5 \sqrt{2}$, is also a surd.

Surds are **irrational** numbers.


Spec links: `spcpt_GwXDbw6JDb2bSZmK`


## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_crHsYXSBMnvfksSw`)

**FRONT**

Why is $\sqrt{16}$ not a surd, when $\sqrt{13}$ is?


**BACK**

$16$ is a **square number**, so $\sqrt{16} = 4$, which is **rational**.

$13$ is not a square number, so $\sqrt{13}$ is **irrational**, and therefore a surd.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_qhTZzNPPwxGcbqgZ`)

**FRONT**

What is the reciprocal of $\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}$?


**BACK**

The fraction under the root **flips**:

$\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}} = \frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}$

## Card 14 — true_or_false (`fl_r69sMyDQynN6txFM`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{a+b}$


**BACK**

**False.**

You **cannot add or subtract under the surd**. The multiplication and division rules work, but there is no matching rule for addition or subtraction.

## Card 17 — keyword_definition (`fl_q4KB8gd26hhsRkRp`)

**FRONT**

Define **rationalising the denominator**.


**BACK**

**Rationalising the denominator** changes a fraction with surds in its denominator into an **equivalent fraction** whose denominator is rational, with any surds moved to the numerator.

How do you rationalise a denominator of the form $\sqrt{a}$?


**BACK**

Multiply the numerator and the denominator by $\sqrt{a}$, since $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a} = a$

## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_RzcFQZn8TywK2jyJ`)

**FRONT**

Why does multiplying by the conjugate of a denominator such as $5 - \sqrt{3}$ rationalise it?


**BACK**

It creates a **difference of squares**, and squaring removes the surds:

`\left(5 - \sqrt{3}\right)\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right) = 25 - 3 = 22`
