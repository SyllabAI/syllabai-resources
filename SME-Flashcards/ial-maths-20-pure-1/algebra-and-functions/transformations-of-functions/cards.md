## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_G2nBYqtv8FGRZPy3`)

**FRONT**

Define a **translation** of a graph.


**BACK**

A **translation** shifts a graph up, down, left or right in the $x y$ plane.

A particular translation is specified by a translation **vector**, giving how far horizontally and how far vertically.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_K83TT2QBVCcjPgTc`)

**FRONT**

Under `y = \text{f} \left(x\right) + a`, which coordinates of a point change?


**BACK**

Only the $y$-coordinates; every $x$-coordinate stays exactly where it was.

For `y = \text{f}\left(x + a\right)` it is the other way round, with the $y$-coordinates unchanged.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_JN2v3vwFv6PS7nTp`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A translation can change the shape of a graph.


**BACK**

**False.**

A translation only moves the graph: its shape, size and orientation are all left unchanged.

That is exactly what separates a translation from a stretch or a reflection.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_6tVdn4w4yZXp4FD4`)

**FRONT**

What happens to a graph's asymptotes under a translation?


**BACK**

They are translated along with the graph.

An asymptote **parallel to the direction of the translation** is the exception, and stays exactly where it is.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_nWJzSTkw63qs7xrd`)

**FRONT**

The graph of $y = \frac{1}{x}$ has an asymptote on each axis. Where are they after the translation to $y = \frac{1}{x-2} + 3$?


**BACK**

They move with the graph, to $x = 2$ and $y = 3$.

This is how a reciprocal graph can end up crossing an axis, which the untranslated version never does.

## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_fGFKq3wPmWf9z53z`)

**FRONT**

For any function, what do `y = a \text{f} \left(x\right)` and `y = \text{f} \left(a x\right)` do to the graph?


**BACK**

`y = a \text{f}\left(x\right)` is a **vertical** stretch of scale factor $a$, centred on the $x$-axis.

`y = \text{f}\left(a x\right)` is a **horizontal** stretch of scale factor $\frac{1}{a}$, centred on the $y$-axis.


Spec links: `spcpt_3fVyTss6mgDbqHS4`


## Card 8 — true_or_false (`fl_pmvD9WwR3vkybC5D`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

`y = \text{f}\left(3 x\right)` is a horizontal stretch of scale factor $3$.


**BACK**

**False.**

The scale factor is $\frac{1}{3}$, so the graph is **squashed** towards the $y$-axis rather than stretched away from it.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_DV4jYbxDscPXFhmc`)

**FRONT**

Which points do not move under a vertical stretch?


**BACK**

The points on the $x$-axis, because their $y$-coordinate is zero and multiplying zero by anything leaves it zero.

Under a horizontal stretch it is the points on the $y$-axis that stay put.

## Card 10 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_rPcqKf8M9d85dPSX`)

**FRONT**

Under `y = a \text{f} \left(x\right)` with $a > 1$, points move `\_\_\_\_\_\_` from the $x$-axis; with $0 < a < 1$ they move `\_\_\_\_\_\_` it.


**BACK**

Under `y = a \text{f}\left(x\right)` with $a > 1$, points move **away** from the $x$-axis; with $0 < a < 1$ they move **towards** it.

All of the movement is parallel to the $y$-axis, so nothing shifts sideways.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_St2Dnp2qQDrzZv4g`)

**FRONT**

Why is the scale factor of `y = \text{f} \left(a x\right)` equal to $\frac{1}{a}$ rather than $a$?


**BACK**

Because the graph reaches any given output at a **smaller** input: whatever $\text{f}$ did at $x$, `\text{f}\left(a x\right)` does at $\frac{x}{a}$.

A larger $a$ therefore pulls the graph inwards rather than stretching it out.

## Card 13 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_W958VFgmnDMqy5pm`)

**FRONT**

`y = - \text{f} \left(x\right)` is a reflection in the `\_\_\_\_\_\_`-axis, and `y = \text{f} \left(- x\right)` is a reflection in the `\_\_\_\_\_\_`-axis.


**BACK**

`y = - \text{f}\left(x\right)` is a reflection in the $x$-axis, and `y = \text{f}\left(- x\right)` is a reflection in the $y$-axis.

The minus **outside** the function acts on the outputs; the minus **inside** acts on the inputs.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_6VG26ZMsWVcxkqcb`)

**FRONT**

Which points stay where they are under a reflection?


**BACK**

The points **on the axis of reflection**.

For `y = - \text{f}\left(x\right)` those are the points on the $x$-axis, whose $y$-coordinate is zero and so is unchanged by a sign flip.


Spec links: `spcpt_ck5SsBrq9RDzWFJK`


## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_wVRSBK2m3Bdc5XPt`)

**FRONT**

Under `y = \text{f} \left(- x\right)`, what happens to the coordinates of a point?


**BACK**

The $y$-coordinates stay the same and the $x$-coordinates have their signs flipped.

Every point not already on the $y$-axis moves to the other side of it.


Spec links: `spcpt_ck5SsBrq9RDzWFJK`


## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_FMXt45zhkNW93YJH`)

**FRONT**

What happens to a graph's asymptotes under a reflection?


**BACK**

They are reflected too, exactly as a straight line would be.

An asymptote lying along a coordinate axis, or perpendicular to the axis being reflected in, is unaffected.


Spec links: `spcpt_ck5SsBrq9RDzWFJK`


## Card 17 — true_or_false (`fl_z6gWc4d4gGgQWk69`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A graph that is symmetrical about the $y$-axis is unchanged by `y = \text{f}\left(- x\right)`.


**BACK**

**True.**

If the graph already matches itself on both sides of the $y$-axis, reflecting it in that axis maps it exactly onto itself.

$y = x^{2}$ and $y = \mathrm{cos} x$ both behave this way.
