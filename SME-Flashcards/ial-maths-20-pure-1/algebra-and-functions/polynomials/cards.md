## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_v5XDnMnK8wjbYmfr`)

**FRONT**

What is the rule for expanding two brackets multiplied together?


**BACK**

Every term in one bracket must be multiplied by **every term** in the other.

So `\left(a + b\right) \left(x + y + z\right)` gives six products: $a x + a y + a z + b x + b y + b z$.

## Card 2 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_h7d2vsWnCKj6kHVX`)

**FRONT**

`\left(a + b\right)^{2}` expands to `a^{2} + \_\_\_\_\_\_ + b^{2}`.


**BACK**

`\left(a + b\right)^{2}` expands to $a^{2} + 2 a b + b^{2}$.

The middle term comes from the **two** cross-products, $a b$ and $b a$, which is exactly what is lost if you square each term separately.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_mV9SnHj4XbPwPzkd`)

**FRONT**

What does **FOIL** stand for, and when can you use it?


**BACK**

**First, Outside, Inside, Last.** It is the each-term-times-each-term rule carried out in a fixed order.

It only applies when **both** brackets contain exactly two terms.

**FRONT**

How do you expand three or more sets of brackets?


**BACK**

**Two at a time.**

Expand and simplify the first pair, then multiply that result by the next bracket, and so on.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_p4sXFwB4RdN7FdZg`)

**FRONT**

Define **factorising**.


**BACK**

**Factorising** is rewriting an expression that is a **sum of terms** as a **product of factors**.

It is the reverse of expanding: $x^{2} + 6 x - 16$ is a sum of three terms, and `\left(x + 8\right) \left(x - 2\right)` is the product of two linear factors.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_cbzhFtt4V2dMYqfT`)

**FRONT**

Whatever kind of expression you are factorising, what should you always check for first?


**BACK**

A **factor common to every term**, which may be a number, a letter, or both.

Taking it out first leaves a simpler expression to factorise: `10 x^{2} + 5 x = 5 x \left(2 x + 1\right)`.

**BACK**

The completed factorisation is:

`9 x^{2} - 49 y^{2} = \left(3 x - 7 y\right) \left(3 x + 7 y\right)`

Each bracket holds the **square roots** of the two terms, and the brackets differ only in the sign between them.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_DqKQyTwH45y5rJps`)

**FRONT**

In the 'ac' method for factorising $a x^{2} + b x + c$, which two numbers are you looking for?


**BACK**

Two numbers whose **product** is $a c$ and whose **sum** is $b$.

For $6 x^{2} + 7 x - 3$ that means a product of $- 18$ and a sum of $7$, so the numbers are $9$ and $- 2$.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_zKdXf6fxnBStsQM6`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The 'ac' method will factorise any quadratic expression that factorises at all, whatever the coefficient of $x^{2}$.


**BACK**

**True.**

Most factorising shortcuts only apply under particular conditions, such as the coefficient of $x^{2}$ being $1$, but the 'ac' method carries no such restriction.

It is most useful exactly where those shortcuts are hardest to use, when that coefficient is greater than $1$ and **not prime**.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_NgHdCGWyVM3xHfM7`)

**FRONT**

You have found the two numbers $m$ and $n$ needed by the 'ac' method on $a x^{2} + b x + c$. What do you do with them?


**BACK**

Split the middle term, writing $b x$ as $m x + n x$.

Factorising the first two terms and the last two terms then leaves a common bracket, which comes out as one of the factors: `4 x^{2} + 20 x - x - 5 = 4 x \left(x + 5\right) - \left(x + 5\right) = \left(x + 5\right) \left(4 x - 1\right)`.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_JvBXr62t8FWmJdkK`)

**FRONT**

Why can you always take out a factor of $x$ when factorising a cubic expression at this level?


**BACK**

Because a cubic at this level has **no constant term**, so every term contains at least one $x$.

What is left inside the bracket is a **quadratic**, which may itself factorise again: `4 x^{3} + 22 x^{2} + 30 x = 2 x \left(2 x^{2} + 11 x + 15\right) = 2 x \left(2 x + 5\right) \left(x + 3\right)`.
