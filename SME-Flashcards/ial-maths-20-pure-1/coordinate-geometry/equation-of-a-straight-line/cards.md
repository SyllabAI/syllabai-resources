## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_67CFyR36fM4SHPSM`)

**FRONT**

Define **line segment**.


**BACK**

A **line segment** is the part of a straight line lying between two given points.

Unlike a full line it has a definite length, which is what makes it possible to talk about its midpoint.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_HJjBThG5Dwk2R5dH`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the length of the line segment joining two points?


**BACK**

Use **Pythagoras' theorem** on the horizontal and vertical differences.

The distance between `\left(x_{1} , y_{1}\right)` and `\left(x_{2} , y_{2}\right)` is `\sqrt{\left(x_{2} - x_{1}\right)^{2} + \left(y_{2} - y_{1}\right)^{2}}`.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_QY7tmyn6RpW4dRXW`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the gradient of a line segment from its two end points?


**BACK**

Divide the change in $y$ by the change in $x$:

$m = \frac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}$

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_zs4cmynbJ5v2JqC3`)

**FRONT**

What are the two standard forms for the equation of a straight line?


**BACK**

$y = m x + c$, where $m$ is the gradient and $c$ the $y$-axis intercept.

$a x + b y + c = 0$, where $a$, $b$ and $c$ are **integers**.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_CQ9dR8Gvtt6bsCRJ`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

In $a x + b y + c = 0$, the letter $c$ is the $y$-axis intercept.


**BACK**

**False.**

That is only true of $y = m x + c$.

Setting $x = 0$ in $a x + b y + c = 0$ gives $y = - \frac{c}{b}$, so the same letter means something quite different in the two forms.

**BACK**

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_gcQtRqMh9Kp9wjwh`)

**FRONT**

A line has equation $3 x + 4 y - 12 = 0$. How do you find its gradient?


**BACK**

Rearrange it into $y = m x + c$: $4 y = - 3 x + 12$, so $y = - \frac{3}{4} x + 3$.

The gradient is therefore $- \frac{3}{4}$.

Parallel lines have `\_\_\_\_\_\_` gradients. Perpendicular lines have gradients whose `\_\_\_\_\_\_` is $- 1$.


**BACK**

Parallel lines have **equal** gradients. Perpendicular lines have gradients whose **product** is $- 1$.

So if one gradient is $m$, a line perpendicular to it has gradient $- \frac{1}{m}$.

## Card 15 — keyword_definition (`fl_cFCYvK5CymKQckgD`)

**FRONT**

Define **collinear**.


**BACK**

Two line segments are **collinear** if they are parts of the same straight line.

Extended far enough, they become one and the same line.


Spec links: `spcpt_vWW6kzvGRnCvPPnr`


## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_J2GfyRbX5gK5sYTk`)

**FRONT**

How do collinear line segments differ from parallel lines?


**BACK**

Both have equal gradients, but collinear segments lie on the **same** line while parallel lines never meet at all.

Rearranged into the same form, collinear equations turn out identical, whereas parallel ones differ in their intercepts.

## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_H4YTP85hxkYXh2ph`)

**FRONT**

In a straight-line model $y = m x + c$, what does the gradient $m$ represent?


**BACK**

The **rate of change** of $y$ with respect to $x$.

For a spring, it is the extra extension produced by each additional kilogram of mass.

## Card 22 — true_or_false (`fl_Xn6sf4Trv9CF6PX3`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

In a straight-line model, the gradient has units.


**BACK**

**True.**

The gradient is a rate, so its units are the $y$-units divided by the $x$-units.

For a spring measured in centimetres against a mass in kilograms, the gradient is in centimetres per kilogram.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_8nPPpQpR5RXWR9Zv`)

**FRONT**

Why might a straight-line model need refining?


**BACK**

Because real data rarely lies exactly on a straight line.

More readings, or readings taken over a wider range, can show that a different gradient or a different shape altogether fits better.
