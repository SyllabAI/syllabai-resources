## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_B627QVw5BTJB6nQ5`)

**FRONT**

Define the **unit circle**.


**BACK**

A circle of radius $1$ centred on the origin, used to define the trigonometric functions.

Angles on it are measured anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis.

## Card 5 — true_or_false (`fl_hMjSWCS9CrZgnWRc`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

On the unit circle, $\mathrm{cos} θ$ can be greater than $1$.


**BACK**

**False.**

Every point on the circle is exactly $1$ unit from the origin, so neither of its coordinates can exceed $1$.

That is why $\mathrm{sin} θ$ and $\mathrm{cos} θ$ always lie between $- 1$ and $1$.


Spec links: `spcpt_MMkgfX7dMcgYqnsz`


## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_Zf8xC8qSsb3SX2Yq`)

**FRONT**

Why does the unit circle have radius $1$ rather than any other value?


**BACK**

Because the hypotenuse of the triangle inside it is then $1$, so dividing by it changes nothing.

The two ratios therefore become simply the two coordinates of the point.

## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_4MjTXrXHHCcS638K`)

**FRONT**

How do you identify the opposite and adjacent sides in a right-angled triangle?


**BACK**

They are named **relative to the angle you are using**: the opposite is across from it, and the adjacent lies between it and the right angle.

The hypotenuse is the only side that never changes, being the longest and always opposite the right angle.


Spec links: `spcpt_Zzm9k5BsSf5z36TF`


## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_9Y5B24d9p2jKkgdw`)

**FRONT**

How do you find a missing **angle** in a right-angled triangle?


**BACK**

Work out the ratio of the two sides you know, then apply the **inverse** function, $\mathrm{sin}^{-1}$, $\mathrm{cos}^{-1}$ or $\mathrm{tan}^{-1}$.

Without the inverse you get the ratio back rather than the angle itself.

## Card 10 — true_or_false (`fl_hsvkxXNwtMrFkxCb`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

SOH CAH TOA can be used on any triangle.


**BACK**

**False.**

It works only in a **right-angled** triangle, because the ratios are defined against the hypotenuse.

Any other triangle needs the sine rule or the cosine rule instead.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_R98dkf7kzjHSNWhc`)

**FRONT**

How can you check that a side length you have found is sensible?


**BACK**

Compare it against the sides you already know.

The hypotenuse has to be the **longest** side, so an answer longer than it, or a hypotenuse shorter than one of the others, must be wrong.

**BACK**

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_8mY8mfj3Dj9YpMsR`)

**FRONT**

When do you use the **sine rule**?


**BACK**

When the question involves **opposite pairs** of a side and its angle.

You need one complete pair, plus one other side or angle to work from.


Spec links: `spcpt_nzB9Nyk9jpsF5HPY`


## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_3Rk8vmZjM99RsCyv`)

**FRONT**

When do you use the **cosine rule**?


**BACK**

When you are given an angle **between** two known sides, or all **three** sides.

Neither of those situations gives you a complete opposite pair to work with.


Spec links: `spcpt_nzB9Nyk9jpsF5HPY`


## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_cwnjfz5qkqrxTN6r`)

**FRONT**

Why might the angle your calculator gives from the sine rule not be the one you want?


**BACK**

Because it always returns the **acute** angle, and the triangle may need the obtuse one instead.

Subtracting from $180 ^{\circ}$ gives the other possibility, and the shape of the triangle decides which fits.


Spec links: `spcpt_nzB9Nyk9jpsF5HPY`


## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_yfrtY727mVkt9QJh`)

**FRONT**

What is the formula for the area of any triangle?


**BACK**

$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} a b \mathrm{sin} C$

The angle $C$ has to be the one **between** the two sides $a$ and $b$.

**FRONT**

What is the first thing to check before choosing a rule for a triangle?


**BACK**

Whether the triangle is **right-angled**.

If it is, the simple trigonometric ratios will do the job far more quickly.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_6XVkDfnBVxNw6nSg`)

**FRONT**

Two angles of a triangle are given. How does that help?


**BACK**

The third angle follows immediately, since the three add to $180 ^{\circ}$.

That often turns a problem with no usable opposite pair into one that has.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The sine rule and cosine rule work on right-angled triangles as well.


**BACK**

**True.**

Both hold for **any** triangle, right-angled or not.

For a right-angled triangle the simple ratios are quicker, which is why they are used instead, not because the rules fail.
