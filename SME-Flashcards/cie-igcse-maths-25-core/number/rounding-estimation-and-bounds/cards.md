## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_jNh8mP9VpJHRCvkw`)

**FRONT**

When rounding, how do you decide whether to round up or down?


**BACK**

Look at the digit immediately to the **right** of the place you are rounding to.

If it is **5 or more** round up, and if it is **less than 5** round down.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_JQ34BSxVsNc9gzn7`)

**FRONT**

Why do you have to take care when the digit in the place you are rounding to is a $9$?


**BACK**

Rounding a $9$ up makes it $10$, which carries into the next place value up.

Rounding $1798$ to the nearest $10$ gives $1800$, so the hundreds digit changes as well.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$2 . 395$ rounded to $2$ decimal places should be written as $2 . 4$.


**BACK**

**False.**

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_ftYK8nHJTsT24kzs`)

**FRONT**

Why does $1567 . 45$ round to $1600$ and not to $16$?


**BACK**

The zeros are **place holders**, keeping the $1$ and the $6$ in the thousands and hundreds columns.

Without them the number would be a hundred times too small.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_6tTv2crpyDcbQZDy`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the **first significant figure** of a number such as $0 . 006207$?


**BACK**

Read from the left and take the first **non-zero** digit, which here is the $6$.

The zeros in front of it only show place value, so they are not significant.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_kJDFzGbXYBCCQtZZ`)

**FRONT**

A class of $31$ students needs at least one adult for every $10$ students. Why must $31 \div 10 = 3 . 1$ be rounded **up**?


**BACK**

Three adults would only be enough for $30$ students, leaving one student without an adult.

So $4$ adults are needed.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_T4Fmq9nVCn2qzjGm`)

**FRONT**

A farmer has $50$ apples and each crate holds $12$. Why is $50 \div 12 = 4 . 16 \dots$ rounded **down**?


**BACK**

A fifth full crate would need $60$ apples, so only $4$ crates can be filled.

In a real situation the direction of the rounding depends on what is being counted, not on the digit after the decimal point.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_KK2VYKs5PCwxrMnN`)

**FRONT**

What is the general rule for rounding numbers before estimating a calculation?


**BACK**

Round each number to $1$ significant figure, then carry out the calculation with the rounded numbers.

So $7 . 8$ becomes $8$, and $1080$ becomes $1000$.

## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_jgqqSNC9vVp7yHc8`)

**FRONT**

Why estimate a calculation you are going to work out exactly anyway?


**BACK**

The estimate acts as a **check** on the exact answer.

If the exact answer turns out much bigger or much smaller than the estimate, there is a mistake in the working.

## Card 15 — question_and_answer (`fl_t7h4CHpRbzBSnhFV`)

**FRONT**

An estimate of $\frac{17.3\times3.81}{11.5}$ uses $\frac{20\times4}{10} = 8$. Is $8$ an overestimate or an underestimate, and why?


**BACK**

$8$ is an **overestimate**.

The numbers on top were rounded **up** while the number underneath was rounded **down**, and both of those changes make a fraction bigger.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_6vH5MxjWsvc2vqhM`)

**FRONT**

When is it better to round to something other than $1$ significant figure?


**BACK**

When a different value makes the arithmetic easier, such as taking $16 . 2$ to $15$ or $1180$ to $1200$.

An estimate is meant to be a quick calculation you can do in your head.

## Card 18 — keyword_definition (`fl_GnN4HKnXVSMFHR39`)

**FRONT**

Define the **bounds** of a rounded number.


**BACK**

The **bounds** are the two values that the true, unrounded number must lie between.

The **lower bound** is the smallest it could be, and the **upper bound** the largest it is allowed to approach.


Spec links: `spcpt_6CCYgyRn7Nf6M5cb`


## Card 19 — question_and_answer (`fl_hVDZjBryrmt8hQzt`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the upper and lower bounds of a rounded number?


**BACK**

Halve the **degree of accuracy** it was rounded to, then add that half for the upper bound and subtract it for the lower bound.

For $24800$ rounded to the nearest $100$, half of $100$ is $50$, giving $24850$ and $24750$.

## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_Qv4XCH4K2PpVNKcG`)

**FRONT**

A stick is measured as $8$ cm, to the nearest centimetre. What are its bounds?


**BACK**

The lower bound is $7 . 5$ cm and the upper bound is $8 . 5$ cm.

The degree of accuracy is $1$ cm, so the true length lies within $0 . 5$ cm either side of $8$ cm.

## Card 22 — true_or_false (`fl_WvBF9BwGZNNkVf98`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

The error interval for a number rounded to the nearest $100$ is wider than for one rounded to the nearest $10$.


**BACK**

**True.**

The interval always has a width equal to the **degree of accuracy** itself.

So the nearest $100$ gives an interval of width $100$, and the nearest $10$ one of width only $10$.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_k9KNyZm5yJZZyRP9`)

**FRONT**

How do you read the degree of accuracy from the way a rounding is described?


**BACK**

It may be given as a **place value**, so $1$ decimal place means $0 . 1$ and $2$ decimal places means $0 . 01$.

Or it may be given as a **measure**, so to the nearest metre means $1$ m, and to the nearest $100$ means $100$.
