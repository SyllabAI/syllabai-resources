## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_NzhQmJHsrs5ND449`)

**FRONT**

Define a **function**.


**BACK**

A **function** is a rule, made of one or more mathematical operations, that turns each **input** into an **output**.

The rule **double it and add 1** turns 3 into 7, and turns $x$ into $2 x + 1$.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_4q2FwYnrP23hyXWb`)

**FRONT**

Why might a question use $\text{g}$ or $\text{h}$ instead of $\text{f}$?


**BACK**

Because a **new letter** is used for each **new function**, so every rule in a question has its own name.

Any letter can be used, and a function may also be written as $y = \dots$ with no letter name at all.

## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_K3XfNy2sC42DYHHD`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If the input to a function is $t + 5$, the output `\text{f}\left(t + 5\right)` can be simplified without knowing what $\text{f}$ does.


**BACK**

**False.**

Until the rule for $\text{f}$ is known, `\text{f}\left(t + 5\right)` is simply the name of the output for that input.

Once the rule is given you can substitute, so for `\text{f}\left(x\right) = 2 x + 1` it becomes `2 \left(t + 5\right) + 1`.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_6HQgWK8RSy8MDmVn`)

**FRONT**

Given `\text{f}\left(x\right) = 3 x^{2} - 2 x + 1`, find `\text{f}\left(7\right)`.


**BACK**

`\text{f}\left(7\right) = 134`.

Replace every $x$ with 7, which gives `3 \left(7\right)^{2} - 2 \left(7\right) + 1 = 147 - 14 + 1`.

## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_zGvPSTmSFjfGN78C`)

**FRONT**

Given `\text{g}\left(x\right) = 3 x - 4`, find the value of $x$ for which `\text{g}\left(x\right) = -16`.


**BACK**

$x = - 4$.

Replace `\text{g}\left(x\right)` with $3 x - 4$ to form the equation $3 x - 4 = - 16$, then solve it by adding 4 to both sides and dividing by 3.

## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_9qYRyrMWszxNqXrR`)

**FRONT**

Define a **mapping diagram**.


**BACK**

A **mapping diagram** shows a whole set of inputs on one side and the set of outputs they produce on the other.

Each input is joined by an arrow to the output that the function turns it into.

## Card 8 — keyword_definition (`fl_QnPyWdSVNmrNbWyM`)

**FRONT**

Define a **local maximum** of a graph.


**BACK**

A **local maximum** is a **turning point** where the curve stops rising and begins to fall, so it is the highest point in its own neighbourhood.

It need not be the highest point on the whole graph, which is exactly what the word **local** is there to signal.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_4GYx3JvWdDQj22cz`)

**FRONT**

What key features can the graph analysis menu on a calculator find for you?


**BACK**

It finds the **zeros** (also called roots), the **local maximum and minimum points**, and the **intersection** of two graphs.

On some models this menu is called **analyze graph**, and on others it is reached with the **G-Solv** button.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_v4S8jgyRdCNtcJwQ`)

**FRONT**

Why must you click either side of a point before the calculator will find it?


**BACK**

The calculator searches only inside the interval you have marked, so the two clicks tell it **where to look**.

A graph can have several zeros or several turning points, and this is how you say **which one** you want.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_j7RdTj4rRGftmgHR`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

To sketch a graph from your calculator you should first make a table of values and plot the points.


**BACK**

**False.**

A **sketch** needs no plotted points and no table of values at all.

You copy the shape as a **single smooth freehand curve** and mark the key features on it, and you do not use a ruler for a curve.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_9Mc8pKPcBHJ2dpyq`)

**FRONT**

What do the graphs of $y = 4$ and $x = 2$ look like?


**BACK**

$y = 4$ is a **horizontal** line crossing the $y$-axis at 4, and $x = 2$ is a **vertical** line crossing the $x$-axis at 2.

Whichever letter is absent from the equation is the one that is free to take any value.


Spec links: `spcpt_t5Q8y7Yrg3nrrNJC`


## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_vhwvk6jZY4ztgQFd`)

**FRONT**

What do the graphs of $y = x$ and $y = - x$ look like?


**BACK**

Both are straight lines through the **origin**, one with gradient 1 and the other with gradient $- 1$.

$y = x$ slopes up from left to right and $y = - x$ slopes down, and on equal axis scales they sit at $45 ^{\circ}$ to the axes.

## Card 18 — keyword_definition (`fl_54wxhcbpK9pYFVdM`)

**FRONT**

Define a **parabola**.


**BACK**

A **parabola** is the smooth, symmetrical curve that a quadratic graph makes.

It is shaped either like a u or like an n, and it never has straight sections or corners.

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_FVChpc9j9fkznnDs`)

**FRONT**

Where is the line of symmetry of a quadratic graph?


**BACK**

It is the **vertical** line through the **vertex**, which is the graph's turning point.

Its equation is $x = k$, where $k$ is the $x$-coordinate of that vertex.

## Card 21 — true_or_false (`fl_v8z7K5GVvzKV3kvd`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A quadratic graph can meet the $x$-axis at exactly one point.


**BACK**

**True.**

When the curve just **touches** the axis at its vertex instead of passing through it, there is exactly one root.

A quadratic may instead have two roots, or none at all if the whole curve stays on one side of the axis.


Spec links: `spcpt_t5Q8y7Yrg3nrrNJC`


## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_z9SFqfssgxrNdGx2`)

**FRONT**

The roots of a quadratic are where its graph crosses the $x$-axis. Why are two such roots always symmetric about the line of symmetry?


**BACK**

Because reflecting the curve in that line leaves it unchanged, so each root must map onto the other.

That puts the vertex exactly **halfway** between the two roots.

## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_FFSjfbDCWpXHZCVX`)

**FRONT**

How many times does a quadratic graph cross the $y$-axis?


**BACK**

Exactly **once**, always.

A graph meets the $y$-axis where $x = 0$, and a quadratic gives just one $y$ value for each $x$ value, so there can only be one such point.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_9tKG6YWj7k5mFRym`)

**FRONT**

How do you solve `\text{f}_{1} \left(x\right) = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` using a graphical method?


**BACK**

Draw `y = \text{f}_{1} \left(x\right)` and `y = \text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)` on the same axes, then find their **points of intersection**.

The solutions are the $x$-coordinates of those points, and not the full coordinate pairs.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_8y7HHqpvyYfDKFVP`)

**FRONT**

How do you put a second graph onto the same axes on a calculator?


**BACK**

Enter it on the next function line down, `\text{f}_{2} \left(x\right)`, underneath the first one.

The **tab** key is often a shortcut for opening that second line, and both curves are then drawn together.

## Card 26 — true_or_false (`fl_gSWsXrnsyV7BbRdN`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Solving `\text{f} \left(x\right) = 0` on a graph means finding where the curve crosses the $x$-axis.


**BACK**

**True.**

The right-hand side is $y = 0$, which is the equation of the $x$-axis itself.

The intersections you are looking for are therefore the curve's $x$-intercepts, which the calculator calls its **zeros**.

## Card 27 — question_and_answer (`fl_nXybyH3RPBqNKhG7`)

**FRONT**

To solve $x^{2} + 3 x + 1 = 1$ on a calculator, what second graph do you draw?


**BACK**

You draw $y = 1$, which is a **horizontal line** cutting the $y$-axis at 1.

A constant on one side of an equation is always a horizontal line, however complicated the other side happens to be.

**BACK**

## Card 29 — question_and_answer (`fl_Sgy9DHHK7qWG5SPP`)

**FRONT**

You already have $y = x^{2} + 3 x + 1$ drawn. How can you use it to solve $x^{2} + 3 x - 4 = 0$?


**BACK**

Rearrange the new equation so that the graph you already have appears on one side, by adding 5 to both sides.

That turns it into $x^{2} + 3 x + 1 = 5$, so you need only add the line $y = 5$ rather than redraw anything.

## Card 30 — question_and_answer (`fl_dVcD66Tv7qFTSDnx`)

**FRONT**

How can you be sure you have found **all** the solutions of an equation from a graph?


**BACK**

Count **every** place the two graphs cross, checking that the viewing window is wide enough to show them all.

A curve and a line can meet more than once: a cubic and a straight line may cross at **three** points, giving three solutions.
