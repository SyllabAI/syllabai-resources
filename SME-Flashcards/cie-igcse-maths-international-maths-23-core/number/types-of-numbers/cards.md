## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_2DKj46SH978MqT7y`)

**FRONT**

Define an **integer**.


**BACK**

An **integer** is a whole number, and it may be **positive, negative or zero**.

So $- 3$, $0$ and $17$ are all integers, whereas $2 . 5$ and $\frac{1}{4}$ are not.

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every natural number is a positive integer.


**BACK**

**False.**

They run 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on.

## Card 3 — keyword_definition (`fl_ZHZs9fnXG9sHkDGt`)

**FRONT**

Define a **rational number**.


**BACK**

A **rational number** is one that can be written as a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ in which $a$ and $b$ are **integers** and $b$ is not zero.

The fraction does not have to be in its simplest form for the number to count as rational.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 4 — true_or_false (`fl_BXMkkwVdRywhyf2Q`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Every integer is a rational number.


**BACK**

**True.**

Any integer can be written over a denominator of 1, so $6 = \frac{6}{1}$ fits the definition exactly.

That makes every whole number rational, negative ones included.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_93CwX3XDnf5hVgrG`)

**FRONT**

Which kinds of decimal are always rational numbers?


**BACK**

Both **terminating** decimals and **recurring** decimals are always rational.

A terminating decimal such as $0 . 32$ is $\frac{32}{100}$, and a recurring decimal such as $0 . 444 \dots$ is $\frac{4}{9}$.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_mgBGhPJ83xHwRvJH`)

**FRONT**

How is a recurring decimal written using dots?


**BACK**

A dot goes above the **first** and the **last** digit of the repeating block.

So `0 . \overset{\cdot}{5} 82 \overset{\cdot}{4}` stands for $0 . 582458245824 \dots$ repeating forever.

A single repeating digit needs only one dot, and a bar drawn over the block means the same thing.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 7 — keyword_definition (`fl_yVN7SyWG75wBYJdD`)

**FRONT**

Define an **irrational number**.


**BACK**

An **irrational** number **cannot** be written as a fraction of two integers.

Its decimal form never ends and never settles into a repeating pattern, which is what makes writing it as a fraction impossible.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_YvKsmbXBpcRgHbFK`)

**FRONT**

Why is $π$ an irrational number?


**BACK**

Because its decimal places carry on forever with **no pattern** and **no recurring digits**.

A fraction involving $π$, such as $\frac{π}{2}$, is irrational too, because rewriting it as a fraction of integers would make $π$ itself rational.


Spec links: `spcpt_D8sx4bXYrZdtkrJz`


## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_NrNHySQQYwqfJ5FK`)

**FRONT**

Define a **multiple** of a whole number.


**BACK**

A **multiple** of a whole number is what you get when you multiply it by a **positive integer**.

For example, 12 is a multiple of 3 because $12 = 3 \times 4$.

## Card 11 — keyword_definition (`fl_KCb92w9GWYGTcnnS`)

**FRONT**

Define a **factor** of a whole number.


**BACK**

A **factor** of a whole number divides into it **exactly**, leaving **no remainder**.

For example, 6 is a factor of 18 because $18 \div 6 = 3$.


Spec links: `spcpt_TMZ9PCbwcPC9S63s`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_Gf4hXfzCSFBP8d5V`)

**FRONT**

How do you make sure you have found **every** factor of a number?


**BACK**

Work through **factor pairs** in order, starting with 1 and the number itself, then testing 2, 3, 4 and so on.

You can stop as soon as the next number you would test is already in your list, because everything beyond that point has already been paired up.


Spec links: `spcpt_TMZ9PCbwcPC9S63s`


## Card 13 — true_or_false (`fl_QTszB6KVhYWVrk8F`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A number that does not divide by 3 cannot divide by 6 either.


**BACK**

**True.**

Every multiple of 6 is also a multiple of 3, since $6 = 3 \times 2$.

So if 3 is not a factor then 6 cannot be one, and the same argument rules out 9, 12 and every other multiple of 3.


Spec links: `spcpt_TMZ9PCbwcPC9S63s`


## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_xHxqn8kh3xHqpY3w`)

**FRONT**

How can you test whether a number divides by 3, or by 9, without dividing?


**BACK**

Add up the digits: the number divides by **3** if the digit sum divides by 3, and by **9** if the digit sum divides by 9.

For 2574 the digits give $2 + 5 + 7 + 4 = 18$, which divides by both, so 2574 divides by 3 and by 9.


Spec links: `spcpt_TMZ9PCbwcPC9S63s`


## Card 15 — keyword_definition (`fl_6hVSD4B8vGx9sdrc`)

**FRONT**

Define a **prime number**.


**BACK**

A **prime number** is a whole number with **exactly two different factors**: itself and 1.

So 7 is prime, because nothing except 1 and 7 divides into it exactly.


Spec links: `spcpt_fDJc5vx3rHjF7mqC`


## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_qwzJ93FKyFx2JDhg`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

1 is a prime number.


**BACK**

**False.**

1 has only **one** factor, itself, so it does not have the two different factors that a prime number needs.

The smallest prime number is 2.

**2** is the only even prime number.

Every other even number has 2 as a factor as well as 1 and itself, so it has more than two factors and cannot be prime.

**FRONT**

Define a **cube number**.


**BACK**

A **cube number** is the result of multiplying a whole number by itself **twice**.

In algebra this is written $a \times a \times a = a^{3}$, so $3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$ makes 27 a cube number.

## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_r67GjM3HNSVQGbK4`)

**FRONT**

What does the **cube root** of a number mean, and what is `\sqrt[3]{64}`?


**BACK**

The **cube root** of a number is the value that gives that number when multiplied by itself twice, so it **undoes cubing**.

`\sqrt[3]{64} = 4` because $4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64$, and in the same way `\sqrt[3]{1000} = 10`.

**FRONT**

**BACK**

The **reciprocal** of a number is what you get when you **divide 1 by it**.

So the reciprocal of 3 is $\frac{1}{3}$, and the reciprocal of $\frac{1}{3}$ is 3.
