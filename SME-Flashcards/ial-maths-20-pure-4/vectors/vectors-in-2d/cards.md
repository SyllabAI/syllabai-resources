## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Y4xv8tqJd67V8sS2`)

**FRONT**

Define **vector**.


**BACK**

A quantity with both **magnitude** and **direction**, representing a movement of a given size in a given direction.

It is the direction that separates a vector from a scalar, which carries size alone.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_J8bC7Xz69hPrt7T5`)

**FRONT**

What is the difference between a speed of $7$ and a velocity of $7$?


**BACK**

**Speed** is a scalar, so the number is the whole story.

**Velocity** is a vector and needs a direction too, which is why velocities of $7$ and $- 7$ describe the same speed travelling opposite ways.

How do you write vector notation by hand?


**BACK**

**Underline** the letter, since bold cannot be written by hand.

A vector printed as $a$ is written `bottom enclose a` in your own working, and the underlining is not optional.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_CCN6dw2BmNwkYn5W`)

**FRONT**

Why is a vector often described as a journey?


**BACK**

Because it records only the movement from one point to another, and not where that movement began.

The same vector can be drawn anywhere on a diagram and it is still the same vector.

## Card 5 — true_or_false (`fl_6Drp3XyfFCt25MRP`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Two vectors drawn in different places on a diagram can still be equal.


**BACK**

**True.**

Vectors are equal if they have the same **magnitude and direction**, wherever they happen to be drawn.

Opposite sides of a parallelogram are the standard example: same length, same direction, different positions.


Spec links: `spcpt_ts8KGt4XJ4wcQFvV`


## Card 6 — question_and_answer (`fl_964rGHBs3XbdnQJn`)

**FRONT**

How do you find the magnitude of a 2D vector?


**BACK**

By **Pythagoras' theorem** on its components, so `\left|x \mathbf{i} + y \mathbf{j}\right| = \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}`.

The two components are the shorter sides of a right-angled triangle and the vector itself is the hypotenuse.


Spec links: `spcpt_Mjs3DbfDZSWQm4sw`


## Card 7 — question_and_answer (`fl_qPhJhwt6H39Dgq9W`)

**FRONT**

How is the direction of a 2D vector specified?


**BACK**

As an angle measured anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis, unless the question says otherwise.

It is found from $\mathrm{tan} θ = \frac{y}{x}$, using the components as the two sides of a right-angled triangle.


Spec links: `spcpt_Mjs3DbfDZSWQm4sw`


## Card 8 — question_and_answer (`fl_8CsXDJHmyFbJvV6Q`)

**FRONT**

A vector has magnitude $r$ and direction $θ$. What are its components?


**BACK**

$x = r \mathrm{cos} θ$ and $y = r \mathrm{sin} θ$, which is the same right-angled triangle read the other way round.

Writing a vector in component form this way is called **resolving** it.


Spec links: `spcpt_Mjs3DbfDZSWQm4sw`


## Card 9 — true_or_false (`fl_9RS29s7XxRtdqQWh`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\mathrm{tan} θ = \frac{y}{x}$ always gives the direction of the vector.


**BACK**

**False.**

A calculator returns an angle between $- 90 ^{\circ}$ and $90 ^{\circ}$, so it cannot distinguish a vector from the one pointing in exactly the opposite direction.

A sketch settles which **quadrant** the vector lies in, and the angle is adjusted to match.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_JYYNJGSXPjZFMhmG`)

**FRONT**

What is the relationship between $a$ and $- a$?


**BACK**

The same **magnitude**, and exactly the **opposite direction**.

Every component changes sign, so $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$ becoming $\overset{\rightarrow}{CB}$ reverses the journey without changing its length.

## Card 11 — keyword_definition (`fl_QXTJKK38mjYmqhVF`)

**FRONT**

Define **resultant** vector.


**BACK**

The single vector produced by adding two or more vectors together.

It describes the overall movement from the start of the first to the end of the last, whatever route was taken in between.


Spec links: `spcpt_2HRssHdD9m3yg6JN`


## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_hYmr3qxM5zc5wy68`)

**FRONT**

How do you add two vectors?


**BACK**

Place them **nose to tail**, so that the second begins where the first finishes, and the resultant runs from the very start to the very end.

In component form you simply add the corresponding components.


Spec links: `spcpt_2HRssHdD9m3yg6JN`


## Card 13 — question_and_answer (`fl_ZT6SSrdRGHdNmfYZ`)

**FRONT**

What does subtracting a vector mean?


**BACK**

**Adding the negative** of it, so $a - b$ means `\mathbf{a} + \left(- \mathbf{b}\right)`.

On a diagram that is travelling along $b$ backwards.


Spec links: `spcpt_2HRssHdD9m3yg6JN`


## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_kFXF73XbND24GVtw`)

**FRONT**

There is no direct route from $A$ to $B$ on a diagram. How do you find $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$?


**BACK**

Take a **detour** through points you do know, such as $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AO} + \overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$.

Every route from $A$ to $B$ gives the same resultant, so choose whichever one uses vectors you already have.

## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_Xj6DXgQwctCK94Th`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Multiplying a vector by a positive scalar changes its direction.


**BACK**

**False.**

A positive scalar changes only the **size**: $3 a$ points the same way as $a$ and is three times as long.

It takes a **negative** scalar to reverse the direction.

## Card 17 — keyword_definition (`fl_rqyz4HNgWqhST2mV`)

**FRONT**

Define **position vector**.


**BACK**

A vector describing the position of a point **relative to the origin**, written $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$ or simply $a$.

Unlike other vectors it is tied to one place, since it must always start at $O$.


Spec links: `spcpt_6fw2v3vcB3GvKrhp`


## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_vXG2ScMF9zwJsxmp`)

**FRONT**

How does a position vector differ from a displacement vector?


**BACK**

A **position** vector is measured from the origin, so it fixes where a point actually is.

A **displacement** vector runs between any two points and gives only the direction and distance from one to the other.


Spec links: `spcpt_6fw2v3vcB3GvKrhp`

## Card 20 — question_and_answer (`fl_FR2n72rY72rZTjtb`)

**FRONT**

How are a point's coordinates related to its position vector?


**BACK**

They are the same numbers: the point `\left(x , y\right)` has position vector $x i + y j$.

That is what lets a vector question be answered with coordinate geometry, and a coordinate question be answered with vectors.

## Card 21 — true_or_false (`fl_xQpcTmXRWFyKw3ZP`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ and $\overset{\rightarrow}{BA}$ are the same vector.


**BACK**

**False.**

They share a magnitude but point opposite ways, so $\overset{\rightarrow}{BA} = - \overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$.

In terms of position vectors, one is $b - a$ and the other is $a - b$.


Spec links: `spcpt_6fw2v3vcB3GvKrhp`


## Card 22 — question_and_answer (`fl_8hkw3qKPGr9McsBv`)

**FRONT**

$P$ divides $A B$ in the ratio $2 : 3$. How do you find its position vector?


**BACK**

Travel to $A$, then go the right fraction of the way along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$, giving `\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{a} + \frac{2}{5} \left(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\right)`.

The fraction is the first number of the ratio over the **total**, so $2 : 3$ gives $\frac{2}{5}$ and never $\frac{2}{3}$.


Spec links: `spcpt_Fj2Hmc45nNr3xrF3`


## Card 23 — question_and_answer (`fl_FG92JyNBkWWTgGWB`)

**FRONT**

What is the position vector of the midpoint of $A B$?


**BACK**

`\frac{1}{2} \left(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\right)`, which is the average of the two position vectors.

It is the ratio result with $1 : 1$, putting the point half of the way along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ from $A$.

## Card 24 — question_and_answer (`fl_S74szSMnzdyHccMm`)

**FRONT**

What does a vector proof of a geometric fact look like?


**BACK**

Express every vector you need in terms of two known ones, then show the relationship you want algebraically.

Choosing which two to build everything from is the whole difficulty, and a labelled diagram is what makes the choice obvious.


Spec links: `spcpt_Fj2Hmc45nNr3xrF3`


## Card 25 — question_and_answer (`fl_FsWfKPnMWdBCygMR`)

**FRONT**

Why use vectors to prove a geometric result at all?


**BACK**

Because a relationship that would otherwise need a construction or an angle chase becomes ordinary algebra.

Parallel, collinear and ratio facts all turn into statements about scalar multiples, which are easy to check.

## Card 26 — true_or_false (`fl_NSRZC9F8ss77kWgW`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

If $P$ divides $A B$ in the ratio $3 : 1$, then $P$ is three quarters of the way from $A$ to $B$.


**BACK**

**True.**

The ratio splits $A B$ into $3 + 1 = 4$ equal parts, and $P$ sits at the end of the third.

So `\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{a} + \frac{3}{4} \left(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\right)`.
