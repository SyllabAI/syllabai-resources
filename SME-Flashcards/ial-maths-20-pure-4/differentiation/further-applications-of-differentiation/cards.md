## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_48MVk6PJxM5RvTwS`)

**FRONT**

Define **connected rates of change**.


**BACK**

A situation involving more than two variables, where the **chain rule** links several rates of change into a single equation.

The rate you actually want is usually built out of rates you can find, rather than being available directly.

## Card 2 — fill_in_the_blanks (`fl_bdSCMBgCXnwyD6HT`)

**FRONT**

Complete the chain rule linking three rates of change:

`\frac{\text{d} V}{\text{d} t} = \frac{\text{d} V}{\text{d} r} \times \_\_\_\_\_\_`


**BACK**

The completed chain is:

$\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}t} = \frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}r} \times \frac{\text{d}r}{\text{d}t}$

The two inner letters cancel in the way they would in ordinary fractions, which is a quick check that the right rates have been chained together.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_3rr3jpV8tmmMDhJm`)

**FRONT**

Where does the middle rate of change in a chain usually come from?


**BACK**

**Differentiating a formula**, either one the question supplies or one you are expected to know.

A sphere's $V = \frac{4}{3} π r^{3}$, for instance, differentiates to give $\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}r}$.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_M9QjKPrbbfVVJfSH`)

**FRONT**

Why must you be clear which letters are variables and which are constants?


**BACK**

Because the two behave completely differently under differentiation: a constant disappears, while a variable does not.

Treating a fixed radius as a variable, or the other way round, changes the derivative entirely.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_7pyBNVGvCZBs29Yj`)

**FRONT**

What is the role of connected rates of change in a differential equation problem?


**BACK**

They are how the equation gets **set up** from the information given.

Solving it afterwards is a separate matter of integration, and assembling the right chain of rates is often the harder half.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_4m4tYqkD3SsGVTRX`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

A rate of change is always a rate with respect to time.


**BACK**

**False.**

A rate of change is with respect to whatever variable sits underneath, so $\frac{\text{d}V}{\text{d}r}$ is a rate with respect to **radius**.

Rates with respect to time are simply the commonest kind in these particular problems.
