## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Fkkt3S4DSqPh44hw`)

**FRONT**

Define **chiral centre**


**BACK**

A **chiral centre** is a carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups of atoms. It is described as asymmetric, as the molecule has no plane of symmetry.

## Card 2 — keyword_definition (`fl_J2zHRrGydbYn4Zc3`)

**FRONT**

What are **enantiomers**?


**BACK**

**Enantiomers** are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. They arise from a chiral centre and differ in how they interact with plane-polarised light and other chiral molecules.


Spec links: `spcpt_tsNxtB8fCWSM7m8h`


## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_RtsPYnq5vxHVWmXg`)

**FRONT**

How many optical isomers arise from one chiral centre?


**BACK**

One chiral centre gives rise to **two** optical isomers (a pair of enantiomers). The relationship is: number of stereoisomers = 2<sup>n</sup>, where n is the number of chiral centres.

## Card 6 — true_or_false (`fl_2FNhfj2x4dxx6s3J`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Enantiomers are superimposable mirror images of each other.


**BACK**

**False.**

Enantiomers are **non-superimposable** mirror images of each other. Just as a left hand cannot be placed on top of a right hand to match exactly, enantiomers cannot be superimposed.

## Card 9 — keyword_definition (`fl_MPQ4Jw2prH5gDN5H`)

**FRONT**

Define **racemic mixture**


**BACK**

A **racemic mixture** (or racemate) is a mixture containing equal amounts of each enantiomer. It is optically inactive because the two enantiomers cancel out each other's rotation of plane-polarised light.


Spec links: `spcpt_G4zbHnYzQG9Dg3jg`


## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_6fKP4SHjqPtgkrPK`)

**FRONT**

How do the two enantiomers in a pair differ in their effect on plane-polarised light?


**BACK**

One enantiomer rotates plane-polarised light **clockwise** (+) and the other rotates it **anticlockwise** (-) by the same number of degrees.

**FRONT**

Why are many pharmaceutical drugs sold as racemic mixtures rather than as pure enantiomers?


**BACK**

Separating enantiomers is very **expensive and time-consuming**. For many drugs, the cost is not considered worthwhile, even though only one enantiomer may be pharmacologically active.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_P8s8FBGxw3fvN5Zt`)

**FRONT**

Why does an S<sub>N</sub>1 reaction produce a racemic mixture?


**BACK**

The first step forms a **trigonal planar** carbocation. The nucleophile can attack from either side of the plane, producing equal amounts of each enantiomer and therefore a **racemic mixture**.


Spec links: `spcpt_dPys4TYPtXSQcCCc`


## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_kmHvcZQCMXf8yTg2`)

**FRONT**

Why does an S<sub>N</sub>2 reaction produce an enantiopure product (if starting from one enantiomer)?


**BACK**

In S<sub>N</sub>2, the nucleophile attacks from the **opposite side** of the leaving group only, causing an **inversion of configuration**. One enantiomer in gives one enantiomer out.


Spec links: `spcpt_dPys4TYPtXSQcCCc`


## Card 19 — keyword_definition (`fl_322jJG8f9hqWD8KR`)

**FRONT**

What is meant by **inversion of configuration**?


**BACK**

**Inversion of configuration** is the complete reversal of the spatial arrangement around a chiral centre during an S<sub>N</sub>2 reaction. It is often compared to an umbrella turning inside out.


Spec links: `spcpt_dPys4TYPtXSQcCCc`

## Card 21 — question_and_answer (`fl_CqvBT2sYj3KHRk4d`)

**FRONT**

What type of carbocation intermediate is formed in the first step of an S<sub>N</sub>1 reaction, and why does this lead to a racemic product?


**BACK**

A **trigonal planar** tertiary carbocation forms. Because it is planar, the nucleophile has equal probability of attacking from either face, giving a **racemic mixture** of products.
