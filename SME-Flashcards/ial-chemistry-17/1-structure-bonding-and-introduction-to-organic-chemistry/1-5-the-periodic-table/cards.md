## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_Q8pjNWrqRNhpcW5D`)

**FRONT**

What is the general trend in first ionisation energy across a period, and why does it occur?


**BACK**

First ionisation energy **increases** across a period. Nuclear charge increases while shielding remains roughly constant and atomic radius decreases, making it progressively harder to remove the outer electron.

## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_mRKS7Zc3w9VCKwbr`)

**FRONT**

Why is the first ionisation energy of boron lower than that of beryllium, despite boron having a higher nuclear charge?


**BACK**

In boron, the outer electron is in the **2p subshell**, which is at a higher energy and further from the nucleus than the 2s subshell of beryllium. This more than offsets the increased nuclear charge, lowering boron's *IE*<sub>1</sub>.

## Card 4 — question_and_answer (`fl_wg53THZkFCySX5KS`)

**FRONT**

Why does the melting point increase from sodium to aluminium across Period 3?


**BACK**

Na, Mg and Al have **metallic bonding** in a giant lattice. Going from Na to Al, each atom donates more electrons to the delocalised sea (1, 2 and 3 respectively), increasing the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the electron sea.

## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_2nrDw8Nn3gTpNgnY`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

First ionisation energy decreases down a group in the Periodic Table.


**BACK**

**True.**

Down a group, atomic radius increases, distance between the nucleus and outer electron increases, and shielding increases. These factors outweigh the increased nuclear charge, making the outer electron easier to remove.

## Card 8 — true_or_false (`fl_Qqvynstnnrh6z37N`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon have low melting points because the covalent bonds within their molecules are weak.


**BACK**

**False.**

The covalent bonds within their molecules are strong. It is the weak **instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces** between molecules that require little energy to overcome, giving low melting points.

## Card 10 — keyword_definition (`fl_3GjSz7S2ytqgvWrb`)

**FRONT**

What is a **giant metallic lattice**?


**BACK**

A **giant metallic lattice** is a regular arrangement of positive metal ions held together by a sea of delocalised electrons. The electrostatic attraction between the ions and the electrons gives metals their high melting points.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_ydTr6KyGz6Y89bbd`)

**FRONT**

Give three reasons why ionisation energy decreases down a group.


**BACK**

1. **Atomic radius increases** as more electron shells are added.
2. **Distance** from the nucleus to the outer electron increases.
3. **Shielding** by inner electron shells increases.

All three reduce the effective nuclear attraction on the outer electron.

## Card 12 — question_and_answer (`fl_wvBcGTZQT5frW4wJ`)

**FRONT**

Why does sulfur have a higher melting point than phosphorus in Period 3?


**BACK**

Sulfur exists as **S**<sub>**8**</sub> molecules, which are larger than the **P**<sub>**4**</sub> molecules of phosphorus. Larger molecules have stronger instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces between them, requiring more energy to overcome.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_XDPr2CYWGvVy8bcM`)

**FRONT**

Why does increasing nuclear charge not always lead to increasing ionisation energy across a period?


**BACK**

Two specific cases cause the general trend to dip: when the outer electron moves to a **higher-energy subshell** (e.g. Be to B, Mg to Al), and when **spin-pair repulsion** in a half-filled orbital makes removal easier (e.g. N to O, P to S).

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_XHCjzRpcmVWs6fhX`)

**FRONT**

Why do the elements from phosphorus to argon have much lower melting points than silicon in Period 3?


**BACK**

Silicon has a giant covalent structure requiring large amounts of energy to break strong covalent bonds. Phosphorus to argon exist as **simple molecules** held together only by weak instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces, which are easily overcome.
