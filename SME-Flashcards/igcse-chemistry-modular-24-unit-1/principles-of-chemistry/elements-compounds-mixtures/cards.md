## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_qWqnHNZWXzjqfvYH`)

**FRONT**

Define **element**.


**BACK**

An **element** is a substance made of atoms that all contain the **same number of protons**, and cannot be broken down into anything simpler.

There are 118 elements in the Periodic Table — for example, copper, iron and magnesium.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_M4qFgYnWFYdWXMxg`)

**FRONT**

How does a compound differ from a mixture?


**BACK**

A compound differs from a mixture in that its elements are **chemically combined**, not just mixed together.

This means a compound cannot be separated into its elements by **physical methods**, whereas a mixture can.

**FRONT**

Define **mixture**.


**BACK**

**FRONT**

Why do impure substances have a lower melting point than the pure substance?


**BACK**

Impure substances have a lower melting point because the added impurities **disrupt the regular structure** of the pure substance.

This means less energy is needed to break the particles apart, so melting begins at a **lower temperature**.

## Card 17 — keyword_definition (`fl_Tv373cprNtYzqX5x`)

**FRONT**

What is **filtration**?


**BACK**

**Filtration** is a separation technique used to separate an undissolved **solid** from a **liquid** or solution.

The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. Small liquid particles pass through as **filtrate**, while large solid particles are left behind as **residue**.

When would you use crystallisation rather than simple distillation?


**BACK**

You would use crystallisation rather than simple distillation when you want to obtain the **dissolved solid** from a solution, not the liquid.

Simple distillation is used when you want to collect the **pure liquid** (for example, water from salt water).

How does fractional distillation separate ethanol from water?

Ethanol **evaporates** first, passes through the condenser and is collected. When the temperature begins to rise to **100 °C**, heating is stopped so the two liquids are now separated.

## Card 26 — question_and_answer (`fl_fP9ZdWbrwqcFzmd8`)

**FRONT**

Why must the pencil line be above the level of the solvent in paper chromatography?


**BACK**

The pencil line must be above the solvent level so that the samples are not **washed away** into the solvent.

If the spots were submerged, the substances would dissolve directly into the solvent rather than travelling up the **chromatography paper** by capillary action.

## Card 27 — keyword_definition (`fl_cKwd5XDtXkQB2bbC`)

**FRONT**

What is an *R*<sub>f</sub> value?


**BACK**

*R*<sub>f</sub> values are used to **identify** the components of mixtures in chromatography.

The *R*<sub>f</sub> value is a **ratio** with no units, calculated by dividing the distance moved by the substance by the distance moved by the **solvent**. It is always less than 1.

You can tell a substance is pure from a chromatogram because it produces only **one spot**.

If more than one spot appears, the substance is a **mixture** containing more than one component.

## Card 31 — question_and_answer (`fl_fFnMWnvZCqsXJmZ8`)

**FRONT**

How do you use a chromatogram to identify the components of an unknown mixture?


**BACK**

You identify the components of an unknown mixture by comparing the **position** of its spots to those of known reference substances on the same chromatogram.

If a spot from the unknown is at the **same height** as a known substance, or has the same *R*<sub>f</sub> value, they are the same substance.

Why does changing the solvent change the *R*<sub>f</sub> value of a substance?


**BACK**

Changing the solvent changes the *R*<sub>f</sub> value because different solvents interact differently with each substance.

**FRONT**

Why is a pencil line used to mark the baseline in paper chromatography, rather than a pen?


**BACK**

A pencil line is used because **pencil (graphite) does not dissolve** in the solvent.

If a pen were used, the **ink** would run up the paper along with the samples and interfere with the chromatogram.

## Card 41 — question_and_answer (`fl_9ddrvtTdRXXCKCDs`)

**FRONT**

How do you calculate the *R*<sub>f</sub> value from measurements taken from a chromatogram?


**BACK**

You calculate the *R*<sub>f</sub> value by dividing the **distance moved by the substance** by the **distance moved by the solvent**.

Both distances are measured in mm from the **baseline** to the centre of the spot (or solvent front). The *R*<sub>f</sub> value has no units.
