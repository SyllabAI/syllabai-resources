## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_Qwdxm4qy3QD5xtQQ`)

**FRONT**

Define **transition element**.


**BACK**

A **transition element** is a d-block element that forms one or more stable ions with an incomplete d subshell.

## Card 2 — true_or_false (`fl_MY2NzY9j3XC25kFJ`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Scandium is classified as a transition element.


**BACK**

**False.** Scandium is not a transition element because its only stable ion, Sc<sup>3+</sup>, has no electrons in the 3d subshell — giving it the electronic configuration of [Ar].


Spec links: `spcpt_H7SJdYbRm6qYMXvq`


## Card 3 — question_and_answer (`fl_qP2q9FJXKxh8Brw5`)

**FRONT**

Why is zinc not classified as a transition element?


**BACK**

Zinc forms only one ion, Zn<sup>2+</sup>, which has a **complete** 3d subshell with the electronic configuration [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>. Transition elements must form at least one ion with an **incomplete** d subshell.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_PzQwVwVq43B7zb7j`)

**FRONT**

Why can transition elements have variable oxidation states?


**BACK**

The 3d and 4s subshells are **similar in energy**, so a similar amount of energy is needed to remove different numbers of electrons. This allows transition elements to form ions by losing varying numbers of electrons.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_bbjZTBxHkS8j3JDV`)

**FRONT**

Why do higher oxidation states become less stable towards the end of the 3d transition series?


**BACK**

As **nuclear charge** increases across the d block, the 3d electrons are attracted more strongly to the nucleus and become **harder to remove**. Higher oxidation states are therefore less stable and elements are more likely to retain their 3d electrons.
