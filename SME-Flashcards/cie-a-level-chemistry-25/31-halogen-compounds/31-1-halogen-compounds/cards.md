## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_qdXKhGZTRZF3YdQN`)

**FRONT**

Define **halogenoarene**.


**BACK**

A **halogenoarene** is an aromatic compound in which one or more halogen atoms are bonded directly to the benzene ring. They are also called aryl halides.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_Z9DCSHg8Z47nZ6Kk`)

**FRONT**

What reagents and conditions are needed to produce chlorobenzene from benzene?


**BACK**

Chlorine gas is bubbled into benzene at room temperature in the presence of anhydrous AlCl<sub>3</sub> catalyst. The AlCl<sub>3</sub> generates the **Cl**<sup>**+**</sup>** electrophile**, which attacks the benzene ring in an electrophilic substitution reaction.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_3Q8zmJrfgwmYyKhm`)

**FRONT**

Why does chlorination of methylbenzene give two products rather than one?


**BACK**

The methyl group is a **2,4-directing** electron-donating group that activates the 2 and 4 positions on the ring. Electrophilic substitution therefore occurs at both positions, giving 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene.

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_jjwNB8gTsDGQ7yj9`)

**FRONT**

What is restored at the end of the electrophilic substitution of benzene with chlorine, and how does this happen?


**BACK**

**Aromaticity is restored**. A hydrogen atom is removed in the second stage of the reaction (heterolytic cleavage of the C–H bond), returning the bonding electrons to the benzene π system and regenerating the delocalised ring.

## Card 10 — keyword_definition (`fl_rjHksvhDPcntXqKH`)

**FRONT**

Define **partial double bond character**.


**BACK**

A **partial double bond character** is a property of a bond that has intermediate character between a single and a double bond, arising from electron delocalisation. In halogenoarenes, the C–X bond has partial double bond character due to lone pair overlap with the benzene π system.


Spec links: `spcpt_8XsxkP29yk9mkKxS`


## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_kz5nXhfn8Vrj53h5`)

**FRONT**

Why are halogenoarenes much less reactive than halogenoalkanes?


**BACK**

In halogenoarenes, a lone pair on the halogen overlaps with the benzene π system, giving the C–X bond **partial double bond character**. This strengthens the bond and makes it very difficult to break, so nucleophilic substitution does not readily occur.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_MnvCRPpYWb9X9vfz`)

**FRONT**

How does chloroethane (a halogenoalkane) undergo nucleophilic substitution, and why is this different for chlorobenzene?


**BACK**

In chloroethane, the carbon bonded to chlorine is slightly positive, so a **nucleophile** such as OH<sup>-</sup> attacks and forms a covalent bond, displacing Cl<sup>-</sup>. In chlorobenzene, **lone pair delocalisation** into the ring strengthens the C–Cl bond so much that a nucleophile cannot break it under normal conditions.

## Card 18 — question_and_answer (`fl_7zKgC3zx79JWCthd`)

**FRONT**

Explain why the unreactivity of halogenoarenes can be attributed to delocalisation.


**BACK**

A lone pair on the halogen delocalises into the benzene π system, providing additional stabilisation of the whole molecule and giving the C–X bond **partial double bond character**. This strengthens the bond, making it harder to break. The result is that halogenoarenes are much less reactive than halogenoalkanes.
