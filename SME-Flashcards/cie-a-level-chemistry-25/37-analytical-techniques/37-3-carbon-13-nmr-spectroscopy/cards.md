## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_n2jc7wTFYm7sx8Wg`)

**FRONT**

Define **chemical shift**.


**BACK**

A **chemical shift** (δ, in ppm) is the position of a peak on an NMR spectrum, measured relative to the TMS reference peak at 0 ppm, indicating the electronic environment of the atom.

## Card 3 — true_or_false (`fl_pWyD2VtRCSSmB8hH`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

In <sup>13</sup>C NMR, the height of each signal is proportional to the number of carbon atoms in that environment.


**BACK**

**False.** In <sup>**13**</sup>**C NMR**, the height of each signal is **not** proportional to the number of carbon atoms. Each non-equivalent carbon produces one signal regardless of how many carbons share that environment.

## Card 5 — question_and_answer (`fl_fGJp7J99CMqR3c2G`)

**FRONT**

How many <sup>13</sup>C NMR signals does propanone produce, and why?


**BACK**

**Propanone** produces **2 signals**: one for the **carbonyl carbon** (200 ppm) and one for the **two equivalent methyl carbons** (30 ppm). The two CH<sub>3</sub> groups are in identical environments due to the molecule's symmetry.

## Card 6 — keyword_definition (`fl_m5BvTxD6nCXMxVB2`)

**FRONT**

Define **carbon-13 NMR spectroscopy**.


**BACK**

**Carbon-13 NMR spectroscopy** is an analytical technique that detects distinct <sup>13</sup>C environments in a molecule to determine molecular structure, producing one signal per type of non-equivalent carbon.


Spec links: `spcpt_3gMJXKdXVpBfkmWS`


## Card 7 — true_or_false (`fl_NqhZ22DmjfsWrPMR`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

<sup>13</sup>C NMR spectra show splitting patterns similar to those seen in <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectra.


**BACK**

**False.** <sup>**13**</sup>**C NMR** spectra show **sharp single signals** with no complicated splitting patterns, unlike <sup>1</sup>H NMR where spin-spin splitting produces multiplets.

**FRONT**

Define **equivalent carbons**.


**BACK**

**Equivalent carbons** are carbon atoms in identical molecular environments due to symmetry, producing a single <sup>13</sup>C NMR signal rather than separate signals for each carbon.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_CvSD4NrgvvW4jCG6`)

**FRONT**

How many <sup>13</sup>C NMR peaks would you predict for 1,3-dihydroxybenzene, and how are they determined?


**BACK**

**1,3-Dihydroxybenzene** gives **4 peaks**. The molecule has a plane of symmetry, making some ring carbons equivalent. Counting unique environments — two types of C–OH, one C between them and one equivalent pair of unsubstituted carbons — gives 4.

**BACK**

A **molecular environment** in <sup>13</sup>C NMR is the unique chemical surroundings of a carbon atom; non-equivalent carbons in different environments appear as separate peaks on the spectrum.

A molecule with five carbon atoms must always show five peaks in its <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum.


**BACK**

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_38FzVHgWNNzwXCDM`)

**FRONT**

Why is counting the number of <sup>13</sup>C NMR resonances a useful first step in analysing an unknown spectrum?


**BACK**

It immediately tells you the number of **distinct carbon environments**, which can distinguish between structural isomers — for example, the three isomers of dihydroxybenzene each have a different number of environments.
