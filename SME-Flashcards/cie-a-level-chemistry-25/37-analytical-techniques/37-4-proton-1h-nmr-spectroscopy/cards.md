## Card 1 — keyword_definition (`fl_wC9qFJbmNf3kqFjf`)

**FRONT**

Define **spin-spin splitting**.


**BACK**

A **spin-spin splitting** is the splitting of an NMR peak into sub-peaks caused by a proton's spin interacting with the spin states of protons on adjacent, non-equivalent carbons.

## Card 2 — question_and_answer (`fl_Mt5HNZzB4nNXpr38`)

**FRONT**

State the n+1 rule for <sup>1</sup>H NMR and explain what n represents.


**BACK**

The **n+1 rule** states that a proton with *n* protons on adjacent carbons splits into *n*+1 sub-peaks. **n** is the number of non-equivalent protons on the directly neighbouring carbon atom(s).

**FRONT**

What information does the area under a peak in a <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum provide?


**BACK**

The **area under a peak** is proportional to the **number of protons** in that particular molecular environment, allowing the ratio of different types of hydrogen to be determined.

**FRONT**

**BACK**

A **molecular environment** in <sup>1</sup>H NMR is the unique chemical surrounding of a proton; chemically equivalent protons share the same environment and produce a single peak at the same chemical shift.

**BACK**

**Tetramethylsilane (TMS)** is Si(CH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>, used as the reference compound in NMR spectroscopy. Its single sharp peak is assigned a chemical shift of 0 ppm.

## Card 11 — question_and_answer (`fl_YDQB64DqCVrswBt2`)

**FRONT**

Why does TMS produce only one sharp peak in an NMR spectrum, despite containing 12 hydrogen atoms?


**BACK**

All 12 hydrogen atoms in **TMS** are in the **same molecular environment** (four identical CH<sub>3</sub> groups bonded to Si), so they all absorb at the same frequency and produce one high-intensity peak.

## Card 14 — question_and_answer (`fl_g4XV6q4v5N67Rb4b`)

**FRONT**

Why is CDCl<sub>3</sub> preferred over an ordinary solvent when dissolving a sample for <sup>1</sup>H NMR?


**BACK**

**CDCl**<sub>**3**</sub> contains **deuterium (**<sup>**2**</sup>**H)** instead of hydrogen (<sup>1</sup>H). Deuterium absorbs radio waves in a different region, so the solvent peak does not interfere with the proton signals from the sample.

## Card 15 — keyword_definition (`fl_y3WqtHPFKMxgTMr4`)

**FRONT**

Define **deuterated solvent**.


**BACK**

A **deuterated solvent** is a solvent in which hydrogen atoms (<sup>1</sup>H) are replaced by deuterium (<sup>2</sup>H), preventing solvent peaks from interfering with the proton NMR signals of the dissolved sample.


Spec links: `spcpt_pYmRWxPrtMDKj5ym`


## Card 16 — true_or_false (`fl_P63T8cRZbbFgJX7h`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

After adding D<sub>2</sub>O to a sample, the –OH signal disappears from the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum.


**BACK**

**True.** Adding **D**<sub>**2**</sub>**O** causes the proton in **–OH** groups to exchange with deuterium, replacing <sup>1</sup>H with <sup>2</sup>H. The signal disappears because <sup>2</sup>H does not absorb in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR region.

## Card 17 — question_and_answer (`fl_9gPRcHFQzVpFtqZ6`)

**FRONT**

Why can –OH and –NH peaks in <sup>1</sup>H NMR be difficult to identify without using D<sub>2</sub>O exchange?


**BACK**

**–OH** and **–NH** peaks are often **broad**, their chemical shift ranges can **overlap** with other proton types, and proton exchange with traces of water means only one average peak appears — making confident assignment difficult.
