## Card 1 — question_and_answer (`fl_8ZqxXGPt9t9tDtfz`)

**FRONT**

What are the two main reactions that the amide group can undergo?


**BACK**

The amide group can undergo:

1. **Hydrolysis** — by refluxing with aqueous acid or aqueous alkali.
2. **Reduction** — using LiAlH<sub>4</sub> in dry ether to form an amine.

## Card 2 — true_or_false (`fl_KymV8M4Rp988RBT6`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Hydrolysis of a non-substituted amide with aqueous acid produces a carboxylic acid and a primary amine.


**BACK**

**False.**

Hydrolysis of a non-substituted amide produces a **carboxylic acid** and **ammonia** (not a primary amine). Hydrolysis of a substituted amide produces a carboxylic acid and a primary amine.

## Card 3 — keyword_definition (`fl_k26rwrjNJKSJPfhX`)

**FRONT**

Define **hydrolysis of an amide**.


**BACK**

A **hydrolysis of an amide** is the breakdown of the amide bond by refluxing with aqueous acid or alkali, producing a carboxylic acid plus either ammonia (non-substituted) or a primary amine (substituted).

## Card 9 — question_and_answer (`fl_3K3ZPKx3WcYWnY8p`)

**FRONT**

How do the products of alkaline hydrolysis of a substituted amide differ from those of acidic hydrolysis?


**BACK**

In **alkaline** hydrolysis, the carboxylic acid product is deprotonated to a **carboxylate ion**, and the **primary amine** is free. In **acidic** hydrolysis, the amine is protonated to form an **alkylammonium salt**, and the carboxylic acid is the free acid.

## Card 10 — question_and_answer (`fl_HwyjQF5gqTgtCtV2`)

**FRONT**

Why are amides much weaker bases than amines?


**BACK**

The **electron-withdrawing oxygen** in the amide group removes electron density from the nitrogen atom. This makes the lone pair on nitrogen **less readily available** for dative covalent bonding, so amides are much weaker bases.

## Card 11 — true_or_false (`fl_cFqWp4WbDwKGxDRP`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Amides are stronger bases than amines because the nitrogen atom in amides still has a lone pair.


**BACK**

**False.**

Although amide nitrogen does have a lone pair, the electron-withdrawing carbonyl oxygen **reduces its availability** for dative bonding. Amides are therefore **much weaker** bases than amines.

## Card 12 — keyword_definition (`fl_DXGHWPwMbXnDGNhV`)

**FRONT**

Define **basicity**.


**BACK**

**Basicity** is the ability of a species to donate its lone pair of electrons to form a dative covalent bond with a proton (H<sup>+</sup>). It depends on how readily available the lone pair is.

**FRONT**

What feature of the amide group reduces the availability of the nitrogen lone pair?


**BACK**

The **carbonyl oxygen** (C=O) in the amide group is electron-withdrawing. It removes electron density from the nitrogen atom, making the lone pair less available for donation to an electron-deficient species.

## Card 15 — true_or_false (`fl_JCMYm2SD4kzR8ZVx`)

**FRONT**

**True or False?**

Propylamine is a stronger base than phenylamine because alkyl groups donate electron density to nitrogen.


**BACK**

**True.**

The propyl group has a positive inductive effect, increasing electron density on nitrogen and making the lone pair more available. In phenylamine, the benzene ring withdraws the lone pair through delocalisation, decreasing basicity.

## Card 16 — question_and_answer (`fl_qvnvg6Ds7jjnkJWP`)

**FRONT**

What do amines and amides have in common in terms of their basic properties?


**BACK**

Both contain a nitrogen atom with a lone pair of electrons. However, the **basicity** of each depends on how available that lone pair is for dative covalent bonding. In amides, the lone pair is much less available due to the electron-withdrawing oxygen.

## Card 18 — keyword_definition (`fl_KxNJnfCNcCWD9x3W`)

**FRONT**

Define **electron-withdrawing group**.


**BACK**

An **electron-withdrawing group** is a substituent that removes electron density from adjacent atoms. In amides, the carbonyl oxygen withdraws electron density from nitrogen, reducing the basicity of the compound.
