### Q1

- The bank statement shows that the bank account has \$2620 - \$2300 = \$320 more than is shown in the cash book
- Consider the four options
- Option A

  - The \$160 unpresented cheques to suppliers will already have been subtracted from the balance in the cash book
  
    - But the bank statement wouldn't 'know about them' yet
  - So the bank statement would 'think' that there was \$160 more in the account than the cash book showed
  
    - \$2300 + \$160 = \$2460
  - That is not equal to \$2620, so answer A is not correct
- Option B

  - The \$160 payment from a customer should have been entered on the debit side of the cash book
  
    - Because it is increasing the bank balance, which is an asset
  - If it was entered on the credit side by mistake, then it would have decreased the bank balance by \$160 instead
  - To correct the error the credit entry would first have to be removed
  
    - This would undo the \$160 reduction and increase the bank balance by \$160
  - Then the correct \$160 debit entry would have to be entered
  
    - This would increase the bank balance by another \$160
  - So in total correcting the error would increase the bank balance by \$160 + \$160 = \$320
  
    - \$2300 + \$320 = \$2620
  - Therefore B is the correct answer
- Option C

  - The \$320 bank charges would already be included in the bank statement
  - Entering them into the cash book would decrease the cash book balance by \$320
  
    - \$2300 - \$320 = \$1980
  - That is not equal to \$2620, so answer C is not correct

  - So the bank statement would 'think' that there was \$320 less in the account than the cash book showed
  
    - \$2300 - \$320 = \$1980
  - That is not equal to \$2620, so answer D is not correct

### Q2
**Final answer:** **The correct answer is A**

- Start with the cash book balance, and use the information to figure out what the bank statement balance would show as being in the account
- The cash book shows a \$780 debit balance

  - Bank is an asset account for the business, so a \$780 debit balance means there is \$780 in the bank
  - (A credit balance in the cash book would mean the account was overdrawn)
- The \$650 'cheque not yet credited' is an uncredited deposit

  - Uncredited deposits are cheques that the trader has paid into his account, but that have not yet appeared on the bank statement
  - The trader has already added them into his bank balance
  - But the bank statement doesn't 'know about them' yet, so take those away from the balance
  
    - \$780 - \$650 = \$130
- The \$55 'cheque not yet presented' is an unpresented cheque

  - Unpresented cheques are cheques the trader has written, but which haven't been deposited yet
  - The trader has already subtracted them from his bank balance
  - But the bank statement doesn't 'know about them' yet, so add those back onto the balance
  
    - \$130 + \$55 = \$185
- So the bank statement 'thinks' that the trader has \$185 in his account

  - Remember that bank statements are written from the point of view of the bank
  
    - So a credit balance means there is money in the bank account
    - (And a debit balance means the account is overdrawn)
  - Therefore the bank statement would show a \$185 credit balance

**B** is incorrect; the amount is right, but it is given as a debit instead of a credit

**C** is incorrect; this is what you would get if you added the uncredited deposit and subtracted the unpresented cheque

**D** is incorrect; the amount is what you would get if you added the uncredited deposits and subtracted the unpresented cheques, but it is given as a debit instead of a credit

    - \$495 - \$112 = \$383
  - That is still a credit balance, which on the bank statement means money in the bank
  
    - So it should be shown as an asset in the statement of financial position
