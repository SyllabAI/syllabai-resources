## Q32 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 3((g)) — 8 marks
Figure 3c shows that the type of plate margin strongly influences the scale and frequency of tectonic hazards, such as earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.

At divergent plate margins, hazards tend to be lower in explosivity but more frequent. Figure 3c shows Iceland is located on a divergent boundary and experiences volcanic eruptions with a VEI of 3, occurring around once every 25 years **[S]**. These eruptions usually produce runny lava rather than explosive ash clouds, meaning they are less dangerous to people, even though they happen relatively often** [Ap]**.

In contrast, convergent plate margins are associated with larger-scale but less frequent hazards. Figure 3c shows that Nepal lies on a convergent boundary between two continental plates and experienced a 7.8 magnitude earthquake, although this only happens about once every 100 years **[S]**. These earthquakes are powerful because pressure builds up as plates lock together before suddenly slipping** [Ap]**. Similarly, the Tonga eruption, also at a convergent margin, had a high VEI of 5, showing that convergent margins can produce very explosive and dangerous volcanic eruptions **[S][Ap]**.

Transform plate margins can also produce high-magnitude earthquakes. Figure 3c shows New Zealand experienced a 7.8 magnitude earthquake, but these occur much more frequently, around once every 3 years, due to plates grinding past each other and repeatedly sticking and slipping** [S]**.

Overall, convergent and transform margins tend to produce the largest and most dangerous hazards, while divergent margins usually create smaller-scale hazards** [Ap]**.
