In developed countries such as Japan, Figure 1c shows that tertiary sector employment was already high at around 60% in 1991 and increased to over 70% by 2019 **[AO4]**. This reflects Japan being in the post-industrial stage of development. As countries become more developed, manufacturing declines and demand for services such as healthcare, education, finance and retail increases, leading to a higher proportion of tertiary employment **[AO3]**.

In developing and emerging countries, tertiary employment has increased more rapidly. For example, Figure 1c shows Cambodia’s tertiary employment rising from about 14% to nearly 40% between 1991 and 2019 **[AO4]**. Some countries show variable change, such as Egypt, which increased its tertiary employment until around 2003, then experienced a small decline before rising again after 2008 **[AO4]**. This may reflect economic instability or slower structural change, meaning growth in services is uneven rather than continuous **[AO3]**.

Overall, level of economic development is the most important factor explaining differences in tertiary employment. Figure 1c shows that highly developed countries consistently have the highest proportions of tertiary employment, while developing countries experience faster growth as they industrialise, confirming the Clark–Fisher model** [****AO3****][****AO4]**.

## Q31 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 1((g)) — 8 marks
Figure 1c shows the Clark–Fisher model, which explains how employment sectors typically change as a country develops over time.

In the pre-industrial stage, Figure 1c shows that the primary sector dominates, accounting for around 70% of employment **[AO4]**. This is because economies at this stage rely heavily on agriculture and extraction for food and income. Limited technology means farming is labour-intensive, so most people work in the primary sector **[AO3]**.

During the industrial stage, Figure 1c shows a sharp decline in primary employment and a rise in secondary and tertiary employment** [AO4]**. Mechanisation of agriculture reduces the need for farm workers, pushing people to move to cities for factory jobs, causing rural–urban migration. As factories grow, services such as transport, retail and banking are also needed, which explains the growth of the tertiary sector alongside the secondary sector **[AO3]**.

In the post-industrial stage, Figure 1c shows that tertiary employment becomes dominant, reaching over 50%, while the secondary sector declines and the quaternary sector begins to grow** [AO4]**. This happens because manufacturing is increasingly automated or moved overseas, while demand grows for services such as education, healthcare, ICT and research. Advances in technology reduce factory labour but create knowledge-based jobs **[AO3]**.

Overall, the most important reason for the changes in employment sectors is economic development. Figure 1c shows that as countries develop, employment shifts from primary to secondary and then towards tertiary and quaternary sectors, confirming the Clark–Fisher model** [AO3][AO4].**
