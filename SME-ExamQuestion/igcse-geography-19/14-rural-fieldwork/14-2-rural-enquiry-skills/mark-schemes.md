One advantage of a pie chart is that it clearly shows the proportion of sites with high, medium, or low landscape change scores relative to the total. [**1]**

This makes it easy to see at a glance that most sites in Ashdale show a moderate to high level of change, which supports the overall conclusion of the enquiry. [**1]**

> - **One mark** for development of that disadvantage

> **[4 marks]**

> **Alternative content**
> 
> The answer above is just one example of how to respond to this question. Alternative information that could be provided in the answer includes:

> 
> - Shows proportions and relative sizes clearly
> - Visually simple and easy to interpret quickly
> - Effective for summarising a small number of categories

## Q12 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 8 marks
The primary data I collected had both strengths and limitations when it came to reliability. **[Ap]**

I used a land-use survey at multiple sites, recording the dominant land use at each location on a structured recording sheet. **[K]**

This method was relatively reliable because it produced consistent, comparable data across all sites using the same criteria each time. **[Ap]**

However, some land uses were difficult to classify clearly, particularly where more than one type of farming was visible at the same site, which introduced an element of subjectivity into the results. **[U]** To improve reliability, the group could have used a more detailed classification system with clearer categories agreed in advance. **[Ap]**

I also completed a landscape quality survey at each site, which was a qualitative primary method. **[K]** This was less reliable than the land use survey because scores depended on individual judgement rather than measurable criteria. **[Ap]** Different members of the group may have scored the same site differently, reducing consistency across the dataset. **[U]**

My secondary data came from historic maps of the study area, which I used to compare past and present land use. **[K]** This source was reliable because it was produced by an official body and provided an objective record of land use at a specific point in time. **[U]** However, the maps were not always up-to-date for the most recent changes, which limited their usefulness for drawing conclusions about very recent farming changes. **[Ap]**

Overall, the secondary data was the more reliable of the two sources because it was produced systematically and was free from the observer bias that affected my primary surveys. **[Ap]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> The command word '**evaluate**' means the answer must make judgements about the reliability of the data. It is not enough to describe what was collected. A strong answer will weigh up strengths and limitations across both primary and secondary data and reach a supported conclusion.
> 
> **Mark allocation**
> 
> This answer is assessed using a '**level of response**' approach. Each point made does not equal one mark.
> 
> A top-level 3 answer **[7 to 8 marks]** will:
> 
> - **AO1 ****[K]** demonstrate accurate knowledge of primary and secondary data collection methods used in a rural environments enquiry
> - **AO2 ****[U]** show clear understanding of what makes data reliable or unreliable, including subjectivity, consistency, source credibility, and currency
> - **AO3 ****[Ap]** apply evaluative judgement to a specific enquiry context, reaching a clear and supported conclusion about overall reliability
> 
> **No credit for:**
> 
> - Simply listing data collection methods without any evaluation of their reliability
> - A conclusion that only repeats earlier points without making a judgement
> - Responses that do not refer to both primary and secondary data as the question requires
> 
> **Alternative content**
> 
> The answer above is just one example of how to respond to this question and will depend on the fieldwork. Alternative information that could be provided in the answer includes:
> 
> - Questionnaires with farmers or local residents as primary data
> - Annotated field sketches or photographs as qualitative primary data
> - Environmental quality surveys of the rural landscape
> - DEFRA agricultural census data as secondary data
> - Aerial photographs or satellite images from different time periods
> - Local planning authority records of land use change
> - Interviews with local stakeholders
> - Local council planning records as secondary data
> - News articles or energy company data as secondary sources
> 
> **Reliability:**
> 
> - Sample size and representativeness
> - Subjectivity of qualitative scoring methods
> - Currency and relevance of secondary sources
> - Weather or seasonal conditions affecting primary data collection
> - Difficulty of accessing remote rural sites consistently
> 
> **Common errors**
> 
> - Describing the enquiry process rather than evaluating the reliability of the data
> - Focusing entirely on primary data and neglecting secondary data (or vice versa)
> - Using evaluative language in the introduction but drifting into description by the end
> - Writing a conclusion that does not make a clear comparative judgement between primary and secondary data
