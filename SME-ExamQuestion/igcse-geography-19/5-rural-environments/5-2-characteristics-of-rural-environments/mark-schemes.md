## Q28 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 8 marks
Figure 1 shows changes in the urban and rural population of the UK between 1950 and 2050. Rural areas in developed countries are changing due to a combination of human and economic factors, including counter-urbanisation, agricultural change and government policy.

One major factor leading to change in rural areas is counter-urbanisation. Figure 1 shows that while the urban population has grown, the rural population has remained relatively stable or shown slight increases in recent decades **[AO4]**. People move from cities to rural areas seeking a better quality of life, lower house prices and improved transport links. This leads to rural areas gaining population, often wealthier commuters, which can increase house prices and change the character of villages. **[AO3]**

Another factor is changes in agricultural employment. Figure 1 shows that despite population changes, rural areas have not experienced a significant population decline **[AO4]**. As farming becomes more mechanised and efficient, fewer workers are needed in agriculture. This has led to diversification in rural areas, with farmers developing tourism, farm shops or renewable energy projects to supplement income. **[AO3]**

Government policy also affects rural areas. Figure 1 shows overall population growth concentrated in urban areas **[AO4]**. Policies such as green belt restrictions limit urban sprawl but can increase pressure on existing rural settlements. Investment in rural broadband and infrastructure can attract new businesses and remote workers to rural areas. **[AO3]**

Overall, counter-urbanisation is arguably the most significant factor changing rural areas in developed countries like the UK, as it brings new residents, changes local services and alters the social composition of rural communities.
