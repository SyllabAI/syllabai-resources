Figure 2c shows four types of ecosystem goods and services: provisioning, regulating, supporting and cultural. Exploiting natural ecosystems can increase the supply of some goods in the short term, but it can also damage services, reducing the long-term benefits ecosystems provide.

One way exploitation affects ecosystems is through increasing provisioning goods such as food and fuel. Figure 2c shows provisioning services including food, biomass fuel and freshwater **[AO4]**. For example, forests may be cleared for farming to increase food production. While this can increase food supply and support development, it can also reduce other ecosystem functions, which means it is not sustainable in the long term. **[AO3]**

Exploitation can also reduce regulating services. Figure 2c shows regulating services such as climate regulation, erosion control and water run-off **[AO4].** When ecosystems like forests are exploited through logging, carbon storage decreases and soil erosion increases. This can lead to flooding and climate impacts, reducing the ecosystem’s ability to protect people and the environment.** [AO3]**

Supporting can also be affected. Figure 2c shows supporting services like nutrient cycling and soil formation **[AO4]**. Intensive farming or mining can damage soils and habitats, reducing biodiversity. This shows how exploiting ecosystems can disrupt linked systems. **[AO3]**

Overall, exploiting natural ecosystems can increase some goods temporarily, but it often damages services, meaning long-term environmental and human benefits are reduced.

### 2((i)) — 8 marks
Figure 2c shows the global distribution of the world’s biomes. Biomes are large-scale ecosystems such as deserts, tropical rainforests and tundra. Their distribution is mainly controlled by climate, especially temperature and rainfall, although other factors also influence the pattern.

The most important reason for the distribution of biomes is latitude and climate. Figure 2c shows tropical rainforests found near the Equator, where temperatures are high and rainfall is heavy all year **[AO4].** In contrast, tundra is found in high-latitude areas such as northern Canada and Russia, where temperatures are very low. This shows that temperature and precipitation strongly control which biome can exist in each region. **[AO3]**

Rainfall patterns also help explain biome distribution. Figure 2c shows deserts in areas such as North Africa and central Australia **[AO4]**. These regions receive very little rainfall, making it difficult for vegetation to grow. Areas with seasonal rainfall often have grassland or savanna biomes, while places with year-round rainfall support forests. This shows how different climate conditions create different biomes. **[AO3]**

Local factors such as altitude can disrupt the global pattern. Figure 2c shows mountain ranges like the Andes, Himalayas and Rockies **[AO4]**. At high altitudes, temperatures are lower, so biomes such as taiga and tundra can be found even near the Equator. Human activity can also change vegetation locally, although this is not shown clearly on the map.** [AO3]**

Overall, climate linked to latitude is the main reason for the distribution of the world’s biomes, but altitude and local factors can also affect the pattern.

## Q18 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 2((h)) — 8 marks
Figure 2c shows the range of goods and services provided by natural ecosystems. Ecosystem goods are physical products such as food and water, while services are benefits that support human wellbeing. Natural ecosystems are important because both goods and services work together to support human life and economic activity.

One important ecosystem good is food and drink. Figure 2c shows that ecosystems provide food and drink as a key good **[AO4]**. Ecosystems such as forests, oceans and grasslands supply crops, fish and meat. Without healthy ecosystems, food production would decrease, showing how important these goods are for human survival. **[AO3]**

Water supply is another important ecosystem good. Figure 2c shows water supply as a key good provided by ecosystems **[AO4]**. Rivers, lakes and wetlands store and filter water naturally, making it suitable for drinking and farming. This also links to services such as water and nutrient cycling, which help maintain water quality.** [AO3]**

Although goods are important, ecosystems also provide many services that support the supply of goods. Figure 2c shows services such as carbon storage, flood management and habitats for species **[AO4]**. These services protect ecosystems and allow goods like food and materials to be produced. For example, forests store carbon and reduce flooding, helping to protect farmland.** [AO3]**

Overall, although goods are important, services are necessary to maintain ecosystems. The value of services is not as easy to calculate, but without them, the goods would not be available.
