One advantage of using a bar graph is that it clearly shows the difference in solar panel numbers between sites at a glance. **[1] **

For example, the contrast between Site 4 (24 panels) and Site 5 (1 panel) would be immediately visible, making it easy to identify spatial patterns across Thornfield. **[1]**

However, one disadvantage is that a bar graph only shows the data at one point in time. **[1] **

> The command word '**explain**' means each advantage and disadvantage must be developed with a reason, not simply stated.

> 
> - **One mark** for a valid advantage of a bar graph
> - **One mark** for development of that advantage with reference to Figure 1
> - **One mark** for a valid disadvantage of a bar graph
> - **One mark** for development of that disadvantage
> 
> **[4 marks]**
> 
> **Alternative content**
> 
> The answer above is just one example of how to respond to this question. Alternative information that could be provided in the answer includes:
> 
> **Advantages:**
> 
> - Suitable for discrete data such as panel counts
> - Can accommodate all five sites clearly on one axis
> 
> **Disadvantages**:
> 
> - Does not show the relationship between two variables (for example, panels and EQS score)
> - Does not show proportions or percentages
> - A line graph or scatter graph might be more appropriate for showing trends or correlations

> - Stating an advantage or disadvantage without any development

## Q13 — hard — 8 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 8 marks
An investigation into types of energy use by people in Solus Street, Brightland, UK.

My primary data collection had both strengths and limitations in terms of reliability. **[K]** I used a structured questionnaire with closed questions to gather data from members of the public about their energy use. **[K]** This method produced quantitative data that was straightforward to compare and present as graphs. **[K]** However, the sample size was relatively small, which means the results may not represent the views of the whole community. **[U]** A larger sample would have improved reliability by reducing the risk of bias in the responses. **[U]**

The environmental quality survey (EQS) I completed at each site was also a primary method, but its reliability was limited by subjectivity. **[K]** Different students may score the same location differently depending on personal judgement, which reduces the consistency of the data.** [U]** To improve this, the group could have completed the EQS independently at each site and then compared scores, using an agreed scoring framework to reduce variation. **[Ap]**

My secondary data came from a government energy report on regional renewable energy trends. **[K]** This source was reliable because it was produced by an official body using large datasets. **[U]** However, the data was collected at a regional level, which made it difficult to apply directly to my local study area. **[Ap]**

Overall, my primary data was the less reliable of the two sources because of the small sample size and subjective scoring, though both sources together gave a more complete picture of energy use than either could alone. **[Ap]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> The command word '**evaluate**' means the answer must make judgements about the reliability of the data, not simply describe what was collected. A strong answer will weigh up strengths and limitations and reach a supported conclusion.
> 
> **Mark allocation**
> 
> This answer is assessed using a '**level of response**' approach. Each point made does not equal one mark.
> 
> A top-level 3 answer **[7 to 8 marks]** will:
> 
> - **AO1 ****[K]** demonstrate accurate knowledge of primary and secondary data collection methods used in an economic activity and energy enquiry
> - **AO2 ****[U]** show clear understanding of what makes data reliable or unreliable, including sample size, subjectivity, source credibility, and relevance
> - **AO3 ****[Ap]** apply evaluative judgement to their specific enquiry context, reaching a clear and supported conclusion about overall reliability
> 
> **No credit for:**
> 
> - Simply listing data collection methods without any evaluation of their reliability
> - A conclusion that only repeats earlier points without making a judgement
> - Responses that do not refer to both primary and secondary data as the question requires
> 
> **Alternative content**
> 
> The answer above is just one example of how to respond to this question and will depend on the fieldwork. Alternative information that could be provided in the answer includes:
> 
> - Visual surveys of energy infrastructure (wind turbines, solar panels, pylons)
> - Annotated photographs as qualitative primary data
> - Interviews with local stakeholders
> - Local council planning records as secondary data
> - News articles or energy company data as secondary sources
> 
> **Reliability:**
> 
> - Sample size and representativeness
> - Subjectivity of qualitative methods
> - Currency and relevance of secondary sources
> - Weather or time of day affecting primary data collection
> - Observer bias in visual surveys
> 
> **Common errors**
> 
> - Describing the enquiry process rather than evaluating the reliability of the data
> - Focusing entirely on primary data and neglecting secondary data (or vice versa)
> - Using evaluative language in the introduction but drifting into description by the end
> - Writing a conclusion that does not make a clear judgement about overall reliability
