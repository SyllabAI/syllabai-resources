## Q1 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
| ### Model answer<br><br>In A View from the Bridge, Arthur Miller explores the theme of helping others as a central force driving character relationships and tensions. Throughout the play, many characters show willingness to support others, but Miller also demonstrates how this desire can be limited, misguided or tragically unsuccessful. Through language, structure and dramatic form, the play presents both genuine and failed attempts to help others, revealing complex human motivations and the inevitability of tragedy.<br><br>Beatrice and Eddie’s guardianship of Catherine is one of the earliest examples of help in the play. Beatrice has raised Catherine since her mother’s death and Eddie reminds Catherine of his promise: “I promised your mother on her deathbed. I’m responsible for you”. His protective language shows Eddie’s sense of duty; however, his help is not purely selfless. His insistence that Catherine is “a baby” reflects a patronising view, suggesting his protection is quite possessive and controlling, rather than being genuinely helpful.<br><br>Beatrice, by contrast, offers more constructive support. When Catherine is offered a job, Beatrice encourages her: “Be the way you are, Katie, don’t listen to him”. Her advice empowers Catherine to become independent, highlighting how Beatrice tries to ease tensions by helping her get Eddie’s approval. Throughout the play, Beatrice serves as a peacemaker, helping others emotionally, especially in the buildup to the wedding, when she tries to reason with Eddie: “When am I gonna be a wife again, Eddie?” Even in her frustration, she attempts to reconnect with him and stop the escalating conflict.<br><br>Beatrice’s act of sheltering her cousins, Marco and Rodolpho, is clearly helpful and rooted in family loyalty. Eddie also initially justifies the decision morally, saying, “suppose… I had people in America could keep me a couple of months?” Here, Miller uses rhetorical questioning to show Eddie’s conflicted values. He sympathises with their situation but cannot handle Rodolpho’s closeness to Catherine and Eddie’s “help” soon transforms into hostility.<br><br>Marco is presented as a truly selfless character, whose main goal is to support his family: “I want to send right away maybe twenty dollars.” His economic sacrifice and strong sense of duty contrast with Eddie’s increasingly possessive behaviour. Set in a 1950s Italian-American working-class community in Red Hook, Brooklyn, the play explores how immigration, masculinity, and family honour shape each character’s actions. Eddie’s betrayal of Marco and Rodolpho to the immigration authorities — a desperate and distorted attempt to protect Catherine — reflects these cultural tensions and shows how societal expectations can twist even well-meaning intentions. When Marco lifts the chair “like a weapon” over Eddie’s head, it becomes a powerful, symbolic act of defending his brother without resorting to physical violence. Miller uses this gesture structurally to mark a turning point as Marco’s warning is an attempt to prevent further harm, even as tensions spiral.<br><br>Miller, the son of immigrants himself, explores the pressures placed on men like Eddie to provide and protect, which often conflict with legal boundaries and moral duties. Alfieri’s role as narrator and lawyer embodies the limits of helping. From the start, Alfieri admits he “watched it run its bloody course”. Despite trying to guide Eddie, “You won’t have a friend in the world, Eddie”, he recognises that the law and rational advice cannot always prevent destruction. Alfieri uses the image of “a river will drown you if you buck it now” to warn Eddie about the dangerous path he is on — a force that now seems unstoppable. This sense of inevitability is also embedded in the play’s structure. Eddie’s downfall is foreshadowed early, and each character’s attempt to help becomes increasingly futile. Even at the end, when Eddie lies dying, “the two women support him… calling his name again and again”. Their physical act of helping cannot save him, showing the tragic limitations of human compassion. Beatrice remains by his side, reinforcing her loyalty, but her kindness cannot undo his actions.<br><br>Miller presents helping others as a double-edged theme. While characters like Beatrice and Marco show genuine care, others like Eddie and, to a degree, Alfieri, struggle to help without causing harm. |

Miller presents Eddie Carbone as a tragic hero whose love for his niece curdles into an obsession he cannot admit, destroying him and those around him.

Miller frames Eddie’s story through the lawyer-narrator Alfieri, who introduces him as an ordinary longshoreman before warning that the story will run its “bloody course”. This structural device gives the play the shape of a Greek tragedy: the audience knows from the start that Eddie is doomed, so we watch not what will happen but how and why. Alfieri’s later image of “a passion that had moved into his body, like a stranger” presents Eddie’s downfall as something beyond his control, inviting our sympathy even as we judge him.

At first Eddie seems a caring guardian to his niece Catherine, but Miller’s dialogue slowly reveals something darker. His objections to her growing up — her high heels, her new job, the way she walks — are obsessive in their detail, and his constant focus on Catherine betrays a possessiveness he refuses to name. Miller makes Eddie’s inability to say what he feels the heart of the tragedy: the more fiercely he denies his desire, the more violently it leaks out in his behaviour towards Rodolpho.

That jealousy drives the central conflict. Eddie tries to undermine Rodolpho by mocking his blond hair, his singing and his dressmaking, hinting that he “ain’t right” — that he is not a real man. Miller uses these attacks to show Eddie projecting his own anxieties: unable to compete with Rodolpho for Catherine, he attacks his masculinity instead. The shocking moment when Eddie kisses both Catherine and Rodolpho is an action rather than a speech, and Miller lets Eddie’s body reveal the desire and aggression his words keep hidden. Because this happens in front of the audience as stage action, it carries a force no dialogue could, exposing in seconds the truth Eddie has spent the whole play denying.

Eddie’s flaw leads him to break the community’s deepest code by reporting the cousins to the Immigration Bureau. Miller foreshadows this through the earlier story of Vinny Bolzano, the boy who informed on his own family and was spat on in the street. By having Eddie tell this cautionary tale himself, Miller makes his later betrayal bitterly ironic: Eddie becomes the very figure he despises. The act shows how completely his obsession has overridden his judgement and his loyalty.

Miller also uses Alfieri to define exactly what is wrong in Eddie. Alfieri admits that, like most people, he has learned to “settle for half”, and he repeatedly begs Eddie to accept that Catherine must be let go. Eddie’s tragedy is that he cannot settle for half: he wants all of Catherine and will not compromise. Miller uses Alfieri’s reasonable, measured voice as a contrast to Eddie’s rising desperation, so that we see how far Eddie has slipped beyond ordinary good sense. This structural pairing of the calm narrator and the man who will not listen makes Eddie’s destruction feel both avoidable and inevitable, which is the essence of tragedy.

At the end, Eddie cares only about his “name”, demanding that Marco publicly take back his accusation. Beatrice finally says the truth he cannot face — “you want somethin’ else, Eddie, and you can never have her!” — but he would rather die than admit it. He is killed with his own knife, a fitting image of a man destroyed by his own desires. Alfieri’s closing verdict, that Eddie “allowed himself to be wholly known”, suggests a strange dignity in the total, destructive honesty of his feeling, even as Alfieri mourns the waste of it.

Miller presents Eddie as both guilty and pitiable: a man undone by a love he cannot understand or control. Through Alfieri’s framing, the slow exposure of Eddie’s obsession and the tragic structure of the play, Miller turns one man’s private shame into a powerful drama about desire, denial and the cost of refusing to know yourself.
