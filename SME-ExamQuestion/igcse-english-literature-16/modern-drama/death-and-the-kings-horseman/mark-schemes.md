# Mark Schemes — Death and the King's Horseman
**Modern Drama** · Edexcel IGCSE English Literature (4ET1)


## Q1 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Soyinka presents Iyaloja as the guardian of Yoruba tradition: a wise and powerful woman who is determined that the sacred ritual is carried out properly, even when it costs her dearly.

Iyaloja is presented as a figure of great authority. As "mother" of the market, she commands respect from the women and speaks for the community's values. Soyinka gives her dignified, proverb-rich language that sets her apart from the lighter talk around her and marks her out as wise and serious. Through her control of the market women, who together drive out the colonial messenger Amusa — scorning him as an "eater of left-overs" — Soyinka shows Iyaloja upholding the community's order and refusing to let outsiders interfere with what is sacred. From her first appearance she is the steady centre of the community's moral world.

Iyaloja wants, above all, for Elesin to complete his sacred duty. She understands that the King's Horseman must die to accompany the dead King and keep the world in balance, and she is determined that nothing should prevent this. Soyinka presents her as the voice of tradition, constantly reminding Elesin of the weight of what he must do. Her seriousness contrasts sharply with Elesin's pleasure-seeking, and this contrast is one of Soyinka's main methods: it shows that Iyaloja grasps the cost of the ritual far more clearly than the horseman himself.

Iyaloja reveals how far she will go to do the right thing when she grants Elesin's request to take a young bride on his final night — even though the girl is betrothed to Iyaloja's own son. Soyinka uses this painful decision to show Iyaloja sacrificing her family's happiness for the sake of the ritual and Elesin's peace of mind. Her willingness to give up something so personal proves how completely she places duty and tradition above her own feelings, and it deepens our sense of her as a woman of principle. That she makes the choice without bitterness, treating the girl's future child as the community's gain rather than her family's loss, shows how completely she thinks in terms of the whole people rather than herself.

Iyaloja is also presented as clear-eyed and warning. She senses the danger in Elesin's hunger for life and his marriage, and she cautions him not to let earthly pleasures weaken his resolve. Soyinka gives her a solemn, almost prophetic tone, so that when Elesin later fails, her fears are shown to have been right all along. This dramatic irony — the audience remembering her warnings as disaster unfolds — makes Iyaloja seem wiser than every other character, including the horseman she serves.

When Elesin fails to die and is arrested, Iyaloja turns on him with fierce contempt. "You have betrayed us," she tells him, accusing him of choosing to "eat the world's left-overs" rather than the "sweetmeats" the community had prepared for his honourable death. Soyinka gives her bitter food imagery that throws Elesin's hunger for life back at him, turning the appetite he indulged throughout the play into the proof of his shame. For her, his failure is a betrayal of the living, the dead and the unborn, and her harsh judgement confirms that she has always wanted the right thing done and cannot forgive its undoing.

Even amid this ruin, Soyinka shows Iyaloja thinking of the community rather than herself. Her command "Now forget the dead, forget even the living. Turn your mind only to the unborn" redirects everyone's grief towards the future, and the steady imperative verbs give her the calm authority Elesin has lost. As she arranges what must follow, she is the leader still holding the world together after the horseman has let it fall, so that by the end it is she, not Elesin, who carries the dignity of tradition.

Through her authority, her painful sacrifices and her fierce condemnation of failure, Soyinka presents Iyaloja as the determined guardian of tradition. She is the character who most clearly wants the right thing done, and who suffers most deeply when it is not.

## Q2 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

The death ritual is the centre of the entire play: Soyinka makes Elesin's required death the act on which the balance of the Yoruba world depends, and its failure becomes the source of the tragedy.

The ritual is significant first as a sacred duty. As the King's Horseman, Elesin must die in order to follow his dead master and keep the cosmos in order. Soyinka presents this not as a simple act of suicide but as a profound matter of faith, using the Praise-Singer's chants and the steady drumming to surround it with ceremony and weight. From the opening scene, the whole community's attention is fixed on this single sacred event, so that the ritual shapes the form and atmosphere of the play from the start.

The ritual's significance is shown through Elesin's apparent willingness. He boasts that he does not fear death, telling the famous story of the "Not-I bird" from which everyone else flees, and declaring "My rein is loosened" as he prepares to go. Soyinka uses Elesin's vivid, confident language to show that the horseman is meant to embrace the ritual gladly. This makes the ritual feel noble rather than grim — and makes Elesin's later failure all the more shocking.

Soyinka also makes the ritual significant through the proverb-rich language that surrounds it. Elesin sums up its whole meaning in the short creed "Life is honour. / It ends when honour ends", and the women answer "We know you for a man of honour". The plain, balanced phrasing of this creed presents the ritual not as death for its own sake but as the highest form of honour, so that to refuse it is to lose everything that gives life value. Soyinka uses this weight of proverb and shared response to show that the ritual is woven into the community's very language and beliefs.

The ritual is also significant as a piece of theatre, not just an idea. Soyinka fills the early scenes with drumming, dance and song, so that the audience experiences the approaching death as a living ceremony rather than a discussion. The drums in particular act almost as a second voice, building a trance-like rhythm that is meant to carry Elesin gently towards his death. By making the ritual so physical on stage, Soyinka shows how completely it belongs to the whole community, drawing the audience into the same atmosphere of solemn celebration that the Yoruba characters feel.

The ritual becomes the play's central conflict when the colonial officer Pilkings tries to stop it. To the British it looks like barbaric self-destruction, but Soyinka presents their interference as a dangerous failure to understand the Yoruba world. The play warns that to halt the ritual is to "push our world from its course and sever the cord that links us to the great origin", an image that shows the ceremony holding the whole community's universe together. The clash between these two views of the ritual drives the drama.

The ritual's importance is proved by the disaster its failure causes. Distracted by his marriage and weakened by his love of life, Elesin hesitates, and Pilkings arrests him before he can complete the duty. Soyinka structures the play so that this single failure throws everything into crisis: the dead King is left unaccompanied and the community is shamed. The unfinished ritual becomes a wound in the world that the rest of the play must somehow answer.

Finally, the ritual's significance is shown when Elesin's son, Olunde, dies in his father's place. Having studied in England, Olunde nonetheless understands the duty his father abandoned, and his death restores the ritual at terrible cost. Soyinka uses this reversal — the Western-educated son completing the sacrifice the traditional father failed to perform — to show how vital the ritual is, and how its meaning passes down to the next generation rather than dying out.

The death ritual is significant, then, as the spiritual heart of the play. Soyinka makes it the duty that holds the Yoruba world together, the source of the central conflict, and the measure by which every character is finally judged.

## Q3 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### Model answer

Although the play ends in death and failure, Soyinka threads hope through it, locating that hope not in the living but in duty fulfilled and in the future carried by the unborn.

At first, hope lies in the proper completion of the ritual. The community believes that if Elesin dies as he should, the world will stay in balance and life will continue safely. Soyinka surrounds the early scenes with celebration, song and dance, creating a hopeful, festive mood built on faith that tradition will hold. This opening hope depends entirely on Elesin doing his duty, and Soyinka's bright, ceremonial staging makes the audience share the community's confidence.

This early hope rests on a belief that the world has always held together. The Praise-Singer reminds Elesin that "Our world was never wrenched from its true course", and that in the ancestors' time it was never "tilted from its groove". Soyinka uses these proverb-like images of a world kept steady to suggest that the community's hope is grounded in an unbroken line of duty stretching back through the generations. The hope is not blind optimism but trust in a tradition that has never yet failed — which makes the threat to it feel all the more serious.

Iyaloja's instruction to "forget the dead, forget even the living" and "turn your mind only to the unborn" places hope firmly in the future. Soyinka uses her words to show that, in the Yoruba worldview, the living, the dead and the unborn are bound together, and that hope lies in protecting the generations still to come. Even as death approaches, Iyaloja points beyond it, and Soyinka gives her the play's calm, proverb-like wisdom so that her vision of hope feels deep and lasting rather than naive.

Elesin's failure seems to destroy this hope. When he hesitates and is arrested, the ritual is broken and the community is left shamed and endangered. Soyinka structures the play so that the bright hope of the opening collapses into darkness, suggesting that hope built on one weak man is fragile. The contrast between the festive beginning and the grim later scenes is one of Soyinka's strongest structural methods, and it makes the loss of hope feel devastating. By placing the brightest hope at the very start, Soyinka ensures that its collapse falls hardest, so that the structure itself teaches the audience how fragile a hope resting on one person can be.

Hope returns, painfully, through Olunde. By dying in his father's place, Olunde proves that the community's values survive even in a young man educated abroad. Soyinka uses Olunde's sacrifice to suggest that hope lies in the next generation's willingness to honour duty, even when their elders fail. His death is tragic, but it restores the broken order, showing that hope can outlive even terrible failure.

Olunde even gives this hope words before he acts on it. Talking to Jane Pilkings, he calls a willing death "an affirmative commentary on life", arguing that a sacrifice freely made does not deny life but gives it meaning. Soyinka uses this calm, reasoned line to show that, for Olunde, hope and death are not opposites: the right death can be a way of affirming the community's future. His own sacrifice then proves the point, so that the most hopeful idea in the play is spoken by the character who is about to die for it.

The hope Soyinka finally offers is hard-won and bitter. Elesin dies at last, shamed, while the community's attention turns, as Iyaloja urges, towards the unborn — the children who must carry the world forward. Soyinka refuses any easy comfort, but he leaves the audience with the sense that the community's deepest values, though wounded, will endure. Hope here survives not in any one person but in tradition passed on to those not yet born.

Soyinka presents hope as something rooted not in the survival of individuals but in duty done and in the unborn future. Through Iyaloja's words and Olunde's sacrifice, the play suggests that even amid death and failure, the community's values can be carried forward.

Soyinka presents the Praise-Singer as Elesin's devoted companion and the voice of Yoruba tradition, using him to celebrate, guide and finally mourn the King's Horseman.

The Praise-Singer is presented as Elesin's constant companion. From the opening he chants "Elesin O! Elesin Oba!", praising and following the horseman through the market. Soyinka uses him almost like a chorus in classical drama, weaving music and proverb around Elesin's words, so that the audience hears the community's voice praising its hero. His presence makes Elesin seem larger than life and surrounds him with ceremony, establishing the play's ritual atmosphere from the very first lines.

The Praise-Singer is significant for the rich, poetic language Soyinka gives him. He speaks in vivid images, promising that Elesin's name will be "like the sweet berry a child places under his tongue", and reminding him that in the ancestors' time the world was never "tilted from its groove". Soyinka uses this proverb-laden style to carry the wisdom and beauty of Yoruba oral tradition, making the Praise-Singer the play's most poetic voice and a living link to the past.

The Praise-Singer also acts as a guide and gentle conscience. He warns Elesin about the dangers of his appetite for life and women, hinting that pleasure may weaken his resolve. Soyinka uses him to voice the community's expectations, reminding Elesin of his duty even as he flatters him. In this way the Praise-Singer is both admirer and warner, holding Elesin to the high standard that tradition demands and quietly preparing the audience for the danger ahead.

Soyinka also uses the Praise-Singer to create dramatic irony. Early on he asks Elesin, "Are you sure there will be one like me on the other side?", a question meant only to praise the bond between them. By the end, after Elesin has failed, the question takes on a darker meaning, since there will now be no companion to follow at all. Soyinka plants this line so that the audience, looking back, feels the gap between the Praise-Singer's confident hope and the failure to come, deepening the sense of tragedy.

Soyinka also uses the Praise-Singer to give the play its distinctive sound. His chants work as call and response, with the community echoing his words, so that his voice never feels like a single speaker but like the voice of a whole people. Soyinka uses this shared music to bind the Praise-Singer to the community he speaks for, making him sound less like a character and more like tradition itself given a voice.

As the ritual approaches, the Praise-Singer becomes the voice meant to carry Elesin into death. He even imagines following the horseman, hoping for someone like himself on "the other side". Soyinka uses the Praise-Singer's chanting, together with the drumming, to create the trance-like atmosphere of the ritual, making him essential to the ceremony's spiritual power. His voice is the thread that should lead Elesin across into the world of the ancestors.

When Elesin fails, the Praise-Singer's tone turns from praise to grief and reproach. The companion who once sang Elesin's glory now mourns the broken ritual and the shame it brings. Soyinka uses this shift in the Praise-Singer's voice to measure the scale of Elesin's failure: even his most loyal admirer is filled with sorrow. Through the Praise-Singer, the audience feels the depth of the community's disappointment.

This change is sealed in the Praise-Singer's bitter farewell. He tells Elesin, "I call you by that name only this last time", reminding him that he once had "a tongue that darted like a drummer's stick". Soyinka contrasts this memory of Elesin's old eloquence with his present silence and shame, so that the Praise-Singer's grief becomes a kind of verdict. The voice that existed to praise him can now only say goodbye, which shows just how completely Elesin has fallen from the honour the Praise-Singer once celebrated.

Soyinka presents the Praise-Singer as Elesin's poetic shadow and the voice of tradition — celebrating, guiding and finally grieving for him. Through him, the play carries the beauty of Yoruba oral culture and measures the tragedy of the horseman's fall.

Soyinka presents respect as central to the play, contrasting the deep respect the Yoruba community holds for tradition and the dead with Pilkings's narrow respect for nothing but British law and order.

The Yoruba community is presented as bound together by respect for sacred duty. They honour the dead King, the ritual and the roles each person must play, and they treat Elesin's coming death as a matter of the highest importance. Soyinka uses the ceremony, song and proverb of the opening to show a society in which respect for tradition shapes everything. When the women declare that "tonight our husband and father will prove himself greater than the laws of strangers", Soyinka shows how completely the community respects its own values over colonial authority.

This respect is bound up with the idea of honour. Elesin states the community's creed plainly — "Life is honour. / It ends when honour ends" — and the women answer "We know you for a man of honour". Soyinka uses this exchange to show that respect in the Yoruba world is earned by living up to duty, not by holding power. The short, balanced phrasing of the creed makes respect feel like a shared law that everyone accepts, so that to lose honour is to lose the community's respect entirely — which is exactly what happens to Elesin.

Against this, Soyinka presents Pilkings as a man who respects only British power. He cannot see the ritual as anything but barbaric, and he moves to stop it without ever trying to understand its meaning. Most strikingly, Pilkings and his wife wear a confiscated ancestral egungun costume to a colonial ball, treating a sacred object as mere fancy dress. Soyinka uses this casual act of desecration as a powerful visual symbol of Pilkings's total lack of respect for the Yoruba world.

Respect is closely tied to power in the play. The colonial officials expect respect simply because they hold authority, while dismissing the community's own forms of respect as superstition. Soyinka exposes the cruelty and absurdity of this through the messenger Amusa, who is caught between two worlds and mocked by the market women as an "eater of white left-overs". This sharp, comic insult shows how the community withholds respect from those who serve the colonial power, even as that power demands respect it has not earned.

Olunde, Elesin's son, embodies a deeper respect that crosses cultures. Educated in England, he nonetheless respects the duty his father must perform, and he is appalled by the British failure to understand it. Soyinka uses Olunde's calm, reasoned speech to show that real respect means understanding the values of others — something Pilkings never manages. The contrast between Olunde's dignity and the officials' ignorance makes Soyinka's point about respect unmistakable.

Soyinka sharpens this through Olunde's defence of self-sacrifice. Olunde reminds Jane Pilkings that the British honour their own soldiers who die for others, calling such a death "an affirmative commentary on life". Soyinka uses this to expose how selective British respect really is: they admire sacrifice in their own people but condemn it in the Yoruba. By having Olunde turn their values back on them, Soyinka argues that true respect means judging another culture by the standards you accept for yourself, which Pilkings and his world consistently refuse to do.

The play finally turns on respect lost. Elesin forfeits the community's respect when he fails his duty, and his shame is unbearable precisely because respect matters so much in this world. Soyinka structures the ending so that Olunde's sacrifice restores some of the honour his father lost, showing that respect, once broken, can only be repaired at great cost. The whole tragedy grows from how much respect the failure destroys.

Through the community's profound respect for tradition, Pilkings's narrow respect for British law and Olunde's wider understanding, Soyinka makes respect the value that divides the two worlds of the play. The tragedy grows from the colonial failure to respect a world it does not even try to understand.

## Q6 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### Model answer

Olunde is the play's most interesting character because he overturns every expectation: the Western-educated son who, far from rejecting tradition, understands and finally embraces the duty his father abandons.

Olunde is interesting first because of how Soyinka uses him to surprise the audience. Having gone to England to study medicine, he might be expected to scorn the ritual as primitive. Instead he returns home calm and resolute, prepared for his father's death. Soyinka uses Olunde to challenge the colonial assumption — shared by characters like Pilkings — that a Western education must make a person abandon their own culture. From his first entrance, Olunde quietly defies what both the British and the audience might expect of him.

Olunde is presented as unusually clear-sighted. In his conversation with Jane Pilkings, he calmly defends the ritual and points out the hypocrisy of a Britain that honours soldiers who die for their country while condemning the Yoruba sacrifice. Soyinka gives Olunde measured, intelligent speech that makes him the character who sees both worlds most clearly. This composure makes him far more thoughtful and dignified than the colonial officials who casually dismiss his culture.

Soyinka deepens this clear sight through Olunde's defence of self-sacrifice. Recalling a British captain who blew himself up to save his men, Olunde calls the act "an affirmative commentary on life", arguing that a willing death can give life meaning rather than deny it. Soyinka uses this to show Olunde turning the colonists' own values back on them: the very sacrifice the British admire in their own people is what they condemn in his. There is a quiet dramatic irony here, since the audience knows Olunde will soon prove his argument with his own life.

The same irony runs through Olunde's calm acceptance of his father's death. He tells Jane simply, "I came home to bury my father", expecting Elesin to die that night as duty demands. Soyinka loads this quiet line with dramatic irony, because Olunde will indeed bury a horseman — but it will be himself, dying in his father's place, while Elesin lives on in shame. By making Olunde so composed about a death he believes is certain, Soyinka shows how completely he has accepted the values his father will betray, and prepares the audience for the terrible reversal to come.

What makes Olunde compelling is his loyalty to a duty that is not even his to perform. When his father fails, Olunde steps into the role himself, choosing to die so that the ritual is completed and the community's world restored. Soyinka presents this as a profound act of love and duty, made more striking because Olunde, as a modern young man, has every reason to refuse. His decision is the moral high point of the play, and it gives the tragedy its terrible weight.

Olunde is also memorable for his devastating rejection of his father. Looking on the shamed, still-living Elesin, he declares "I have no father, eater of left-overs", before walking calmly away to his own death.
