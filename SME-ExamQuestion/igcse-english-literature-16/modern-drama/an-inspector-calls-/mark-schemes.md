Initially depicted as immature and irresponsible, he becomes more morally conscious and responsible.<br><br>As a character, Priestley first presents Eric as a reckless and self-centred young man, illustrating his lack of awareness and irresponsible attitude towards himself and others. Eric remains on the fringes of the action in the first two acts and this enables the other characters in the play to dominate proceedings, which further highlights his immaturity. Indeed, Eric attempts to leave when he suspects the Inspector may want to question him and thus initially avoids taking any responsibility for his actions.<br><br>As the play progresses, Eric’s attitude begins to change as he becomes increasingly aware of his responsibility to others. His guilt for his actions towards Eva in Act 3, when he confesses his affair and admits Eva was pregnant with his child, shows some attempt at responsibility when he suggests marriage to Eva. However, as the father of her child, he ultimately fails in his duty to protect both of them. Like Gerald, Eric abuses his power and status as a middle-class man with Eva and discards her when it suits him. He also hints at the potential for sexual violence and shows that Eva resisted him, his behaviour became threatening: “I was in that state when a chap easily turns nasty”. He fails to acknowledge the fundamental social and economic vulnerability of women like Eva, who could be easily exploited by wealthy men like Eric. While Gerald hints that he may have been in love with Eva, Eric admits he was not, and so his behaviour could also be viewed as more malicious.<br><br>By the end of the play, Priestley presents Eric as a significantly altered character, one who is ashamed and regretful over his conduct with Eva. During his confession, he uses emotive language and repetition, “you killed them both — damn you, damn you”, to reveal his anger and condemnation which is juxtaposed with his parents’ dismissive reactions, and he heavily criticises his mother’s treatment of Eva. While he could appear to express a stronger sense of guilt than the others (through his emotional outburst), his emotional outburst may reflect immaturity rather than deep remorse, especially when compared to his sister’s response. Eric and Sheila do not share the same sense of relief as the others when the Inspector is discovered to be a fraud, as this is irrelevant to them. Through Sheila and Eric, Priestley offers the audience a more optimistic view of the future, that the younger generation is capable of change as he depicts them as more compassionate and empathetic, incapable of avoiding their social responsibilities. In doing so, Priestley suggests the younger generation will avoid the errors of the previous generation, suggesting that the selfish and exploitative capitalist system will be replaced with a more socialist agenda. In part, Eric therefore presents a symbol of hope for the changing attitudes of the younger generation.<br><br>Initially presented as an immature character, Priestley uses the character of Eric to demonstrate the possibility of personal growth and social responsibility. Priestley uses Eric’s personal development to encourage others to change their attitudes, and to look after one another, in order to strive for a society where all members are treated more equally and fairly. |

## Q7 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Priestley uses An Inspector Calls to argue that everyone is responsible for one another, dramatising this idea through the Inspector’s investigation, which forces each character — and the audience — to confront the consequences of their actions.

Priestley opens by establishing the opposite of responsibility. Mr Birling lectures his family that “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own”, dismissing the idea that everybody should look after everybody else as “community and all that nonsense”. Priestley uses dramatic irony here — Birling’s confident predictions that the Titanic is “unsinkable” and that war is impossible are things the audience knows are wrong — so we distrust his selfish philosophy from the start. By making the spokesman for irresponsibility a fool, Priestley discredits his whole worldview before the Inspector has even arrived.

The Inspector is Priestley’s main tool for exploring responsibility. Structurally he controls the play, questioning the family one at a time and forcing each to admit their part in Eva Smith’s death. His role is closer to that of a moral judge than a policeman, and his name, Goole — a homophone of “ghoul” — hints that he may be something more than human. Priestley uses this single, relentless figure to build the argument that every individual action has a consequence for someone else.

Priestley presents responsibility as a chain. Each character adds a link to Eva’s downfall: Birling sacking her, Sheila having her dismissed out of jealousy, Gerald’s affair, Mrs Birling refusing her charity and Eric’s exploitation. Through this structure Priestley shows that responsibility is shared — no single person killed Eva, but together they destroyed her. Sheila grasps this when she insists that “these girls aren’t cheap labour — they’re people”, a line whose dash forces a pause that gives the word “people” real weight. Priestley uses her dawning understanding to model the change he wants the audience to undergo.

Priestley dramatises the theme further through the contrast between the generations. The younger characters, Sheila and Eric, accept their guilt and are changed by it, while the older Birlings and Gerald try to escape it, seizing on the idea that the Inspector was a hoax. Priestley uses this divide to suggest that hope lies with the young, who are still open enough to learn. The parents’ relief that they might avoid a scandal exposes that they have understood nothing about responsibility — only about protecting their reputation.

The theme reaches its climax in the Inspector’s final speech, where he warns that we are “members of one body” and “responsible for each other”. His prophecy that those who refuse this lesson will be taught it in “fire and blood and anguish” lifts a domestic drama into a warning for society as a whole. Priestley then uses the play’s cyclical structure — the final phone call announcing that a real inspector is on his way — to give the characters, and the audience, a second chance to take responsibility. The ending deliberately leaves us asking whether we would change.

Through the Inspector’s investigation, the chain of guilt and the play’s circular structure, Priestley turns responsibility from an abstract idea into a gripping moral test. He argues that no one can live alone, and that ignoring our responsibility for others leads only to suffering — a lesson he is determined his audience will not forget.
