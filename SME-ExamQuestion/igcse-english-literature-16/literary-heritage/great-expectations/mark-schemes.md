# Mark Schemes — Great Expectations
**Literary Heritage** · Edexcel IGCSE English Literature (4ET1)


## Q1 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Dickens presents ambition as a powerful but dangerous force in Great Expectations, showing how Pip's longing to rise in class makes him ashamed of those who love him before he learns that true worth has nothing to do with status.

Pip's ambition is sparked at Satis House. When Estella sneers at his "coarse hands" and "thick boots", Pip is suddenly ashamed of himself, and Dickens uses her cruel words to plant the desire to become a gentleman. The first-person retrospective narration, told by an older Pip looking back, lets Dickens show both the boy's painful longing and the adult's regret at once. In a rigidly class-divided Victorian society, where a blacksmith's apprentice could almost never rise, Estella makes Pip feel that being "coarse and common" is something shameful he must escape.

Dickens shows that ambition makes Pip ashamed of his own family. Once he dreams of being a gentleman, Pip becomes embarrassed by Joe, the kind blacksmith who raised him, and by the forge that was once his home; he admits that his old life now seems "coarse and common". Dickens uses the symbol of the forge, once a place of warmth and honest work, to measure how far ambition has corrupted Pip's values. Through this he criticises a society that judged people by manners and money rather than goodness, showing how ambition can poison love and loyalty.

Dickens also uses Joe as a quiet contrast that exposes the emptiness of Pip's ambition. Even as Pip climbs socially, he privately calls Joe "a dear good fellow", admitting his guardian's real worth at the very moment he is ashamed of him. Dickens uses this contrast between Joe's contentment at the forge and Pip's restless craving for status to suggest that ambition is not the only path to a good life. In a Victorian culture that prized self-improvement and "getting on", Joe embodies an older, humbler ideal of honest labour, and Dickens makes the reader feel that Pip's ambition costs him more than it gains.

The cruellest twist is that Pip's gentlemanly ambition rests on a lie. He assumes his fortune comes from Miss Havisham and is meant to win Estella, but his secret benefactor is really the convict Magwitch, the "fearful man" he once fed on the marshes. Dickens uses this structural irony to attack Victorian snobbery: the "low" criminal is more generous than the respectable rich, and the gentleman Pip is built entirely on convict money. Pip's horror when he learns the truth exposes how shallow and snobbish his ambition has become.

Dickens shows that ambition brings Pip misery, not happiness. In London he falls into debt, grows vain, and neglects Joe, yet feels increasingly empty. Much of his ambition is driven by his hopeless love for Estella, the "poor dreams" of winning her that Dickens later shows to be founded on illusion. The contrast between the warm forge and the cold, fashionable city suggests that climbing the social ladder has cost Pip everything that truly mattered. Through this, Dickens questions the Victorian dream of self-improvement, suggesting that ambition without love and integrity leads only to dissatisfaction and shame.

By the end, Pip's ambition is humbled and reshaped. After Magwitch's death and the loss of his fortune, Pip learns to value Joe's goodness and to work honestly for his living. Dickens uses Pip's three-stage journey — from forge to false gentleman to humbled man — to redefine what it means to be a true gentleman: not wealth or class, but kindness and loyalty. The mature Pip who narrates the novel judges his younger self's ambition with shame, guiding the reader towards Dickens's moral.

Through Pip's rise and fall, Dickens presents ambition as a force that can blind us to real worth. By making his hero rise and then fall, Dickens warns his Victorian readers that the dream of "bettering" oneself can quietly destroy the values that matter most. In a class-obsessed Victorian world, Pip's longing to become a gentleman brings only shame and emptiness, until he learns that true value lies in love and integrity, not status.

## Q2 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Dickens presents guilt as a force that haunts Pip from childhood, using it to explore conscience, class and the moral growth that turns a frightened boy into a better man.

Pip's guilt begins when he steals food and a file for the convict Magwitch. Dickens fills the opening chapters with images of guilt: Pip feels watched by the cattle and the marshes, and imagines the man "with the iron on his leg" pursuing him in the dark. The first-person narration makes the reader share the terrified boy's overwhelming sense of having done wrong, even though he acts out of fear and pity. In a strict Victorian world that punished even small thefts harshly, Pip's guilt feels enormous and inescapable, and Dickens uses the gloomy marsh setting to mirror his troubled conscience.

Dickens makes the boy's guilt feel physical and inescapable through the imagery of the home itself. As Pip dreads the theft he must commit, he sees his crime "rose before me in the avenging coals", as if the fire of Joe's own forge were accusing him. Dickens uses this image of the "avenging coals" to show guilt turning everyday things into reminders of wrongdoing, while the phrase "guilty knowledge" names the secret burden the boy carries alone. Because the harsh Victorian law could transport or hang a thief, even a child's small theft becomes a source of terror, and Dickens makes the reader feel how a frightened conscience magnifies guilt out of all proportion.

Dickens links guilt closely to class and shame. Pip describes the "guiltily coarse and common thing it was, to be on secret terms of conspiracy with convicts", showing how he feels tainted by his connection to crime. As his ambition grows, this guilt mixes with snobbery: he is ashamed both of having helped a criminal and of his own humble background. Dickens uses this to expose how Victorian society made people feel guilty about poverty itself, as though being "common" were a kind of crime.

Pip's deepest guilt is towards Joe, the loving blacksmith he abandons. Once Pip becomes a gentleman, he neglects and is ashamed of Joe, and the older narrator looks back on this with painful regret. Joe's unfailing affection, calling Pip "dear old chap" even after being slighted, only deepens that guilt, since Pip knows he has wronged a man who never stops loving him. Dickens uses the retrospective narration so that the adult Pip judges his younger self harshly, turning the whole novel into a kind of confession. This guilt is moral rather than legal, and Dickens presents it as far more serious than the childhood theft.

Pip's guilt is finally transformed through Magwitch. When Pip learns that the convict is his secret benefactor, he is at first horrified, but as he cares for the dying man he feels guilty for his earlier disgust and learns to love him. Dickens uses this change to show guilt becoming a force for good: it teaches Pip humility and compassion. The "fearful man" he once dreaded becomes the person whose love finally redeems him.

By the end, guilt has reshaped Pip into a better person. His shame at how he treated Joe and Magwitch leads him to value loyalty and kindness over class and wealth. Dickens structures the novel so that Pip must lose his fortune and confront his guilt before he can grow, suggesting that a guilty conscience, properly faced, is the path to becoming a true gentleman. The mature narrator's honesty about his own faults models this moral lesson for the reader.

Through Pip's haunted childhood, his shame over class and his betrayal of Joe, Dickens presents guilt as a powerful moral force. By writing the whole story as the older Pip's confession, Dickens makes guilt the engine of the narrative itself, so that the reader is invited to share in Pip's self-judgement and learn the same lesson. Far from being only painful, guilt becomes the means by which Pip learns humility, compassion and the difference between social status and real worth.

## Q3 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### Model answer

Dickens presents Magwitch as a figure who seems terrifying but proves deeply loyal and generous, using him to overturn Victorian assumptions about criminals and to question what truly makes a gentleman.

Magwitch first appears as a frightening figure on the marshes. Dickens describes "a fearful man, all in coarse grey, with a great iron on his leg", and gives him the violent command "Hold your noise!", threatening to cut Pip's throat. The harsh, physical language and the bleak graveyard setting make Magwitch seem monstrous and dangerous. Dickens plays on Victorian fears of convicts as brutal outcasts, so that the reader, like Pip, at first judges Magwitch entirely by his rough appearance.

Yet Dickens quickly hints at Magwitch's humanity. Even as he demands food, the convict is starving, hunted and pitiable, and he is touched by the boy's kindness. When a second convict appears, "dressed in coarse grey, too", Dickens uses the contrast between the two men to suggest Magwitch is not simply evil but someone shaped by a hard, unjust life. This early flicker of sympathy prepares the reader for the later revelation that Magwitch is far better than he first seems.

Magwitch's importance becomes clear when he is revealed as Pip's secret benefactor. The convict Pip once feared has secretly worked and saved abroad to make him a gentleman, out of gratitude for that childhood kindness. Dickens uses this twist as the structural turning point of the novel and as a powerful attack on snobbery: the "low" criminal is more generous and loving than the respectable Miss Havisham. Pip's snobbish horror at the truth exposes his own shallowness rather than any fault in Magwitch.

Dickens also presents Magwitch as a man whose deepest pride lies in what he has made of Pip. When he reveals the truth he says, "I've made a gentleman on you", and the rough, ungrammatical speech makes his triumph feel both moving and pitiable. Dickens shows that Magwitch has poured his whole life into raising up the boy who once helped him, treating Pip's rise as his own revenge on a society that cast him out. The same Pip who as a child thought only of "My convict" now owes his entire status to that man, and Dickens uses this bond to argue that loyalty and gratitude, not birth, are the real marks of worth.

Dickens presents Magwitch as fiercely loyal and capable of deep love. He risks execution by returning illegally to England simply to see the gentleman he has made, and he treats Pip almost as a son. As Pip slowly overcomes his disgust and comes to care for the dying convict, Dickens shows Magwitch's rough exterior giving way to tenderness. Tending the wounded man at the end, Pip admits "my repugnance to him had all melted away", and now "I only saw a man" who had loved him faithfully for years. Dickens uses this change in Pip's own feelings to measure Magwitch's true worth: once the snobbery is stripped away, the convict is revealed as gentle and devoted. His loyalty quietly shames the cold, self-interested behaviour of the novel's more "respectable" characters.

Through Magwitch's death, Dickens completes his criticism of Victorian society. The convict dies still loving Pip, and Pip's loyalty to him at the end marks how far Pip has grown morally. Dickens uses Magwitch to argue that goodness is not decided by class or by the law: a transported criminal can be more honourable than a wealthy lady. In a society that harshly punished and exiled the poor, Magwitch stands as Dickens's challenge to readers to look beyond appearances.

Dickens presents Magwitch as far more than the frightening convict of the opening. By making the most feared figure in the novel into its most loving, Dickens forces his Victorian readers to question their easy assumptions about who is "respectable" and who is not. Through his loyalty, generosity and love, Magwitch overturns prejudices about criminals and becomes the means by which Pip learns that true worth has nothing to do with class.

## Q4 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Miss Havisham is significant as a powerful symbol of the destructive effects of bitterness and revenge, and as the figure whose influence shapes both Pip's false ambition and Estella's coldness.

Miss Havisham is significant first as a striking symbol of a life frozen by heartbreak. Jilted on her wedding day, she has stopped all the clocks "at twenty minutes to nine", still wears her decaying wedding dress, and lives shut away from daylight in the gloom of Satis House. Dickens uses this powerful imagery — the stopped clocks, the rotting bridecake, the locked-out sunlight — to present her as a woman trapped in a single moment of pain. The precise detail "twenty minutes to nine" makes the reader feel that her whole life has frozen at the exact second her heart broke, so that time itself seems to have died with her hopes. She becomes a symbol of how clinging to the past can rot a person from within, and Dickens uses her decay to suggest that refusing to move on is itself a kind of living death.

Miss Havisham is significant because she turns her own pain into revenge. She has adopted Estella and raised her to "break their hearts", whispering "Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!" Dickens presents her as deliberately twisting a child into a weapon against all men. The fierce repetition of "break their hearts" makes her sound almost like a chant or a curse, showing how completely revenge has taken over her mind. This makes her both villain and victim: she causes terrible harm, yet her cruelty grows from her own deep wound, which gives her real tragic depth.

Miss Havisham is significant for the part she plays in Pip's false ambition. By inviting him to Satis House and allowing him to believe she is his secret benefactor, she encourages his dream of becoming a gentleman worthy of Estella. Dickens uses her to mislead both Pip and the reader, since the real benefactor is the convict Magwitch. Her cold game with Pip's hopes shows how the wealthy in the novel toy with those beneath them, treating people as playthings for their own purposes.

Dickens gives Miss Havisham a powerful moment of change near the end. Seeing the misery she has caused Pip and Estella, she is overwhelmed by guilt and begs Pip's forgiveness, crying out "What have I done!" over and over. Dickens uses this desperate repetition to show her finally grasping the full weight of her revenge, realising too late how far it has gone. Through her remorse he delivers a clear moral: revenge destroys not only its targets but the person who seeks it. Her dramatic death, when her old wedding dress catches fire, works almost as a symbolic punishment for a life wasted on bitterness, as though the past she refused to leave behind at last consumes her.

Miss Havisham's significance reaches across the whole novel. Her coldness helps create Estella's inability to love, which in turn causes Pip lasting pain; she has trained Estella so thoroughly that the girl cannot return the feeling behind Pip's "poor dreams" of winning her. Her decaying house stands throughout as a warning against living in the past, and Dickens uses the parallel with Magwitch to sharpen her significance: where the convict's benefaction comes from love, hers comes from spite. Through her, Dickens explores how heartbreak, if nursed into revenge, spreads its damage from one person to the next. She is one of the novel's clearest examples of a life ruined by refusing to let go.

Miss Havisham is significant, then, as both a haunting symbol and a driving force in the plot. By making her at once frightening and pitiable, Dickens ensures she lingers in the reader's mind as a warning rather than a simple villain. Through her frozen world, her cruel revenge and her final remorse, Dickens warns that bitterness destroys the bitter as surely as their victims, leaving a trail of broken hearts behind.
