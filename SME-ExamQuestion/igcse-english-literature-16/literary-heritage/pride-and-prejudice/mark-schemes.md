| ### Model answer<br><br>In Pride and Prejudice, Jane Austen presents two contrasting views on marriage through the characters of Elizabeth Bennet and Charlotte Lucas. Elizabeth is shocked when Charlotte accepts Mr Collins and she believes her friend is sacrificing her happiness for security. While Elizabeth’s view is rooted in ideals of love and compatibility, Charlotte’s decision reflects the societal expectations and economic pressures of women in the early 19th century. Austen presents both perspectives with sympathy and while Elizabeth’s ideals are admirable, Charlotte’s pragmatism is understandable and in many ways is justified.<br><br>Elizabeth’s reaction to Charlotte’s engagement to Mr Collins is one of disbelief and disappointment. Elizabeth cannot understand how Charlotte could agree to marry “a conceited, pompous, narrow-minded, silly man” simply to secure her future. Elizabeth values emotional connection and mutual respect in a marriage and this informs her rejection of Mr Collins herself. The contrast between the two women is evident. While Elizabeth seeks love and refuses to marry without it, Charlotte takes a more practical approach, stating plainly that she is “not romantic”. From a modern perspective, it may seem that Elizabeth is right to criticise Charlotte’s choice. Mr Collins is clearly absurd and embarrassing, both in his flattery of Lady Catherine and his social awkwardness. He had, after all, proposed to Elizabeth and even considered Jane first, showing a total lack of romantic feeling. Charlotte acknowledges that he is “neither sensible nor agreeable”, but she still accepts him, suggesting that happiness in marriage is “entirely a matter of chance”. Through Charlotte’s words, Austen highlights the lack of agency many women had during this period.<br><br>However, Charlotte is 27 and has limited prospects. At a time when marriage was often a woman’s only option for financial security, Charlotte’s decision is not without merit. She makes a calculated, rational choice based on her circumstances and Austen never condemns her for this. In fact, she allows Charlotte to succeed in making the best of her situation. When Elizabeth visits Charlotte and Mr Collins, Charlotte has cleverly arranged her life to avoid much of her husband’s company, placing her sitting room at the back of the house. This shows that she has maintained some independence within the constraints of her marriage. Austen herself never married, though her novels are preoccupied with love and marriage and she understood the limited choices available to women. While the novel promotes the idea that love should be at the heart of marriage, it also recognises that this is not always possible. Charlotte’s honesty, “it is better to know as little as possible of the defects of the person with whom you are to pass your life”, shows her acceptance of reality, not weakness.<br><br>Austen uses Charlotte’s marriage to contrast with the eventual union of Elizabeth and Darcy, which is based on love, respect and mutual growth. Structurally, this contrast strengthens the novel’s overall message: that while love should be the foundation of marriage, society often forces women to compromise. Charlotte’s marriage functions as a foil to Elizabeth’s, showing how women with different fortunes must make different choices. Indeed, Charlotte’s brothers are relieved by her marriage, since it removes the financial burden of supporting her. Mr Collins believes the marriage is successful and claims, “My dear Charlotte and I have but one mind”. This is both comical and informative as Austen uses irony here, showing Mr Collins’ inability to see the truth and Charlotte’s achievement in making the best of the marriage.<br><br>In conclusion, while Elizabeth’s belief that Charlotte should not have married Mr Collins reflects her own values and emotional courage, Charlotte’s decision is practical, rational and in many ways admirable. Austen presents both perspectives with balance, showing the societal constraints that force Charlotte’s hand, while still allowing the reader to admire Elizabeth’s integrity. I therefore partially agree with Elizabeth, but I also understand and respect Charlotte’s choice. |

## Q7 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Austen presents Mr Darcy as a proud, reserved gentleman whose apparent arrogance gradually gives way to reveal a generous and principled man, so that his transformation drives the novel’s exploration of pride, prejudice and class.

Austen first presents Darcy as arrogant and cold. At the Meryton ball he refuses to dance and dismisses Elizabeth as merely “tolerable; but not handsome enough” to tempt him. The dismissive word “tolerable” instantly makes him unlikeable and fuels Elizabeth’s prejudice against him. Because the novel is told largely from Elizabeth’s point of view, the reader shares her low opinion, and Austen uses this limited perspective to make Darcy’s later transformation more surprising. His wealth of “ten thousand a year” and his high rank help explain, but do not excuse, his pride.

Darcy’s pride is really the pride of his class. As a wealthy landowner, he has been raised to look down on those of lower rank, like the Bennets with their embarrassing relations and modest fortune. Writing in a rigidly hierarchical Regency society, Austen uses Darcy to satirise the snobbery of the upper class. Elizabeth condemns “his abominable pride”, and even his first marriage proposal insults her by dwelling on her family’s inferiority. Austen presents his pride as a genuine flaw, but one shaped by his upbringing rather than pure cruelty.

Darcy’s first proposal is a turning point in how Austen presents him. He begins “In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed”, language that reveals a genuine, overpowering love — yet he ruins it by detailing how hard he has fought against her low connections. Austen uses this contradiction to show a man torn between real feeling and class prejudice. His blunt honesty, also seen when he admits that “my good opinion once lost is lost for ever”, shows a proud, unbending nature, but also an integrity that refuses to flatter.

Austen presents Darcy’s change through the letter and the visit to Pemberley. His letter, explaining his actions over Wickham and Jane, forces Elizabeth — and the reader — to reconsider him. At Pemberley, the housekeeper’s praise of him as a generous master and landlord reveals a private kindness that the proud public man hides. Austen uses the contrast between Darcy’s cold manner and his good deeds to suggest that his pride masks a fundamentally good character, and his quiet rescue of Lydia, done without seeking thanks, confirms his moral growth.

Because this praise comes from a servant who has known him since childhood, and has nothing to gain by flattering him, Austen makes it far more convincing than anything Darcy says about himself. In Regency society a great landowner held real power over the lives of his tenants and servants, so a reputation for being a good master signals genuine moral worth, not just good manners. Austen uses this outside testimony to overturn Elizabeth’s first impressions and to show the reader the responsible, generous man hidden beneath Darcy’s cold pride.

Their union, crossing the boundaries of class, lets Austen suggest that personal worth should matter more than rank.

Through Elizabeth’s biased viewpoint, the drama of the two proposals and the slow revelation of his true character, Austen presents Darcy as far more than a proud aristocrat. He becomes the novel’s proof that first impressions can be wrong, and that pride, once recognised and overcome, can give way to love, generosity and respect.
