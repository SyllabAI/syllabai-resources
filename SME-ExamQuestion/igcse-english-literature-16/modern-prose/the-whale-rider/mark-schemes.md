## Q1 — very_hard — 40 marks · exam-questions

### 8() — 40 marks
| ### Model answer<br><br>In The Whale Rider, Ihimaera uses journeys — both physical and spiritual — to explore themes of growth, identity, cultural conflict, and the deep interconnection between humans and nature. Travel is not simply geographical; it represents transformation, returning to roots, and the reawakening of traditions.<br><br>One of the most powerful portrayals of travel is through Rawiri’s physical journey overseas. Rawiri leaves Whangara and travels to Australia and Papua New Guinea. This journey exposes him to new environments but also deepens his understanding of racism and inequality. For example, while staying at Jeff’s family’s plantation, he witnesses the mistreatment of native workers, particularly the scene where a labourer is left bleeding by the roadside and ignored by his employers. This experience shocks Rawiri into recognising the deep injustices faced by indigenous peoples globally. It is significant because it prompts his spiritual return home, showing that travel can clarify where one truly belongs. His exposure to a world of inequality strengthens his connection to his Māori heritage and compels him to be part of preserving it.<br><br>Kahu’s journey, though mostly confined to Whangara, is equally transformative. From birth, she travels between her mother’s family and Whangara every summer, finally settling in the village at age six. Her physical movement mirrors her growing importance as the prophesied leader. The most symbolic act of travel is when she rides the bull whale out to sea. This spiritual journey is life-threatening but deeply significant, connecting her to her ancestor Kahutia This travel is both literal and symbolic — it is a rite of passage that confirms her destiny and reunites the old traditions with the present generation. Her miraculous return from the ocean reinforces her role as saviour of her people and their culture.<br><br>The whales' migration serves as a powerful metaphor for ancestral memory and the cyclical journey of life. Led by the ancient bull whale, the pod travels from the Antarctic, recalling the original voyage made with Kahutia Te Rangi. The route they take, especially their turn towards New Zealand, is loaded with symbolism. The old bull whale’s longing for his master causes him to lead the herd towards dangerous waters, despite the protests of the females. This reveals how memory and emotion can override logic and lead to peril. The whales’ journey links the present with the mythic past, demonstrating how the legacy of Paikea continues to shape the present. The journey also reflects ecological dangers; Ihimaera references nuclear testing near Moruroa as a reason why the whales divert their route, subtly showing how human interference disrupts natural migratory paths.<br><br>Travelling also highlights generational and cultural divides. Koro Apirana’s reluctance to travel, both physically and ideologically, symbolises his resistance to change. In contrast, Porourangi, who has travelled abroad and experienced other approaches to Māori education, returns with a broader perspective. He wants to prepare his people for “new challenges and the new technology”, while Koro clings to traditional, male-led leadership. Their differing views reflect the central tension of the novel: how to preserve culture while adapting to modernity. Travel, in this context, becomes a metaphor for openness to progress.<br><br>The structure of the novel itself echoes the idea of travel and cyclical return. Divided into four sections (Spring, Summer, Autumn, and Winter) this structure reflects the natural passage of time and life. Each season signifies a phase in the narrative and in Kahu’s development, reinforcing the idea that all journeys, even dangerous or painful ones, are part of a larger, natural cycle.<br><br>In conclusion, travel in The Whale Rider is deeply significant. Whether through Rawiri’s international experiences, Kahu’s mythic journey on the back of a whale, or the pod’s ancestral migration, Ihimaera uses travel to explore identity, heritage, and transformation. It is not just about movement, but about rediscovery — of self, culture, and the sacred ties between land, sea, and people. |

Ihimaera presents Kahu as a gentle but determined girl whose love and natural connection to her people’s traditions make her the rightful leader of her tribe, even though her great-grandfather rejects her for being a girl.

From birth Kahu is defined by love, especially for her great-grandfather Koro Apirana. Even though he coldly rejects her, she adores him, affectionately calling him “Paka” and weeping “Love Paka” when she fears she has upset him. Ihimaera presents this unconditional love as a strength rather than a weakness. Because the story is narrated by her uncle Rawiri, the reader sees Kahu’s warmth clearly while Koro remains blind to it, creating a dramatic irony in which we recognise her worth long before he does.

Kahu’s central struggle is that she is born a girl into a culture that expects its leader to be male. She is named after Kahutia Te Rangi, the ancestor who rode a whale to found the tribe, but Koro feels that naming “a girlchild” after him is “belittling” the great ancestor. He blames her “for being a girl and the eldest grandchild”, refusing to teach her the sacred knowledge he saves for boys. Ihimaera uses Koro to represent rigid tradition and male authority, and Kahu’s quiet endurance of his rejection makes her both sympathetic and admirable.

Ironically, Kahu has the very gift Koro is searching for. She is drawn instinctively to the sea and seems able to sense and communicate with the whales themselves. When Koro drops a carved stone into the sea and challenges the boys to dive for it, none of them can retrieve it, yet it is Kahu who later brings it back. Ihimaera uses moments like this to show that leadership and spiritual connection are not decided by gender: Kahu embodies the tradition more truly than any of the boys Koro favours.

Where Koro rejects Kahu, Ihimaera uses her great-grandmother Nanny Flowers to champion her and to challenge male authority directly. Loud and defiant, Nanny Flowers constantly threatens to “divorce” Koro for his stubbornness, and she insists that women too can lead. She does this by retelling the legend of her own ancestor Muriwai, who saved a sinking canoe by taking command and crying “Now I shall make myself a man”, declaring proudly that “Muriwai’s blood flows in my veins”. Ihimaera uses this embedded story to show that Maori tradition itself already contains powerful women, so Koro’s refusal to accept a female leader is exposed as his own prejudice rather than a true law of the culture. Nanny Flowers’ fierce love also reassures the reader that Kahu is not entirely alone against Koro.

Kahu’s role as the true heir is confirmed at the climax, when a herd of whales beaches itself and the tribe cannot save the great bull whale. Kahu climbs onto its back and rides it out to sea, echoing the ancient whale rider and saving her people from disaster. Ihimaera structures the whole novel around this parallel between the legendary Kahutia Te Rangi and the modern child, so that Kahu’s act feels like the fulfilment of a destiny. Crucially, where the boys dive only to prove themselves, Kahu acts out of pure love for her people, and Ihimaera presents her courage as instinctive rather than trained, which makes her the truer heir. The novel withholds whether she will even survive, so the reader shares the tribe’s fear and feels the full weight of her sacrifice. Her willingness to risk her life for her community shows the selfless courage of a true leader.

Only after this does Koro finally accept Kahu as his successor, admitting he was wrong to reject her. Koro’s change of heart, when it comes, feels earned rather than given, and Ihimaera uses it to model the wider lesson that an older generation must learn to trust the young, and women, if its culture is to endure. Ihimaera presents this change as the heart of the novel’s message: that a tradition can survive only if it adapts and allows women to lead. Writing partly for his own daughters, Ihimaera deliberately makes a girl the hero of a Maori legend, challenging both the sexism within the culture and the wider weakening of Maori identity in a modern, colonised New Zealand.

Kahu is presented as a new kind of leader — gentle, loving and brave — whose strength lies in her connection to her people rather than in power or gender. Through her, Ihimaera argues that culture and tradition are kept alive not by rigid rules but by those who love them most, whoever they happen to be.
