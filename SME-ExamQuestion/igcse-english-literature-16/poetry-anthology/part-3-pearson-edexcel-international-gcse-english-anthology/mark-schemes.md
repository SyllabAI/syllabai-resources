## Q1 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Chosen second poem: **Poem at Thirty-Nine** by **Alice Walker**

Both poems present growing up as something deeply shaped by a parent: Kipling's If— offers a father's advice on how to become a man, while Walker's Poem at Thirty-Nine looks back on what a father taught a now-grown daughter.

Both writers root growing up in the bond between parent and child, but they face in opposite directions. Kipling writes as a father directly addressing his son, ending "you'll be a Man, my son!", so the whole poem is a lesson in how to grow up well. Walker instead writes as an adult child, opening "How I miss my father", looking back on his influence. The contrast is clear: Kipling looks forward to the child's future, while Walker looks back with love and loss on a father who has died.

The two writers present what growing up requires very differently. Kipling makes it a test of virtues, building condition upon condition through the anaphora of "If you can…" — to "keep your head", to "meet with Triumph and Disaster / And treat those two impostors just the same". The relentless repetition makes maturity sound like a demanding checklist. Walker presents growing up more gently, as absorbing a parent's example: her father "taught me how" to manage money and "that telling the truth / did not always mean / a beating". Both show growing up as a kind of learning, but Kipling's is a stern code while Walker's is warm and personal.

Both poems present growing up as gaining independence, yet they shape it differently. In If—, independence means self-mastery — holding "on when there is nothing in you / Except the Will". Walker shows independence as becoming her own person: she learned to "escape / the life he knew", and now cooks and lives "just like him" but in her own way, "seasoning none of my life / the same way twice". Walker's growing up frees her from her father's path even as she carries his influence.

Both writers also present growing up as learning to cope with hardship, but they value endurance and freedom differently. Kipling makes maturity a matter of grim resilience: the grown man must "never breathe a word about your loss" and "stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools", so growing up means hiding pain and pressing on whatever happens. Walker presents coping not as silent suffering but as winning the freedom to live her own way; the closing image of her "chopping wood, / staring into the fire" shows a calm, self-possessed adult, not one braced against the world. Kipling's growing up hardens the child into a stoic; Walker's frees the child into herself.

The forms reinforce this difference. Kipling uses regular eight-line stanzas and a steady rhyme scheme, and the drilling repetition of "If" drives the poem forward like a code of manhood, suggesting that growing up is about discipline and control. Walker uses free verse with very short, broken lines that tumble down the page, suggesting a freer, more natural kind of growth and a mind moving through memory. The reader feels Kipling's growing up as something earned through effort, and Walker's as something absorbed through love.

Both poems close with a parent's pride, but the tone differs sharply. Kipling's promise — "Yours is the Earth" — is triumphant and forward-looking, a reward for growing up correctly. Walker imagines that her father "would have grown / to admire / the woman I've become", a quieter, sadder pride mixed with grief, since he is gone. Kipling's growing up is a challenge still to be met; Walker's is a journey already travelled and mourned.

In the end, both writers present growing up as shaped by a father's influence, but where Kipling offers a confident, forward-looking set of rules for becoming a man, Walker reflects back with tenderness and loss on the woman her father helped her become. Together they show growing up as both a lesson taught and a bond remembered.

## Q2 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Chosen second poem: **War Photographer** by **Carol Ann Duffy**

Both poems present fear of human cruelty, but MacNeice's Prayer Before Birth voices the terror of an unborn child dreading the world, while Duffy's War Photographer shows a man haunted by the suffering he has witnessed.

Both writers present fear of what human beings do to one another. MacNeice's unborn speaker fears that "the human race may with tall walls wall me, / with strong drugs dope me, with wise lies lure me", listing the ways people harm each other. Duffy's photographer carries the fear and pain of real atrocities, summed up in the bleak half-line "All flesh is grass", which reduces human life to something that quickly withers. Both poems are filled with dread of human violence, but MacNeice fears it in advance while Duffy's photographer has already seen it.

The writers use very different speakers to create fear. MacNeice gives a voice to a child "not yet born", which is strange and unsettling, making the fear feel universal — every human enters this frightening world. Duffy uses a third-person account of one ordinary man whose "hands, which did not tremble then / though seem to now", a small physical detail that shows fear surfacing only later, in safety. MacNeice's fear is a desperate plea against a world not yet faced; Duffy's is the slow, haunting aftershock of horror already witnessed.

Language and sound heighten the fear in both. MacNeice uses heavy repetition and pounding internal rhyme — "on black racks rack me, in blood-baths roll me" — so the sounds themselves feel violent, like blows. Duffy uses sudden, clipped sentences and place names — "Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh." — whose flatness makes the suffering feel endless and matter-of-fact. Both control sound carefully: MacNeice to make fear feel overwhelming, Duffy to make it feel cold and inescapable.

The forms shape the fear differently. MacNeice builds his poem as a prayer, each stanza beginning "I am not yet born", and the stanzas grow longer and more frantic, the lines tumbling and breaking as the fear mounts towards the final cry, "Otherwise kill me." Duffy uses controlled, regular stanzas, and this calm order is itself disturbing, suggesting how easily the world packages suffering — "five or six" images — for a comfortable Sunday reader. One form enacts panic; the other enacts a chilling detachment.

Both poems also fear powerlessness, the fear of being unable to act or feel. MacNeice's deepest dread is of being reduced to something without feeling at all, begging "Let them not make me a stone", as if losing his humanity would be worse than death. Duffy's photographer is powerless in a different way: he can record horror but cannot change it, and in the end "stares impassively" from the aeroplane, drained and numb. Even the reader is powerless to truly care, feeling only fleeting "tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers". MacNeice's speaker pleads to be killed rather than emptied of feeling, while Duffy's photographer has already been hollowed out, so both writers present numbness as a fate more frightening than death itself. For MacNeice the fear is of being turned to stone; for Duffy the fear is that everyone, witness and reader alike, already has been.

Finally, both poems fear that people lose their humanity. MacNeice dreads being made "a cog in a machine, a thing with / one face", terrified of becoming inhuman. Duffy fears the opposite kind of inhumanity in the reader, whose "eyeballs prick / with tears" but who ultimately "do not care". Both writers present a deep fear that cruelty and indifference will strip people of their humanity, whether the unborn child's or the comfortable reader's.

In the end, both poems present fear of a cruel, dehumanising world, but MacNeice voices the raw terror of a child about to be born into it, while Duffy shows the quieter, lasting fear of a man who has already seen its horrors and a world that looks away.

## Q3 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### Model answer

Chosen second poem: **Search For My Tongue** by **Sujata Bhatt**

Both poems present community, but Dharker's Blessing shows a whole neighbourhood drawn together by a sudden flow of water, while Bhatt's Search For My Tongue presents a more personal sense of belonging to a language and culture.

Dharker presents community as a crowd united by shared need. When the pipe bursts, "every man woman / child for streets around / butts in", and the list of containers — "brass, copper, aluminium, / plastic buckets" — suggests a whole mixed community rushing together. Bhatt's idea of community is quieter and inward: it lives in the "mother tongue", the language that ties her to her people and homeland. Both poems show belonging, but Dharker's is a public, physical gathering while Bhatt's is the private bond of shared language and roots.

The writers use religious language to show how precious community is. Dharker calls the gathered people a "congregation" and the water "the voice of a kindly god", turning the neighbourhood's rush for water into something sacred and shared. Bhatt treats her mother tongue almost as a living, sacred part of herself, something she fears losing when forced to "speak a foreign tongue". For both writers, what binds a community — water or language — is something holy that gives life meaning.

Language and imagery create very different moods. Dharker's writing is bright and joyful: "silver crashes to the ground" and children scream "in the liquid sun", the light imagery making the moment feel like a celebration. Bhatt uses a startling extended metaphor of a tongue that can "rot" and "die" but then "grows back", a "bud" that "blossoms out of my mouth". Dharker's images are about a shared outer event; Bhatt's are about an inner struggle to hold on to belonging.

Both poems also use sound, and the image of "tongues", to capture what binds people. Dharker's community finds its voice in "a roar of tongues" and a rush of "frantic hands", the noise turning a crowd into one excited body, a shared voice. Bhatt's poem is full of tongues too, but they belong to a single speaker holding "two tongues in your mouth"; the same word that means a roaring crowd in Dharker means private heritage in Bhatt. The shared image links a loud public community to a quiet personal one, showing belonging can be heard in a street or felt on a single tongue.

The forms reflect these differences. Dharker's poem builds to one long, breathless central stanza, the lines piling up to mirror the crowd surging together — the structure itself becomes a gathering. Bhatt actually weaves another language into her poem, including lines of Gujarati, so the form embodies the two cultures she belongs to. One structure shows a community coming together in a single rush; the other shows a speaker holding two communities, two tongues, inside herself.

Both poems also present community through the body, which makes belonging feel physical and real. Dharker fills her crowd with "frantic hands" reaching for water and "naked children" whose "small bones" remind us how bodily and vulnerable this shared moment is. Bhatt locates her community inside the body too, in a tongue that can "rot and die" yet "blossoms out of my mouth", so belonging to a culture is something she physically feels in her mouth. For both poets, community is not an abstract idea but something lived in the body, whether a crowd's grasping hands or a single speaker's living tongue.

Finally, both poems present community as something fragile and precious. In Blessing, the water and the joy it brings are sudden and temporary, and the focus on the children's "small bones" reminds us how vulnerable this community is. In Search For My Tongue, the speaker fears her mother tongue — and so her belonging — will be lost, until it reassuringly "blossoms" back. Both writers show that belonging to a community, whether of place or of language, is something that can be threatened and must be treasured.

In the end, both poems celebrate community, but Dharker presents it as a joyful, physical coming-together around a shared blessing, while Bhatt presents it as a deeper, more personal belonging carried in language and identity.

Chosen second poem: **Half-caste** by **John Agard**

Both poems present identity as caught between two cultures, but Bhatt's Search For My Tongue explores identity through language, while Agard's Half-caste challenges the labels other people use to define mixed identity.

Both writers present a divided or double identity. Bhatt imagines having "two tongues in your mouth", the "mother tongue" and "the foreign tongue", to show a speaker living between two languages and cultures. Agard's speaker is described by others as "half-caste", a single word that tries to split his identity in two. Both poems explore what it means to belong to more than one culture, but Bhatt looks inward at her own fear of loss, while Agard looks outward, confronting the people who label him.

The writers use extended images to capture identity. Bhatt's central metaphor is a tongue that can "rot and die" yet "grows back", a "bud" that "blossoms out of my mouth", showing that her cultural identity cannot be lost. Agard turns the insult "half-caste" into a series of images that mock it, asking whether "when light an shadow / mix in de sky" you get "a half-caste weather". Both use vivid imagery, but Bhatt's reassures her that identity survives, while Agard's exposes how absurd it is to call a person only "half" a being.

Language itself becomes a statement of identity. Bhatt weaves Gujarati into her poem, printing whole lines in her mother tongue, so the form proves her identity has survived. Agard writes in Caribbean Creole — "Explain yuself / wha yu mean" — using non-standard spelling and grammar as a proud assertion of who he is. For both poets, the very language of the poem is an act of identity, refusing to be erased by a dominant "standard" culture.

Both poems are also framed as a reply to an outsider who has questioned the speaker's identity, putting the listener on the spot. Bhatt begins "You ask me what I mean" and immediately turns the challenge back with "I ask you, what would you do", making the reader share her dilemma rather than judge it from outside. Agard speaks directly to his accuser throughout and ends by ordering them to "come back tomorrow" to hear the rest of his story on his own terms. Both poets refuse to let their identity be defined by the questioner, taking control of the conversation about who they are.

The tone each poet takes towards identity is strikingly different. Bhatt's tone is anxious and inward, full of dread that her identity will "rot and die" and that she will have to "spit it out", a private fear of disappearing. Agard's tone is defiant and witty from the start, mocking the label rather than fearing it: he jokes about having to "cast half-a-shadow" and throws in the scornful aside "ah rass", using humour as a weapon. Bhatt quietly defends an identity she is frightened of losing; Agard loudly attacks the prejudice that tries to diminish his.

The forms shape the argument. Bhatt's poem moves from fear to reassurance: the structure carries us from the tongue that seems to "die" to the moment it "blossoms", so the shape enacts the recovery of identity. Agard builds his poem through repetition and a challenging, questioning structure, repeating "Explain yuself / wha yu mean" so that he turns the tables on the accuser. One form dramatises an inner journey; the other stages a confrontation.

Finally, both poems end with confidence about identity. Bhatt closes in triumph as her mother tongue "blossoms out of my mouth", showing identity cannot be suppressed. Agard ends by demanding the listener return "wid de whole of yu mind" to hear "de other half / of my story", insisting on being seen as a whole person. Both writers refuse to let their identities be reduced or lost, ending on a note of self-assertion.

In the end, both poems present identity as something to be defended and celebrated, but Bhatt explores it through the survival of her mother tongue, while Agard fights back against the labels others use to divide and diminish him.

## Q5 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

Chosen second poem: **Hide and Seek** by **Vernon Scannell**

Both poems capture childhood from a child's-eye view, but Fanthorpe's Half-past Two shows a boy lost in a timeless innocence, while Scannell's Hide and Seek shows a child's excitement turning to lonely abandonment.

Both writers recreate a child's way of seeing. Fanthorpe invents compound words like "Gettinguptime" and "Timeformykisstime" to capture how a small child experiences the day, not by clock-time but by events. Scannell uses the second person and the present tense — "Call out. Call loud: 'I'm ready! Come and find me!'" — to put us inside the child's mind during the game. Both use language to make the reader feel childhood from the inside, though Fanthorpe focuses on a child's confusion about time while Scannell focuses on the thrill of hiding.

Both poems show adults failing children. In Half-past Two, the teacher punishes the boy but, "Being cross, she'd forgotten / She hadn't taught him Time", so he is trapped by an adult's carelessness. In Hide and Seek, the child's friends abandon him — they "scuffle, and they're gone" — leaving him alone in the cold shed. Both writers show childhood as a vulnerable state in which children are let down by the bigger people around them.

Both use rich sensory detail to capture the child's world. Fanthorpe's boy escapes "Into the smell of old chrysanthemums" and "the silent noise his hangnail made", a dreamy, timeless place beyond adult rules. Scannell's child is wrapped in physical sensation too — "The sacks in the toolshed smell like the seaside", "the cold bites through your coat". Both root childhood in vivid, bodily experience, but Fanthorpe's sensations are magical and freeing while Scannell's grow cold and uncomfortable as the wait drags on.

Both poems also show childhood running on a child's own sense of time rather than the clock. Fanthorpe's boy cannot read "half-past two" and so escapes into a "clockless land" where adult time stops altogether, making his childhood a freedom from the clock. Scannell's child has no clock either, only feeling — "It seems a long time since they went away" — and that slow, dragging time is what turns his game sour. For both writers, childhood is measured by feeling, not minutes, but the same freedom from clock-time frees one boy and traps the other.

Both poems also present childhood as a state of powerlessness, though the children seem to have very different amounts of control. Fanthorpe's boy is helpless before an adult world he cannot understand: he "couldn't click its language" of clocks, and at the end is "slotted… back into schooltime" like an object moved by others. Scannell's child appears to be in charge, even ordering himself about with confident commands — "Push off the sacks" — yet his triumphant "I've won!" is met by an empty garden, so his control is an illusion. Both poems show that children, whether confused or confident, are finally at the mercy of a world run by others.

The structures move in different directions. Fanthorpe's poem lets the boy "escape" into "the clockless land for ever", and although he is returned to "schooltime", the ending keeps a sense of wonder and lost innocence. Scannell's poem builds suspense and then collapses into a chilling twist: the child emerges to find "The darkening garden watches. Nothing stirs." The shift from excited present-tense commands to that silent, empty ending turns a game into a small tragedy of abandonment.

The two poems end on very different notes. Fanthorpe's tone stays tender and slightly magical, presenting childhood as a place of innocent imagination, where "time hides tick-less waiting to be born". Scannell's ends bleak and lonely, the final question — "But where are they who sought you?" — leaving the child, and the reader, with a sense of being forgotten. One poem celebrates the freedom of the childish mind; the other exposes childhood's hidden fears.

In the end, both writers present childhood through a vivid child's-eye view in which adults let children down, but Fanthorpe finds in it a magical, timeless innocence, while Scannell uncovers loneliness and abandonment beneath a simple game. Together they show childhood as both wonderful and frighteningly fragile.

## Q6 — very_hard — 30 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 30 marks
### Model answer

Chosen second poem: **Poem at Thirty-Nine** by **Alice Walker**

Both poems explore powerful memories of a parent, but Lawrence's Piano is overwhelmed by a longing to return to childhood, while Walker's Poem at Thirty-Nine remembers a father with warmth and a sense of what he gave her.

Both poems show a memory suddenly triggered. In Piano, a woman "singing to me" carries the speaker "back down the vista of years" to a childhood scene. In Poem at Thirty-Nine, the everyday act of "Writing deposit slips and checks" makes the speaker "think of him". Both writers show how ordinary present moments can unlock the past, but Lawrence is pulled helplessly backward while Walker reflects more calmly.

Both remember a parent through tender, specific images. Lawrence pictures himself "A child sitting under the piano" pressing "the small, poised feet of a mother who smiles as she sings", a warm, safe memory. Walker remembers her father teaching her "how" to write slips and that "telling the truth / did not always mean / a beating". Both memories are loving, but Lawrence's is wordless and sensory while Walker's centres on the lessons and values her father passed on.

The poems differ on what memory does to the speaker. Lawrence calls the song's pull "insidious", admitting it "Betrays me back" until "the heart of me weeps", as if the memory overpowers his adult self. Walker's memory strengthens her: she has grown into someone who cooks and lives "just like him". For Lawrence memory is almost a weakness that unmans him; for Walker it is a source of identity and pride.

The senses work differently in each poem's memory. Lawrence's memory is triggered and ruled by sound: the "insidious mastery of song" overwhelms him and the "tinkling piano" of the past drowns out the present, so memory is something heard and helplessly surrendered to. Walker's memory lives instead in active doing, especially food: her father "cooked like a person / dancing" and "craved the voluptuous / sharing / of good food", and she keeps him alive by cooking herself. Lawrence is washed back into the past by a sound he cannot resist; Walker carries her father forward through what she does with her hands.

The forms suit each mood. Lawrence uses regular rhyming couplets and long, flowing lines, and the soft sounds — "the boom of the tingling strings" — lull the reader like the music itself, mirroring how he is swept away. Walker uses free verse with short, broken lines that feel thoughtful and unhurried, as if she is turning the memory over piece by piece. One form drowns the speaker in feeling; the other lets her examine it.

The two poems also treat childhood within memory very differently. Lawrence longs to climb back into his own childhood, confessing that "The glamour / Of childish days is upon me" until his grown-up "manhood is cast / Down", as if memory drags the adult back into being a boy.
