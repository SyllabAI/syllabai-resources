# Mark Schemes — How to Answer the Unseen Poetry Question
**Unseen Poetry** · Edexcel IGCSE English Literature (4ET1)


## Q1 — very_hard — 20 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 20 marks
## Model answer

In ‘Mirror Self’, Khadeesha Williams presents family and heritage as essential parts of personal identity. The poem shows how the speaker’s sense of self is built from physical features, cultural background and emotional connections to both family and place.

From the very start, Williams uses the mirror as a powerful symbol of reflection and family connection. When the speaker looks in the mirror, they see not only their own face but also features of their family: “grandmother cheekbones / mother’s strong brow / father’s proud mouth”. This imagery suggests that family traits are not just physical, but represent a deeper connection to past generations. It suggests that identity is inherited as much as it is individual. The use of the adjectives “strong” and “proud” reflects admiration and respect, suggesting the speaker deeply values their family’s influence.

Williams also uses metaphors from nature and geography to explore the theme of heritage. The lines “carved by island wind / polished by city rain” suggest that the speaker’s identity has been shaped by different environments. The word “carved” gives a sense of something carefully formed, while “polished” suggests refinement and personal growth. Together these contrasting images show that the speaker’s family heritage is linked to both rural and urban experiences, blending together to create who they are.

The poet explores the idea of being made from many influences through the image of a “patchwork quilt / stitched by two worlds.” The quilt symbolises the way the speaker’s identity is made from different cultural, familial and geographical elements. The idea of being “stitched by two worlds” could suggest the merging of different cultures, perhaps immigrant heritage, or simply the experience of growing up between two different places. This metaphor shows that identity is complex and layered.

Despite these external influences, the poem shows that the speaker’s inner identity is something unique and personal. The phrase “But my song / sing soft / from inside” suggests that underneath all the inherited features and cultural influences, there exists a quiet yet significant personal and individual voice. This “song” symbolises the speaker’s true self, shaped by heritage but not limited by it.

Finally, the poet uses the structure of the poem — especially the short, fragmented lines — to reflect the theme of mixed identity. Lines like “Not here / not there / but all of me / at once” show the speaker’s acceptance of being from multiple places and backgrounds at the same time. Indeed, this fragmented form reflects the idea of piecing together multiple elements, heritage, culture, family, into something whole. Similarly, the repetition of “not” followed by “all of me” emphasises that the speaker refuses to be defined by just one part of their heritage. Instead, they embrace the complexity and richness of their whole identity. The use of enjambment, where the speaker’s thoughts spill over from one line to the next, creates a sense of movement, similar to the speaker’s evolving sense of self.

In conclusion, Williams presents family and heritage as essential but not limiting. Through images of physical features, nature metaphors and the idea of a patchwork quilt, the poem shows how identity is made from many influences, yet the speaker’s personal voice still emerges clearly.

>

> - Select **key images or metaphors** and explore their effects in detail

## Model answer

In ‘The Trees are Not Impressed’, Ruby O’Neill presents nature as calm, purposeful and indifferent to human worries. The poem contrasts human emotional dramas with nature’s simple, practical focus on survival, using humour, blunt imagery and a conversational tone to highlight this difference.

The poet immediately sets up this contrast by mocking human self-importance. The phrase, “The trees couldn’t care less / about your existential crisis”, shows how little nature is concerned with human concerns or their overthinking. The casual, sarcastic tone of “couldn’t care less” creates a humorous tone, which is used to undercut the seriousness of their “existential crisis”. This irony not only amuses the reader but also reinforces the idea that nature operates independently of our personal dramas and anxieties.

Throughout the poem, O’Neill uses humorous comparisons to emphasise how nature’s concerns are physical and functional. The trees are described as “manufacturing oxygen / like intertwining green sweatshops,” with the simile comparing them to tireless factories, emphasising their constant, purposeful work. Similarly, the phrase “slow-motion applause / for the sun” adds a touch of gentle irony and shows how nature celebrates simple, essential things, unlike humans who become absorbed in personal and emotional problems.

The poet highlights human self-absorption with an everyday example: “you’re crying in a Lidl car park / because you sent a risky text”. This abrupt shift to a relatable, slightly embarrassing scenario shows how trivial our concerns can be when compared to the vastness of nature’s ongoing work. The casual mention of a “risky text” being “left on read”(a very modern and personal dilemma) contrasts sharply with the important and vital processes of the natural world. This juxtaposition makes human worries seem small and fleeting, especially when viewed alongside nature’s quiet resilience.

Birds and daffodils are also shown as indifferent to human interpretations. The birds are “singing, / not for you, / but because they have biological imperatives”, suggesting they act out of instinct, not emotion. Similarly, the poet dismisses poetic symbolism by stating “the daffodils don’t dream / of being symbols / of renewal or rebirth.” This blunt rejection of a traditional romantic metaphor reinforces the idea that nature’s concerns are practical, not poetic.

The structure of the poem, with its short stanzas and fragmented lines, helps maintain a casual tone. The brevity of each line mimics natural speech and gives the poem a conversational rhythm, reinforcing its almost spoken-word quality. This makes the poem feel direct and accessible, as if the poet is having a frank conversation with the reader. Similarly, the irregular line lengths and free verse form allow the poem to flow in a way that feels spontaneous which  matches the poet’s wry, observational tone.

The poem ends on a more thoughtful note with the line “And maybe, / that’s what makes them perfect.” Here, O’Neill suggests that nature’s simplicity and lack of self-awareness is admirable. While humans are often overwhelmed by feelings and self-analysis, nature just exists and thrives. Indeed, this final reflection invites the reader to reconsider their own priorities and offers a moment of sincerity, which contrasts with the speaker’s earlier sarcasm.

In conclusion, O’Neill presents the difference between nature and human concerns through humour, blunt imagery and relatable examples. Nature is shown as practical and indifferent, while humans are often caught up in unnecessary emotional drama. The poem invites us to admire nature’s simplicity and perhaps learn from it.

In ‘The city knows my name’, Tayo Anichebe presents city life as both harsh and threatening, but also familiar and inspiring. Through personification, vivid imagery, sound effects and personal reflections, the poet shows how the city can be intimidating yet deeply connected to the speaker’s identity.

At the start of the poem, the city is described as aggressive and impolite. The personification of the city “coughing its diesel breath” creates a dirty, polluted image, showing how unpleasant city life can be. The harsh onomatopoeic sound of “Sirens shriek” adds to the atmosphere of noise and chaos, making the city feel hostile. The phrase “the city snarls beneath my feet” again uses personification to give the impression of a dangerous, living beast, suggesting that the city is alive but unfriendly.

Anichebe uses sensory imagery to highlight the grimy, neglected side of the city. The “pavements sweat / with last night’s rain” creates an uncomfortable, unpleasant feeling. The “oily puddles reflecting / the distorted fried chicken glow / of late shops” blends visual and tactile imagery, showing the city as both dirty and artificial. Even graffiti is described negatively, as “scars” that are “old wounds, reopened,” suggesting the city has a history of damage and violence.

However, as the poem develops, the tone shifts to show a more personal, affectionate view of the city. The volta signified by the line, “But the city is ours,” marks a change from criticism to ownership and pride. The speaker states how the cracked pavements “knows my name,” and the billboard “nods” as the speaker walks past. This continued use of personification shows a sense of belonging — despite its flaws, the city recognises and welcomes the speaker.

Anichebe also presents the city as a place of inspiration. The corner shop window becomes “a mirror,” giving the speaker a glimpse of their own identity within the city. The reflection is “blurred but shining,” suggesting that life in the city might be messy, but it still has moments of beauty and self-recognition.

The final lines compare the city’s movement to “inspiration in my chest” and “a poem half-sung, / half-built.” This metaphor shows that, for the speaker, the city is not just a place to live but a source of creativity, as it is an integral part of the speaker’s world. The idea of a poem being “half-built” suggests that the city is constantly being created and shaped, just like the speaker’s evolving identity.

The structure of the poem reflects these contrasts. The poet uses free verse and enjambment, allowing the lines to mimic the energy and relentless nature of city life. The use of short, sharp stanzas capture the chaotic noise and fast pace of the city, while longer, more reflective lines slow the pace to show deeper moments of personal connection and reflection. This contrast mirrors the dual nature of the city as a place that is both harsh and inspiring.

In conclusion, Anichebe presents city life as a place of noise, pollution and scars, but also as a place of belonging, pride and creative energy. The poet uses vivid imagery, personification and metaphor to show how these contrasting ideas exist side by side in urban life.

In ‘My Fair Weather System’, Paloma Guzman presents communication with a parent as complicated, often frustrating, but ultimately comforting. Through the use of metaphors, humour and familiar, everyday examples, the poet explores how a relationship with a mother can be a mix of conflict and care.

The poet uses a weather metaphor to describe the mother’s presence, calling her “a low-pressure front / that rolls in around teatime”. This comparison suggests that the speaker’s interactions with their mother bring a certain tension, like gloomy weather, which is accompanied by the smell of “wet laundry and disappointment.” This humorous but slightly critical description shows that their communication is often tinged with frustration or unease.

Guzman also presents the mother’s personality through contrast. She “has two settings: / encouraging me to pursue my dreams / and reminding me to take the chicken out of the freezer.” The everyday task is juxtaposed alongside a life aspiration, showing the mix of everyday nagging and genuine support that often occurs in parent-child conversations. The casual tone and blunt humour here make the relationship feel authentic and familiar.

The poet shows how communication changes over time but remains imperfect. The anecdote about teeth — from being told they are “charmingly crooked” to being offered braces for Christmas — highlights the mother’s way of giving backhanded compliments. This shows that communication can be both caring and critical at once, reflecting a complicated bond.

The line “We communicate best / in passive-aggressive WhatsApp messages” conveys the way modern parental communication often occurs through technology. This image captures the way minor disagreements play out in digital conversations, showing that while the methods of communication have changed, the tensions remain.

However, the poem ends on a more tender note. On “stormy nights”, when things feel uncertain, the mother’s voice becomes “the sound of a kettle boiling / in a house that has never run out of tea.” This comforting image suggests that, despite the frustrations, the mother’s presence is reassuring and familiar. The kettle symbolises warmth, routine and reliability, showing that communication isn’t always verbal — it can be felt through small, everyday actions.

Further, the poet’s use of structure reflects this shift in tone. The previous use of short, impactful stanzas convey the poem’s humour and sarcasm, while the final stanza takes on a slower and more thoughtful pace to reveal a deeper emotional bond. This mirrors the reality of communicating with a parent — full of ups and downs, but grounded in love.

In conclusion, Guzman presents communication with a parent as a mix of dry humour, everyday nagging and deep, unspoken comfort. Through weather metaphors, relatable humour and domestic imagery, the poem shows how complex, but vital, this relationship is.

Muhammed also shows how imagination enables children to reimagine everyday objects as extraordinary things. Despite the fragility of their imagined world, the poem suggests that childhood friendships and bonds are strong. Their “kingdom” is “held by shoelace knots / and forever alliances.” This shows how children build strong bonds through simple things, like tying shoes or making promises, suggesting loyalty and unity. The poem’s free verse and short stanzas reflect the playful, spontaneous nature of children’s games. The final image — “the wind carries / our laughter / like flags” — compares their laughter to victory flags, symbolising joy, freedom and ownership of their space.
