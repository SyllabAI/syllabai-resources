### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The oxidation state and geometry of the platinum complexes are:
> 
> - Oxidation state of the platinum ion = +4 **[1 mark]**
> - Geometry of the complexes = Octahedral **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> To deduce the oxidation state of platinum, use oxoplatin as it contains familiar ligands. The charge contributions are:
> 
> - OH<sup>-</sup> contributes -1
> - Cl<sup>-</sup> contributes -1
> - NH<sub>3</sub> contributes 0 (neutral ligand)
> 
> Since the overall charge on the complex is 0:
> 
> Pt + 2(-1) + 2(-1) + 2(0) = 0
> 
> Pt - 4 = 0
> 
> Pt = +4
> 
> All four complexes have 6 coordinate bonds, so the geometry must be octahedral.

**[4 marks]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for each of the three correct three-dimensional stereoisomer structures (3 marks)
> 
> 1 mark for correctly identifying the enantiomeric pair as the chiral stereoisomers

> **[exam-tip]**
> To find all stereoisomers, arrange the three pairs of different ligands (NH<sub>3</sub>, Cl and OH) in all possible positions around the central platinum atom. Be careful not to draw the same structure twice from a different angle.
> 
> None of the geometric isomers are chiral. You can check this by looking for an internal mirror plane through the structure. If the structure is superimposable on its mirror image, it is not chiral.
> 
> The optical isomers (enantiomers) are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. These are the only oxoplatin stereoisomers that lack any internal mirror plane.

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The electronic configurations of a Co atom and a Co<sup>2+</sup> ion are:
> 
> - Co atom: 1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>6</sup> 3d<sup>7</sup> 4s<sup>2</sup>
> **OR**
> 1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>6</sup> 4s<sup>2</sup> 3d<sup>7</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - Co<sup>2+</sup> ion: 1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>6</sup> 3d<sup>7</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Although the Periodic Table suggests electrons fill in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, the correct filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s. The 3d orbital is slightly lower in energy than 4s, so 3d fills before 4s.
> 
> You can check your answer by adding up all the electrons:
> 
> 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 7 + 2 = 27
> 
> When writing the Co<sup>2+</sup> ion configuration, remember that 4s electrons are lost before 3d electrons. Co<sup>2+</sup> has 25 electrons, so the 4s electrons are removed first, giving 1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>6</sup> 3d<sup>7</sup>.
The complex ion formed is the tetrachlorocobaltate(II) ion:

![Three-dimensional tetrahedral diagram of [CoCl4]2- with cobalt at the centre and four chloride ligands at the corners, showing correct 3D bonds](assets/001-tetrahedral-cocl4-2-complex-with-cobalt-at-the-c.png)

**[2 marks]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for a correct three-dimensional tetrahedral diagram with four chloride ligands and 3D bonds shown
> 
> 1 mark for correct overall charge: 2- / [CoCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>

> **[exam-tip]**
> When concentrated hydrochloric acid is added to [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>, all six water ligands are replaced by four chloride ions. Chloride ions are larger than water molecules, so the coordination number changes from 6 to 4, and the geometry changes from octahedral to tetrahedral.
> 
> The overall charge: Co<sup>2+</sup> + 4(Cl<sup>-</sup>) = +2 + 4(-1) = -2, giving [CoCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>.
The completed three-dimensional diagram of [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> is:

![Three-dimensional octahedral diagram of [Co(H2O)6]2+ with six water ligands and bond angles of 90 degrees and 180 degrees labeled on the diagram](assets/011-three-dimensional-octahedral-diagram-of-co-h2o-6.png)

**[3 marks]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for a correct three-dimensional octahedral structure showing six water ligands **AND** correct 2+ overall charge
> 
> 1 mark for stating octahedral geometry
> 
> 1 mark for stating a bond angle of 90° on the diagram (allow 180°)

> **[exam-tip]**
> Complexes with six ligands always form an octahedral geometry. The bond angles in an octahedral complex are 90° between adjacent ligands and 180° between opposite ligands.
> 
> Since water is a neutral ligand, the overall charge on the complex equals the charge of the metal ion: the [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> complex has an overall charge of 2+.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) Observations and equation for addition of a few drops of NH<sub>3</sub> (aq):
> 
> - Observation: blue / blue-green precipitate **[1 mark]**
> - [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2NH<sub>3</sub> (aq) ⇌ Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>4</sub>(OH)<sub>2</sub> (s) + 2NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> (aq)
> **OR**
> [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2OH<sup>-</sup> (aq) ⇌ Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>4</sub>(OH)<sub>2</sub> (s) + 2H<sub>2</sub>O (l) **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Observations and equation for addition of excess NH<sub>3</sub> (aq):
> 
> - Observation: (straw) yellow / brown solution **[1 mark]**
> - Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>4</sub>(OH)<sub>2</sub> (s) + 6NH<sub>3</sub> (aq) ⇌ [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 4H<sub>2</sub>O (l) + 2OH<sup>-</sup> (aq)
> **OR**
> Co(OH)<sub>2</sub> (s) + 6NH<sub>3</sub> (aq) ⇌ [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2OH<sup>-</sup> (aq) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Cobalt(II) and copper(II) are the two transition metals commonly tested for ligand substitution reactions with aqueous ammonia.
> 
> Cobalt(II) undergoes complete substitution with six water ligands replaced by six ammonia ligands, forming [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>. In contrast, copper(II) undergoes partial substitution with only four of the six water ligands replaced.
> 
> The [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> complex is unstable in air and readily oxidises to form a blue [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> solution. You may be asked about this in a follow-on question.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The meaning of the term hexadentate ligand:
> 
> - A ligand that can donate six lone pairs of electrons (to a central metal atom / ion) **[1 mark]**
> - To form six dative (covalent) / coordinate bonds **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) The type of reaction is:
> 
> - Ligand exchange / substitution / replacement / displacement **[1 mark]**
> 
> iii) The stability constant expression for [CoEDTA]<sup>2-</sup> is:
> 
> - *K*<sub>stab</sub> = [[CoEDTA]<sup>2-</sup>] / ([[Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>][[EDTA]<sup>4-</sup>]) **[1 mark]**
> 
> iv) The value of *K*<sub>stab</sub> indicates that:
> 
> - [CoEDTA]<sup>2-</sup> is (very) stable / more stable than [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A ligand is a species that donates a lone pair of electrons to a central metal atom or ion to form a coordinate (dative covalent) bond. The prefix tells you how many lone pairs it can donate: mono (1), bi (2), and so on up to hexa (6).
> 
> Since all six water ligands in [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> are replaced by one EDTA<sup>4-</sup> ligand, this is a ligand exchange reaction. The equation is:
> 
> [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> + EDTA<sup>4-</sup> ⇌ [CoEDTA]<sup>2-</sup> + 6H<sub>2</sub>O
> 
> The *K*<sub>stab</sub> expression follows from this equation in the same way as any equilibrium expression. Water is not included because it is the solvent:
> 
> *K*<sub>stab</sub> = [[CoEDTA]<sup>2-</sup>] / ([[Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>][[EDTA]<sup>4-</sup>])
> 
> The greater the value of *K*<sub>stab</sub>, the more stable the complex ion is relative to the aquo complex it replaced.
