### Q1
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The full electronic configuration of a Cu atom and a Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion:
> 
> - Cu atom: 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>10</sup>4s<sup>1</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion: 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>9</sup> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Three characteristic properties of copper and its compounds:
> 
> Any **three** from (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - Variable oxidation states
> - Form coloured ions / compounds
> - Form complexes / complex ions
> - Act as catalysts
> 
> **[3 marks]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part i), copper has an anomalous electronic configuration. The expected configuration would be [Ar]3d<sup>9</sup>4s<sup>2</sup>, but the actual configuration is [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>4s<sup>1</sup>, because a fully filled 3d subshell is particularly stable. When Cu loses two electrons to form Cu<sup>2+</sup>, it loses the 4s<sup>1</sup> electron first, then one 3d electron, giving [Ar]3d<sup>9</sup>.
> 
> For part ii), the four characteristic properties of transition elements are: variable oxidation states, formation of coloured ions, formation of complex ions, and catalytic activity. These apply to all first-row transition elements, not just copper.

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part i), to find the overall equation, add the two steps and cancel species that appear on both sides. V<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> cancels (reactant in step 1, product in step 2) and V<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub> cancels (product in step 1, reactant in step 2), leaving SO<sub>2</sub> + ½O<sub>2</sub> → SO<sub>3</sub>.
> 
> For part ii), the first mark is for identifying that the catalyst is regenerated (not consumed). The second mark is for the mechanism: vanadium cycles between oxidation states +5 and +4 across the two steps, providing an alternative reaction pathway. This ability to cycle between oxidation states is a general property of transition metal catalysts.

### Q3
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states but Group 1 elements do not because:
> 
> Transition metals:
> 
> - The 3d and 4s subshells are similar in energy
> **OR**
> Successive ionisation energies increase gradually **[1 mark]**
> 
> Group 1 elements:
> 
> - The second electron occupies a much lower energy level / inner shell
> **OR**
> There is a very large increase in ionisation energy between the first and second ionisation energies **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Transition metals can exist in multiple oxidation states because the 3d and 4s subshells have similar energies. Electrons from either subshell can be removed with gradually increasing ionisation energies, so transition metals can form ions with different charges (e.g. Fe<sup>2+</sup> and Fe<sup>3+</sup>; Ti<sup>2+</sup>, Ti<sup>3+</sup>, and Ti<sup>4+</sup>).
> 
> For Group 1 elements, the first electron removed comes from the outer shell, but the second would come from a much lower energy inner shell. The very large jump in ionisation energy makes a second ionisation effectively impossible under normal conditions, so Group 1 elements exist only in the +1 oxidation state.

> **[exam-tip]**
> To find the oxidation state of the transition element, use the overall charge and the known oxidation states of the other atoms:
> 
> - Neutral ligands (H<sub>2</sub>O, NH<sub>3</sub>): oxidation state = 0
> - OH<sup>-</sup> ligand: oxidation state = −1
> - O in Mn<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>: oxidation state = −2
> 
> For [Cr(OH)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup>: Cr + (6 x −1) = −3, so Cr = +3.
> 
> For Mn<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>: (2 x Mn) + (3 x −2) = 0, so Mn = +3.
> 
> A common error is to write the oxidation state as 3+ rather than +3. CIE mark schemes will not award the mark for the wrong notation.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The full electronic configuration for the metal ions:
> 
> - [Zn(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>: 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>10</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - [Co(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>: 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>7</sup> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Zinc is not classified as a transition element because:
> 
> - The Zn<sup>2+</sup> ion has a complete / full 3d subshell **[1 mark]**
> - The electronic configuration of Zn<sup>2+</sup> is [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>, showing no partially filled 3d subshell **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part i), when writing the electronic configuration of an ion, the 4s electrons are removed first. Both Zn<sup>2+</sup> and Co<sup>2+</sup> lose their two 4s electrons:
> 
> - Zn: [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>4s<sup>2</sup> → Zn<sup>2+</sup>: [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup> (3d subshell is full)
> - Co: [Ar]3d<sup>7</sup>4s<sup>2</sup> → Co<sup>2+</sup>: [Ar]3d<sup>7</sup> (3d subshell is incomplete)
> 
> For part ii), the definition of a transition element requires at least one stable ion with a partially filled 3d subshell. The Zn<sup>2+</sup> ion has a complete 3d subshell ([Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>), so zinc does not qualify.

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The definition of a transition element:
> 
> - A d-block element **[1 mark]**
> - That forms one or more stable ions with an incomplete / partially filled 3d subshell **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Both marks are needed for the complete definition. The first mark is for identifying it as a d-block element, and the second mark is for the key criterion: it must form at least one stable ion with a partially filled 3d subshell.
> 
> Two d-block elements do not qualify as transition elements: scandium (Sc<sup>3+</sup> has an empty 3d subshell) and zinc (Zn<sup>2+</sup> has a full 3d subshell, [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>). Neither ion has a partially filled d subshell.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The full electronic configuration of Ti<sup>2+</sup>:
> 
> - 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>2</sup>
> **OR**
> [Ar]3d<sup>2</sup> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Zinc is not classified as a transition element because:
> 
> - The Zn<sup>2+</sup> ion has a complete / full 3d subshell **[1 mark]**
> - The electronic configuration of Zn<sup>2+</sup> is 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>10</sup> / [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>, showing no partially filled 3d subshell **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part i), both the full notation (1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>6</sup>3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>6</sup>3d<sup>2</sup>) and the noble gas shorthand ([Ar]3d<sup>2</sup>) are accepted.
> 
> For part ii), the critical point is the electronic configuration of the Zn<sup>2+</sup> ion, not the Zn atom. The Zn atom has configuration [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>4s<sup>2</sup>, but when it forms Zn<sup>2+</sup> it loses both 4s electrons, leaving [Ar]3d<sup>10</sup>. Since the 3d subshell is completely full, Zn<sup>2+</sup> does not have a partially filled d subshell, so zinc does not qualify as a transition element.
