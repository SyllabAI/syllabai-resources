### Q1
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The lithium-iodine electrochemical cell is a dry cell because:
> 
> - There is no water / liquid present in the cell **[1 mark]**
> - Water / liquid could react with the lithium electrode
> **AND**
> (this) could cause the cell to explode
> **OR**
> (this) could cause a build-up of pressure inside the cell **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> This question uses knowledge of Group 1 metal reactivity. Lithium, like all Group 1 metals, reacts vigorously with water to produce a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas. Since the cell is sealed, hydrogen production would cause a dangerous pressure build-up.
> 
> For both marks, you need two separate points: that there is no liquid in the cell, and why the presence of liquid would be harmful.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The overall equation for the lithium-iodine electrochemical cell:
> 
> - 2Li + I<sub>2</sub> → 2Li<sup>+</sup> + 2I<sup>–</sup> **[1 mark]**
> 
> State symbols are not required

> **[exam-tip]**
> Combine the two half-equations, ensuring the electrons cancel:
> 
> - Oxidation (anode): 2Li → 2Li<sup>+</sup> + 2e<sup>–</sup>
> - Reduction (cathode): I<sub>2</sub> + 2e<sup>–</sup> → 2I<sup>–</sup>
> 
> Adding these together cancels the 2e<sup>–</sup>, giving the overall equation 2Li + I<sub>2</sub> → 2Li<sup>+</sup> + 2I<sup>–</sup>.
To calculate *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>cell</sub> for the lithium-iodine electrochemical cell:

*E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>cell</sub> = *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>reduced</sub> − *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>oxidised</sub>

*E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>cell</sub> = (+0.54) − (−3.04)

*E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>cell</sub> = **+3.58 V** **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The iodine half-cell (I<sub>2</sub> / I<sup>–</sup>, *E*<sup>θ</sup> = +0.54 V) undergoes reduction as it has the more positive electrode potential. Lithium (Li<sup>+</sup> / Li, *E*<sup>θ</sup> = −3.04 V) is oxidised.
> 
> The Ni<sup>2+</sup> / Ni half-cell is a distractor. It is not involved in the lithium-iodine cell.
To calculate the number of days for 0.08 g of the lithium electrode to be used up:

- Calculate the moles of lithium:

n(Li) = $\frac{0.08}{6.9}$

n(Li) =  = **1.16 × 10**<sup>**-2**</sup>** mol** **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the charge required:

*Q* = n × *F*

*Q* = 1.16 × 10<sup>-2</sup> × 9.65 × 10<sup>4</sup>

*Q* = **1119 C** **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the time in seconds:

*t* = $\frac{1119}{1.5\times10^{-5}}$

*t* = **7.46 × 10**<sup>**7**</sup>** s** **[1 mark]**

- Convert to days:

*t* = $\frac{7.46\times10^{7}}{60\times60\times24}$

*t* = **863 days** **[1 mark]**

*E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>cell</sub> = *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>reduced</sub> − *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>oxidised</sub>

- Rearrange the cell potential equation for *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>oxidised</sub>:

Since the copper half-cell undergoes reduction, it provides *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>reduced</sub>. Rearrange to find *E*<sup>θ</sup><sub>oxidised</sub> for the chromium half-cell.
> 
> Always identify which half-cell is reduced and which is oxidised before applying the equation. The question tells you copper is reduced, so chromium must be oxidised.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two half-equations are:
> 
> - Cr (s) → Cr<sup>3+</sup> (aq) + 3e<sup>–</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - 2H<sup>+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> → H<sub>2</sub> (g) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The standard electrode potential of Cr / Cr<sup>3+</sup> is negative (−0.74 V from the previous part), so chromium is more easily oxidised than hydrogen. This means chromium undergoes oxidation (loses electrons) and hydrogen undergoes reduction (gains electrons).

charge for 1 mol Cu = $\frac{900}{4.71\times10^{-3}}$

charge for 1 mol Cu = 191 083 C **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In an aqueous solution, H<sup>+</sup> and OH<sup>–</sup> ions from water are also present and can be discharged. To predict which ion is discharged:
> 
> At the cathode, the cation with the most positive *E*<sup>θ</sup> is discharged.
> 
> - In AgF, Ag<sup>+</sup> (*E*<sup>θ</sup> = +0.80 V) and H<sup>+</sup> (*E*<sup>θ</sup> = 0.00 V) are both present.
> - Ag<sup>+</sup> has the more positive *E*<sup>θ</sup>, so Ag is deposited at the cathode.
> 
> At the anode, the anion discharged depends on its identity:
> 
> - Halide ions (Cl<sup>–</sup>, Br<sup>–</sup>, I<sup>–</sup>) are discharged in preference to OH<sup>–</sup> from water, producing the halogen (Cl<sub>2</sub>, Br<sub>2</sub>, or I<sub>2</sub>).
> - F<sup>–</sup> is never discharged in aqueous solution.
> 
>   - F<sub>2</sub>/F<sup>–</sup> has the highest reduction *E*<sup>θ</sup> in the table (+2.87 V), making F<sup>–</sup> far too difficult to oxidise.
>   - OH<sup>–</sup> from water is preferentially oxidised, forming O<sub>2</sub>.
> - Polyatomic anions (SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup>, NO<sub>3</sub><sup>–</sup>) are never discharged in preference to OH<sup>–</sup>.
> 
>   - OH<sup>–</sup> is oxidised, forming O<sub>2</sub>.
> 
> Applying these rules:
> 
> - AgF:
> 
>   - F<sup>–</sup> is never discharged in aqueous solution.
>   - So, OH<sup>–</sup> is oxidised → O<sub>2</sub> at the anode.
> - FeSO<sub>4</sub>:
> 
>   - SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2–</sup> is a polyatomic anion.
>   - So, OH<sup>–</sup> is oxidised → O<sub>2</sub> at the anode.
> - MgBr<sub>2</sub>:
> 
>   - Br<sup>–</sup> is a halide.
>   - So, Br<sub>2</sub> is produced at the anode.

> 
> - PbCl<sub>2</sub> → Pb + Cl<sub>2</sub> **[1 mark]**
> 
> State symbols are not required

From Pb<sup>2+</sup> + 2e<sup>–</sup> → Pb, 2 moles of electrons are required per mole of lead

Coulombs per mole of Pb = 2 x 96 500

Coulombs per mole of Pb = **193 000 C** **[1 mark]**

- Convert time to seconds:

*t* = 10 x 60

*t *= **600 s** **[1 mark]**

*Q* = *I* x *t*

*Q* = 1.65 x 600

*Q* = **990 C** **[1 mark]**

*n*(Pb) = $\frac{990}{193000}$

*n*(Pb) = **5.13 × 10**<sup>**-3**</sup>** mol** **[1 mark]**

mass = n x *M*<sub>r</sub>

mass = 5.13 × 10<sup>-3</sup> x 207.2

mass = **1.06 g** **[1 mark]**

Coulombs per mole of Cl<sub>2</sub> = **193 000 C** **[1 mark]**

### Q7
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The half-equation at the anode:
> 
> - Cu (s) → Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> **[1 mark]**
> 
> State symbols required
> 
> ii) Silver remains as the metal because:
> 
> - *E*<sup>θ</sup> for Ag<sup>+</sup> / Ag (+0.80 V) is more positive than *E*<sup>θ</sup> for Cu<sup>2+</sup> / Cu (+0.34 V) **[1 mark]**
> - Silver is less easily oxidised than copper **[1 mark]**
> 
> iii) Nickel at the anode:
> 
> - *E*<sup>θ</sup> for Ni<sup>2+</sup> / Ni (−0.25 V) is more negative than *E*<sup>θ</sup> for Cu<sup>2+</sup> / Cu (+0.34 V) **[1 mark]**
> - Nickel is readily oxidised and dissolves as Ni<sup>2+</sup> (aq) **[1 mark]**
> 
> iv) The half-equation at the cathode:
> 
> - Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e<sup>–</sup> → Cu (s) **[1 mark]**
> 
> State symbols required
> 
> v) Zinc is not deposited at the cathode because:
> 
> - *E*<sup>θ</sup> for Zn<sup>2+</sup> / Zn is −0.76 V (more negative than Cu<sup>2+</sup> / Cu), so Zn<sup>2+</sup> is less easily reduced than Cu<sup>2+</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The key rule: the more positive the *E*<sup>θ</sup>, the more easily the left-hand species is reduced; the more negative the *E*<sup>θ</sup>, the more easily the right-hand species is oxidised.
> 
> Ag (+0.80 V) is more positive than Cu (+0.34 V), so Ag is harder to oxidise and stays as the metal.
> 
> Ni (−0.25 V) and Zn (−0.76 V) are more negative than Cu, so they dissolve from the anode. At the cathode, Cu<sup>2+</sup> (+0.34 V) is preferentially reduced over Zn<sup>2+</sup> (−0.76 V).
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The blue colour fades because:
> 
> - Cu<sup>2+</sup> ions are removed from solution at the cathode / the concentration of Cu<sup>2+</sup> decreases **[1 mark]**
> - Zn<sup>2+</sup> / Ni<sup>2+</sup> ions replace some Cu<sup>2+</sup> in solution
> **OR**
> the rate of Cu<sup>2+</sup> removal at the cathode exceeds the rate of Cu<sup>2+</sup> replacement from the anode **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The blue colour of copper sulfate solution comes from the Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion. As electrolysis proceeds, Cu<sup>2+</sup> is reduced to Cu at the cathode. Although the anode does release Cu<sup>2+</sup>, Zn and Ni impurities also dissolve as Zn<sup>2+</sup> and Ni<sup>2+</sup>, partially replacing the Cu<sup>2+</sup>. The net effect is a gradual decrease in Cu<sup>2+</sup> concentration, so the blue colour fades.
To calculate the percentage of current wasted:

i) Calculate the moles of copper produced at the cathode:

n(Cu)  = $\frac{\mathrm{mass}}{A_{r}}$

n(Cu) = $\frac{225}{63.5}$

n(Cu) = **3.54 mol** **[1 mark]**
