### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The baseline is drawn in pencil rather than ink because:
> 
> - Pencil does not dissolve in / is not carried up the plate by the solvent **[1 mark]**
>

> **[exam-tip]**

> - Compare the *R*<sub>f</sub> value of each spot with data table / reference values to identify the compound **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> This question asks how to go from a chromatogram to an identification. State the two distances measured (compound and solvent front), write the *R*<sub>f</sub> equation, and explain that the calculated *R*<sub>f</sub> values are compared against known reference data.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> - Expose the TLC plate to UV light
> **OR**
> Place the TLC plate in an iodine vapour chamber **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Colourless compounds are not visible under normal light. Two common methods to reveal spots are UV light (if the silica contains a UV-active indicator) and iodine vapour, which temporarily stains organic compounds brown.
To calculate the *R*<sub>f</sub> values for benzaldehyde and benzyl alcohol:

- Apply the *R*<sub>f</sub> equation:

*R*<sub>f</sub> = $\frac{\mathrm{distance} \mathrm{travelled} \mathrm{by} \mathrm{compound}}{\mathrm{distance} \mathrm{travelled} \mathrm{by} \mathrm{solvent}}$

- Calculate *R*<sub>f</sub> for benzaldehyde:

*R*<sub>f</sub> = $\frac{3.2}{5.0}$

*R*<sub>f</sub> (benzaldehyde) = **0.64** **[1 mark]**

- Calculate *R*<sub>f</sub> for benzyl alcohol:

*R*<sub>f</sub> = $\frac{1.5}{5.0}$

*R*<sub>f</sub> (benzyl alcohol) = **0.30** **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A compound that is very soluble in the mobile phase has a high *R*<sub>f</sub> (close to 1); a compound that adsorbs strongly to the stationary phase has a low *R*<sub>f</sub> (close to 0).
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The maximum *R*<sub>f</sub> value cannot exceed 1 because:
> 
> - *R*<sub>f</sub> is a ratio of the distance a compound has travelled to the distance travelled by the solvent **[1 mark]**
> - An *R*<sub>f</sub> value of 1 means the compound is fully soluble in the mobile phase and does not interact with the stationary phase **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Think about what *R*<sub>f</sub> physically represents: the distance the compound travels as a fraction of the solvent front distance. Since the compound cannot travel further than the solvent, the maximum ratio is 1. An *R*<sub>f</sub> of exactly 1 would mean the compound has no interaction with the stationary phase at all.

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Butanone has a higher *R*<sub>f</sub> value than butan-2-ol because:
> 
> - Butan-2-ol is more polar than butanone
> **OR**
> Butan-2-ol contains hydrogen bonding **[1 mark]**
> - Butan-2-ol adsorbs more strongly to the silica gel / stationary phase
> **OR**
> Butanone is more soluble in the mobile phase / solvent **[1 mark]**
> - Therefore, butanone travels further on the TLC plate **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Butan-2-ol contains an -OH group which enables hydrogen bonding, making it more polar than butanone. More polar compounds adsorb more strongly to the polar silica gel stationary phase and travel less far up the plate, giving a lower *R*<sub>f</sub> value.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The stationary and mobile phases in this chromatography experiment are:
> 
> - Stationary phase = silica gel **[1 mark]**
> - Mobile phase = hexane **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In thin-layer chromatography, the stationary phase is a layer of silica gel (SiO<sub>2</sub>) or alumina (Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) coated onto a plate. The mobile phase is the liquid solvent that moves up the plate by capillary action.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The effect on the *R*<sub>f</sub> value of benzyl alcohol is:
> 
> - The *R*<sub>f</sub> value would increase **[1 mark]**
> - Benzyl alcohol is more soluble in a polar solvent / has a greater affinity for the polar mobile phase, so it travels further up the plate **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Benzyl alcohol contains an -OH group and is relatively polar. A polar solvent increases the solubility of polar compounds in the mobile phase, reducing their interaction with the stationary phase and causing them to travel further, increasing their *R*<sub>f</sub> value.

### Q5
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two phases of thin-layer chromatography and one example of a chemical used for each are:
> 
> - The mobile phase **[1 mark]**
> - Specific named solvent, e.g. water / ethanol / hexane
> **OR**
> General solvent, e.g. alkane / alcohol **[1 mark]**
> - The stationary phase **[1 mark]**
> - Silica / silicon dioxide / SiO<sub>2</sub>
> **OR**
> Alumina / aluminium oxide / Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The mobile phase moves during TLC, carrying the sample up the plate. The stationary phase stays fixed.
> 
> Common examples of polar mobile phases are water and alcohols. Common examples of non-polar mobile phases are alkanes such as hexane.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two factors that determine the distance travelled by a compound in thin-layer chromatography are:
> 
> - The solubility of the compound in the mobile phase **[1 mark]**
> - The adsorption of the compound by the stationary phase **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A compound that is very soluble in the mobile phase is carried further up the plate. A compound that adsorbs strongly to the stationary phase is held back and travels a shorter distance.
> 
> For TLC using a solid stationary phase such as silica, the correct term for the interaction with the stationary phase is adsorption.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The equation used to calculate the retention factor, *R*<sub>f</sub>, of a compound is:
> 
> - *R*<sub>f</sub> = $\frac{\text{distance travelled by compound}}{\text{distance travelled by solvent front}}$ **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> This equation is not often specifically asked for in a question. You are more likely to be asked to apply it to calculate an *R*<sub>f</sub> value from a chromatogram.

**[1 mark]**

- Calculate the *R*<sub>f</sub> value:
