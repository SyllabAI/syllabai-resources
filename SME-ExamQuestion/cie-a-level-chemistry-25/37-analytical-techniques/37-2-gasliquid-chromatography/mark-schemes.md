# Mark Schemes — Gas/Liquid Chromatography
**37. Analytical Techniques** · Cambridge (CIE) A-LEVEL chemistry (9701)


## Structured Questions (5 questions)


### Q1
> **[mark-scheme]**
> How each method contributes to the analysis:
> 
> - Gas chromatography separates the ester mixture into its component compounds **[1 mark]**
> - Mass spectrometry ionises each separated compound and produces a mass spectrum / fragmentation pattern **[1 mark]**
> - The fragmentation pattern is compared to a database of known compounds for identification **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> This question is worth 3 marks but only mentions 2 methods, which means that each method needs a little extra detail to earn all 3 marks.
> 
> GC-MS is used to separate and identify volatile compounds. The GC stage separates the mixture; the MS stage identifies each component. The key final step is database matching, which completes the identification.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Retention time is:
> 
> - The time between injection and detection / emergence of a component
> **OR**
> the time that each component remains in the column **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Defining retention time is not a common exam question, but it is possible. Two equivalent definitions are accepted:
> 
> - The time between injection and detection of a component
> - The time that each component remains in the column
> 
> The retention time depends on the compound’s relative affinity for the mobile gas phase versus the stationary phase.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The mixture of esters can be separated by gas chromatography because:
> 
> - The esters have different retention times
> **OR**
> The esters appear at different times in the chromatogram **[1 mark]**
> - The esters have different affinities for the mobile and stationary phases
> **OR**
> The esters have different solubilities in the stationary phase **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The different structures of methyl propanoate and propyl methanoate mean they interact differently with the stationary phase. One ester partitions more into the stationary phase (spending more time there, moving more slowly); the other moves more readily with the mobile gas phase.
> 
> Although these esters are structural isomers with similar properties, the subtle differences in their structures give them slightly different retention times, allowing separation.

### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The compound with the greatest affinity for the stationary phase is:
> 
> - Compound **C**
> **AND**
> Compound **C** has the greatest retention time **[1 mark]**
> - The longer the retention time, the greater the affinity for the stationary phase **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Questions based on gas chromatograms are often linked to the affinity of a compound for the stationary or mobile phase. Examiners may use the terms stationary phase, solid phase, or liquid phase for the GLC stationary phase.
> 
> Longer retention times mean the compound spends more time in the column. This is because it has a greater affinity for the stationary phase (it is more soluble in the stationary phase liquid).
> 
> Gas chromatograms are often misinterpreted, so make sure you are reading retention time (x-axis) and not peak height.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The most abundant compound in the sample:
> 
> - Compound **B**
> **AND**
> it has the largest peak area / greatest area under the peak **[1 mark]**
> 
> "Highest peak" or "tallest peak" is not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The area under the peak is proportional to the abundance of that compound in the mixture, not the height of the peak. A tall, narrow peak could represent less compound than a shorter, broader peak.
> 
> Always use peak area to deduce relative abundance, not peak height alone.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Gas chromatography could be used to identify the tanker as the source by:
> 
> - Carrying out gas chromatography on a sample of oil from the tanker and a sample of oil from the beach **[1 mark]**
> - Comparing the two chromatograms to determine whether the retention times / peak profiles match **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Strip away the narrative context and focus on the core task: use GC to check whether two samples came from the same source.
> 
> The answer follows the same two steps:
> 
> 1. Run GC on both samples
> 2. Compare the chromatograms. If they match, the samples are from the same source.

### Q3
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The three types of chemical that gas chromatography is used for are:
> 
> - Gases **[1 mark]**
> - Volatile liquids **[1 mark]**
> - Solids in their vapour form **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Gas chromatography (also called gas-liquid chromatography) is used for identifying and separating chemicals that are volatile, meaning they can exist as, or be converted to, a gas under the column conditions.
> 
> All three accepted answers share the key property of volatility: they can all be vaporised and carried through the column by the gas mobile phase.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The stationary phase used in gas-liquid chromatography is:
> 
> - A non-volatile / high boiling point liquid **[1 mark]**
> - (Coated) on an inert solid support **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The stationary phase must be a liquid so that components can dissolve into (partition into) it from the gas mobile phase.
> 
> It must be non-volatile (have a high boiling point) so it does not evaporate during the analysis and contaminate the carrier gas.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The mobile phase in gas chromatography is:
> 
> - An inert (carrier) gas **[1 mark]**
> 
> One suitable example:
> 
> Any **one **from:
> 
> - Helium / He
> - Nitrogen / N<sub>2</sub>
> - Argon / Ar
> 
> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The mobile phase must be an inert gas so that it does not react with any of the components being separated or with the stationary phase.
> 
> The alternative name for gas chromatography is gas-liquid chromatography. This tells you directly that the mobile phase is a gas and the stationary phase is a liquid.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Answers to the questions on retention time in gas chromatography:
> 
> i) The definition of retention time is:
> 
> - The time taken for a component to travel through the column
> **OR**
> The time taken from injection to detection / emergence of the sample **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Three factors that retention time depends upon are:
> 
> Any **three** from (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - The attraction between the sample and the stationary phase
> - The attraction between the sample / component and the mobile phase
> - The polarity of the sample / component
> - The volatility of the sample / component
> - The chemical structure of the sample / component
> 
> **[3 marks]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part (i): the key word in retention time is retained. The retention time is the time a component spends in the column, measured from injection to detection.
> 
> For part (ii): all the listed factors influence how strongly the component partitions into the stationary phase relative to the gas mobile phase. A component with a greater affinity for the stationary phase (due to its polarity, chemical structure, or low volatility) travels more slowly through the column and has a longer retention time.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The relative size or area under a peak on a gas chromatogram tells you:
> 
> - The relative concentration / amount / abundance of that component in the mixture **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The area under the peak (not just the peak height) is proportional to the abundance of that component. A tall, narrow peak and a shorter, broader peak could represent the same amount of a compound. Always refer to peak area, not peak height, when describing abundance.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Using Fig. 1.1 to answer each part:
> 
> i) The compound with the lowest retention time is:
> 
> - **A** **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) The least polar compound is:
> 
> - **A** **[1 mark]**
> 
> iii) The compound with the greatest interaction with the stationary phase is:
> 
> - **C** **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part (i): read the retention time directly from the x-axis. Compound A elutes first and therefore has the lowest retention time.
> 
> For part (ii): the stationary phase is polar, so it attracts polar compounds more strongly, slowing their movement through the column. Compound A has the shortest retention time, meaning it interacts least with the polar stationary phase. It is therefore the least polar compound.
> 
> Parts (i) and (ii) are linked: when a polar stationary phase is used, the compound with the shortest retention time is always the least polar.
> 
> For part (iii): compound C has the longest retention time. It spends the most time in the column, meaning it has the greatest solubility in / interaction with the stationary phase.

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The difference between the mobile phases is:
> 
> - The mobile phase in column chromatography is a liquid
> **AND**
> The mobile phase in gas-liquid chromatography is a gas **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> "Mobile phase" is another term for the eluent, the substance that carries the components through the system.
> 
> In column chromatography (which uses the same principles as TLC), the mobile phase is a liquid solvent. In gas-liquid chromatography, the mobile phase is replaced by an inert carrier gas such as helium, argon, or nitrogen.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The compound with the greatest affinity for the stationary phase is:
> 
> - Compound **C**
> **AND**
> It has the greatest retention time **[1 mark]**
> - A longer retention time indicates the compound spends more time dissolved in / partitioned into the stationary phase, so it has a greater affinity for it **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In GLC, a longer retention time means the component is slowed down by the stationary phase. This occurs because it has a greater affinity for (is more soluble in) the stationary phase relative to the gas mobile phase.
> 
> "Affinity for the stationary phase", "interaction with the stationary phase", and "solubility in the stationary phase" are used interchangeably in CIE mark schemes.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The most abundant compound in the mixture is:
> 
> - Compound **B**
> **AND**
> It has the largest peak area / greatest area under the peak **[1 mark]**
> 
> "Highest peak" or "tallest peak" is not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> In gas chromatography, the area under the peak is proportional to the abundance of that component in the mixture. Peak height alone is not sufficient as a tall, narrow peak could represent less compound than a shorter, broader peak.
> 
> Always refer to peak area when identifying the most abundant compound.

### Q5
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) An appropriate mobile phase:
> 
> Any **one **from:
> 
> - Helium / He
> - Nitrogen / N<sub>2</sub>
> - Argon / Ar
> 
> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Number of compounds present:
> 
> - 4 / four **[1 mark]**
> 
> How to identify the most polar compound:
> 
> - The most polar compound has the longest retention time
> **OR**
> the most polar compound has a retention time of 11.1 minutes **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Name a specific inert gas, do not just say "an inert gas".
> 
> A polar stationary phase will interact more strongly with a polar molecule. This slows the polar compound's passage through the column, giving it a longer retention time.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) Molar mass and molecular formula:
> 
> - Molar mass = 86 (g mol<sup>-1</sup>) **[1 mark]**
> - Molecular formula = C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>14</sub> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Fragments responsible for the signals:
> 
> - *m/e* = 29 = C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub><sup>+</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - *m/e* = 43 = C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>7</sub><sup>+</sup> **[1 mark]**
> - *m/e* = 57 = C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>9</sub><sup>+</sup> **[1 mark]**

iii) Skeletal formula:

![Skeletal formula of hexane showing a straight chain of 6 carbon atoms](assets/006-skeletal-formula-of-hexane-showing-a-straight-ch.png)

**[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for the skeletal formula of hexane showing a straight chain of 6 carbon atoms

> **[exam-tip]**
> The molecular ion peak (*m/e* = 86) equals the molar mass, assuming singly charged ions only.
> 
> The question tells you that the molecules are hydrocarbons and that bromine water does not decolourise — so the molecules are saturated (only single bonds). Using these clues with *m/e* = 86:
> 
> - 6 carbons (72) + 14 hydrogens (14) = 86 g mol<sup>-1</sup>, consistent with the bromine water test (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>14</sub>)
> - 7 carbons would require only 2 hydrogens (C<sub>7</sub>H<sub>2</sub>), which is unrealistic
> 
> All fragments carry a single positive charge and contain only carbon and hydrogen.
> 
> The peak at *m/e* = 57 corresponds to C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>9</sub><sup>+</sup>, which is a four-carbon fragment. This is only possible for straight-chain hexane; a branched isomer such as 2,3-dimethylbutane could not readily form this fragment.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The isotope responsible for the [M+1] peak:
> 
> - Carbon-13 / <sup>13</sup>C **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Types of molecules for which the [M+1] peak becomes more pronounced:
> 
> - Molecules with longer carbon chains / more carbon atoms **[1 mark]**
> 
> Explanation:
> 
> - As the number of carbon atoms in the molecule increases
> **AND**
> The probability that one of them is <sup>13</sup>C increases **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The molecular ion peak assumes all carbon atoms are <sup>12</sup>C (the most abundant isotope). The [M+1] peak arises because <sup>13</sup>C has a natural abundance of approximately 1.1%.
> 
> As the number of carbon atoms in a molecule increases, the probability that at least one is <sup>13</sup>C increases. So, the [M+1] peak becomes relatively larger for compounds with more carbons.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) One way in which the mass spectrum of 2,3-dimethylbutane differs:
> 
> Any **one** from:
> 
> - No peak / minimal peak at *m/e* = 57 (C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>9</sub><sup>+</sup> fragment cannot readily form from 2,3-dimethylbutane)
> - More intense peak at *m/e* = 71 (C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>11</sub><sup>+</sup> from loss of CH<sub>3</sub>)
> 
> **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) An alternative spectroscopic technique:
> 
> - <sup>13</sup>C NMR
> **OR**
> <sup>1</sup>H NMR **[1 mark]**
> 
> iii) The isomer with the longest retention time:
> 
> - Hexane (the straight-chain isomer from part b) **[1 mark]**
> - Hexane is straight-chain with a greater surface area than 2,3-dimethylbutane
> **AND**
> Hexane has stronger London dispersion forces, a higher boiling point (69 °C vs 58 °C), is less volatile, and spends more time partitioned into the stationary phase **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For part i:
> 
> The branching in 2,3-dimethylbutane prevents the formation of C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>9</sub><sup>+</sup> (m/e = 57). Loss of a methyl group (CH<sub>3</sub>) is easy due to the four equivalent methyl groups, giving a more intense peak at m/e = 71 (C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>11</sub><sup>+</sup>).
> 
> For part iii:
> 
> In GLC, separation is by partitioning, where compounds dissolve into and out of the liquid stationary phase. Do not confuse this with adsorption chromatography. Hexane (straight-chain) has a greater surface area, stronger London dispersion forces, and a higher boiling point (69 °C) than 2,3-dimethylbutane (58 °C). Hexane is therefore less volatile and spends more time dissolved in the stationary phase, giving it the longer retention time.
