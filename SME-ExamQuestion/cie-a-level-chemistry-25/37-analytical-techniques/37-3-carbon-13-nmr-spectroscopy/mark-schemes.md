### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Explaining why the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum of benzene contains only one peak:
> 
> - All six carbon atoms are in the same chemical environment **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The key to this question is symmetry. Benzene has a perfect hexagonal structure, so every carbon atom has exactly the same bonding environment. In <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectroscopy, carbon atoms in the same chemical environment absorb at the same frequency and produce a single combined peak.
> 
> Any molecule where all carbons are symmetrically equivalent will show only one <sup>13</sup>C peak. Benzene is the classic example, but watch for other symmetric molecules such as cyclohexane.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The number of peaks in the low-resolution <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum of methylbenzene is:
> 
> - 4 / four **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> To count the number of peaks in a low-resolution <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum, identify how many groups of protons are in different chemical environments. Two protons are in the same environment if they occupy identical positions within the molecule by symmetry.
> 
> For methylbenzene, there are four distinct proton environments:
> 
> - The CH<sub>3</sub> group: 3 protons
> - The two ring C–H groups adjacent to the methyl-bearing carbon: 2 protons
> - The two ring C–H groups one further position around the ring: 2 protons
> - The ring C–H group directly opposite the methyl group: 1 proton
> 
> The two pairs of ring protons are each equivalent by symmetry, so each pair counts as one environment. The key to any benzene derivative NMR question is spotting the symmetry elements of the molecule.

> 
> 1 mark for 1,4-dimethylbenzene: <sup>1</sup>H = 2 **AND** <sup>13</sup>C = 3

> **[exam-tip]**
> To count NMR environments in substituted benzenes, draw each structure and identify all lines of symmetry. Carbons or hydrogens that are mirror images of each other through a line of symmetry are equivalent and produce one combined peak.
> 
> For the three dimethylbenzene isomers:
> 
> - 1,2-dimethylbenzene has one plane of symmetry bisecting the bond between the two methyl-bearing carbons. This gives 4 <sup>13</sup>C environments (the two equivalent methyl carbons plus three distinct ring environments) and 3 <sup>1</sup>H environments.
> - 1,3-dimethylbenzene has one plane of symmetry through the carbon positioned between the two methyl groups. This gives 5 <sup>13</sup>C environments and 4 <sup>1</sup>H environments.
> - 1,4-dimethylbenzene is the most symmetric, with two planes of symmetry. All four ring C–H groups are equivalent, and the two CH<sub>3</sub> groups are equivalent. This gives 3 <sup>13</sup>C environments and 2 <sup>1</sup>H environments.
> 
> As a general rule, the more symmetric the molecule, the fewer NMR peaks it produces.

>

> - TMS produces a single, sharp peak because all 12 protons (and all four carbons) are equivalent by symmetry.
> - Its peak appears at 0 ppm, well to the right of almost all organic compound peaks, so there is no overlap with the sample.
> - TMS is volatile, so it can be easily removed from the sample after analysis.
> - TMS is inert and does not react with the sample.
>

> **[mark-scheme]**
> The number of peaks in the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum of 1,3-dichlorobenzene is:
> 
> - 4 / four **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> To determine the number of <sup>13</sup>C environments in 1,3-dichlorobenzene, draw the structure and look for a vertical plane of symmetry. The plane passes through the two chlorine-bearing carbons (C1 and C3) and the carbon directly opposite (C5 or C2, depending on numbering).
> 
> With this symmetry:
> 
> - C1 and C3 are equivalent (both carry Cl)
> - C2 (between the two Cl groups) is unique
> - C4 and C6 are equivalent
> - C5 (directly opposite C2) is unique
> 
> This gives four distinct <sup>13</sup>C environments, so four peaks.
> 
> A common mistake is to count all six carbons separately and give 6 peaks. Always look for the symmetry axis before counting.
