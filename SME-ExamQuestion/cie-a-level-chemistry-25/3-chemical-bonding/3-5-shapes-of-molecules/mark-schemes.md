### Q5

### Q1
> **[mark-scheme]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Period 2 elements such as carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen cannot accommodate more than 8 electrons in their outer shell because the second shell contains only s and p subshells.
> 
> Sulfur is in Period 3. The third shell contains 3s, 3p, and 3d subshells. The 3d orbitals are empty in ground-state sulfur and are energetically accessible for bonding, allowing sulfur to form 6 bonds in SF<sub>6</sub> and accommodate 12 electrons in its outer shell.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The shape of a molecule of SF<sub>6</sub>:
> 
> - Octahedral **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In SF<sub>6</sub>, sulfur forms 6 bonds with 6 fluorine atoms and has no lone pairs. Six bonding domains with no lone pairs gives an octahedral arrangement.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The F-S-F bond angles in SF<sub>6</sub>:
> 
> - 90° **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In an octahedral molecule, the six bonding domains point towards the vertices of a regular octahedron. All adjacent bond angles are 90°. Because there are no lone pairs to distort the geometry, all F-S-F angles in SF<sub>6</sub> are exactly 90°.
> 
> Do not confuse with trigonal bipyramidal molecules such as PF<sub>5</sub>, which have two different bond angles: 90° (axial-equatorial) and 120° (equatorial-equatorial).
> 
> The molecules listed in the CIE 9701 syllabus with their shapes and bond angles are:
> 
> - BF<sub>3</sub>: trigonal planar, 120°
> - CO<sub>2</sub>: linear, 180°
> - CH<sub>4</sub>: tetrahedral, 109.5°
> - NH<sub>3</sub>: pyramidal, 107°
> - H<sub>2</sub>O: non-linear, 104.5°
> - SF<sub>6</sub>: octahedral, 90°
> - PF<sub>5</sub>: trigonal bipyramidal, 90° and 120°
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The polarity of the S-F bond:
> 
> - The S-F bond is polar **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) The polarity of SF<sub>6</sub>:
> 
> - SF<sub>6</sub> is non-polar / has no net dipole moment **[1 mark]**
> - The six polar S-F bonds are arranged symmetrically / the bond dipoles cancel **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A bond is polar when there is a significant electronegativity difference between the bonded atoms, generally 0.5 or more on the Pauling scale. The electronegativity difference for S-F is 3.98 - 2.58 = 1.40, so each S-F bond is strongly polar, with fluorine as δ- and sulfur as δ+.
> 
> A molecule can contain polar bonds but still be non-polar overall if the bond dipoles cancel due to symmetry. In SF<sub>6</sub>, the octahedral geometry means the six S-F bond dipoles point outward along three perpendicular axes in opposing pairs. The dipoles cancel exactly, giving no net dipole.

### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The basic principles of VSEPR theory:
> 
> - Electron pairs / electron domains in the outer shell of the central atom repel each other **[1 mark]**
> - Electron pairs arrange as far apart as possible to minimise repulsion **[1 mark]**
> - Lone pair–lone pair repulsion > lone pair–bonding pair repulsion > bonding pair–bonding pair repulsion **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> VSEPR theory is based on three key ideas:
> 
> 1. Electron pairs in the outer shell are negatively charged and repel each other.
> 2. To minimise repulsion, electron pairs arrange as far apart as possible in space. The arrangement determines the shape.
> 3. Not all electron pairs repel equally. Lone pairs are concentrated closer to the central atom and exert more repulsion than bonding pairs.
> 
> The repulsion hierarchy explains why molecules with lone pairs have bond angles smaller than the ideal value. For example, H<sub>2</sub>O has 2 lone pairs and a H-O-H angle of 104.5°, compared to the tetrahedral ideal of 109.5°.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The shape and F-O-F bond angle of OF<sub>2</sub>:
> 
> - Non-linear / V-shaped / bent **[1 mark]**
> - F-O-F bond angle in the range 97° to 109° **[1 mark]**
> - Oxygen has 4 electron domains: 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs
> **OR**
> Oxygen has a tetrahedral arrangement of electron pairs **[1 mark]**
> 
> Lone pair–bonding pair repulsion is greater than bonding pair–bonding pair repulsion, reducing the F-O-F bond angle below 109.5°.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Oxygen in OF<sub>2</sub> has 6 outer-shell electrons: 2 used in the two O-F bonds and 4 remaining as 2 lone pairs. Four electron domains give a tetrahedral electron geometry, but the shape is named by bonding domains only: 2 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs = non-linear / V-shaped.
> 
> Fluorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, which slightly increases the F-O-F bond angle compared to H<sub>2</sub>O (104.5°). A value in the range 97° to 109° is accepted.
> 
> The rough rule of thumb is that each lone pair reduces the bond angle by approximately 2.5° from the ideal tetrahedral value of 109.5°.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The shape and bond angle in BF<sub>3</sub>:
> 
> - Trigonal planar **[1 mark]**
> - 120° **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) The shape and bond angle in NBr<sub>3</sub>:
> 
> - Trigonal pyramidal **[1 mark]**
> - 107° **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> For BF<sub>3</sub>: boron (Group 3) has 3 outer-shell electrons, all used in bonding. BF<sub>3</sub> has 3 bonding pairs and no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°.
> 
> For NBr<sub>3</sub>: nitrogen (Group 5) has 5 outer-shell electrons. Three are used in bonding, leaving 1 lone pair. Four electron domains (3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair): trigonal pyramidal, approximately 107°.
> 
> The identity of the substituent (F vs Br) does not change the shape. What matters is the electron domain count around the central atom.

### Q3
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The bond angle in BH<sub>3</sub> is 120° because:
> 
> - BH<sub>3</sub> has 3 bonding pairs of electrons and no lone pairs **[1 mark]**
> - The bonding pairs repel each other equally and arrange as far apart as possible to minimise repulsion **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> VSEPR theory states that electron pairs in the outer shell repel each other and arrange to minimise repulsion. Boron (Group 3) has 3 outer-shell electrons, all used to form the 3 B-H bonds. With 3 bonding pairs and no lone pairs, all repulsions are equal.
> 
> In a planar arrangement, equal repulsion between 3 pairs gives 360° / 3 = 120° bond angles. BH<sub>3</sub> is trigonal planar.
> 
> Note: bond angles are 120° due to repulsion between electron pairs, not between hydrogen atoms.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The H-N-H bond angle in NH<sub>3</sub>:
> 
> - 107° **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> NH<sub>3</sub> has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair on nitrogen = 4 electron domains. Four electron domains give approximately 109.5° (tetrahedral), but the lone pair exerts greater repulsion than a bonding pair, compressing the H-N-H angles to approximately 107°.
> 
> The H-N-H bond angle in NH<sub>3</sub> (107°) is smaller than the H-B-H angle in BH<sub>3</sub> (120°) because NH<sub>3</sub> has a lone pair on nitrogen whereas BH<sub>3</sub> does not.

> The bond angle in NH<sub>3</sub> is smaller than in BH<sub>3</sub> because:
> 
> - NH<sub>3</sub> has a lone pair of electrons on the central nitrogen atom, whereas BH<sub>3</sub> has no lone pairs **[1 mark]**
> - Lone pair–bonding pair repulsion is greater than bonding pair–bonding pair repulsion, reducing the H-N-H bond angle **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The order of electron pair repulsion strength is:
> 
> - lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair
> 
> In BH<sub>3</sub>, all three bonding pairs repel equally, giving 120°. In NH<sub>3</sub>, the lone pair exerts extra repulsion on the bonding pairs, compressing them and reducing the H-N-H angle from the ideal tetrahedral value (109.5°) to approximately 107°.

> **[exam-tip]**
> In the N<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub><sup>2+</sup> cation, each nitrogen has accepted a proton, similar to how ammonia (NH<sub>3</sub>) becomes ammonium (NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>). Each nitrogen now has 4 bonding pairs and no lone pairs.

> Four bonding domains with no lone pairs gives a tetrahedral arrangement with bond angles of 109.5°.
> 
> The structure is analogous to ethane: each carbon in ethane has 4 bonding pairs, no lone pairs, and bond angles of 109.5°.
