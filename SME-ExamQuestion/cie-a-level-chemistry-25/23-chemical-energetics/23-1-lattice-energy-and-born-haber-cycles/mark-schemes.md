> **[exam-tip]**
> Learn all thermodynamic definitions for CIE IAL Chemistry. Lattice energy is always exothermic (negative), as forming ionic bonds from gaseous ions releases energy.

> 
> - Cl (g) + e<sup>-</sup> → Cl<sup>-</sup> (g) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> ½Cl<sub>2</sub> (g) + e<sup>-</sup> → Cl<sup>-</sup> (g) is not an acceptable answer because this represents both the enthalpy of atomisation and the electron affinity, not just the electron affinity.
To calculate the first electron affinity of chlorine:

Δ*H*<sub>f</sub> = Δ*H*<sub>at</sub>(Mg) + IE<sub>1</sub>(Mg) + IE<sub>2</sub>(Mg) + 2Δ*H*<sub>at</sub>(Cl) + 2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>) + Δ*H*<sub>latt</sub>(MgCl<sub>2</sub>)

–641 = 148 + 738 + 1450 + 2(121) + 2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>) + (–2526) **[1 mark]**

- Simplify:

–641 = 52 + 2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>) **[1 mark]**

2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>) = –693 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>

- Calculate the result:

EA<sub>Cl</sub> = **–346.5 kJ mol**<sup>**-1**</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**

> **OR**
> 1 mark for a correctly drawn and labelled Born-Haber cycle
> 
> 1 mark for correct simplification: –641 = 52 + 2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>)
> **OR**
> 2(EA<sub>Cl</sub>) = –693 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>
> 
> 1 mark for EA<sub>Cl</sub> = –346.5 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>

> **[exam-tip]**
> When calculating using a Born-Haber cycle, always check:
> 
> - Correct values from the data table
> - Lattice energy is negative (energy released on forming the solid)
> - Correct multipliers for the formula (MgCl<sub>2</sub> contains 2 Cl, so use 2 x EA<sub>Cl</sub> and 2 x Δ*H*<sub>at</sub>(Cl))
> 
> A very common error is forgetting the stoichiometry of the chlorine. Since the formula is MgCl<sub>2</sub>, two chloride ions are formed. Therefore both the enthalpy of atomisation of chlorine and the electron affinity must be multiplied by 2.

>

> - The charge density of M<sup>2+</sup> decreases as a result **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**

>

> - → lower charge density
> - → less polarisation of CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup>
> - → C–O bonds less weakened
> - → harder to decompose
> - → greater thermal stability.
> 
> When explaining polarisation, focus on the carbonate anion and use the word "polarisation" or "distortion" explicitly. Restating that thermal stability increases does not explain the underlying reason.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> The definition of lattice energy:
> 
> - Formation of 1 mole of an ionic lattice from its constituent gaseous ions **[1 mark]**
> - Under standard conditions **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> There are many thermodynamic definitions you need to know for CIE IAL Chemistry. They are typically worth 2 or 3 marks and are worth memorising.
The missing species are:

- (Step **E**) Ca<sup>2+</sup>(g) + O (g) + 2e<sup>-</sup> **[1 mark]**
- (Step **G**) Ca<sup>2+</sup>(g) + O<sup>2-</sup>(g) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Step E in the Born-Haber cycle begins with Ca<sup>+</sup>(g) + O (g) + 2e<sup>-</sup>. The calcium ion needs to lose another electron to form a 2+ ion (second ionisation energy), so Ca<sup>2+</sup>(g) + O (g) + 2e<sup>-</sup> is the missing species.
> 
> Step G represents the second electron affinity of oxygen. This is the enthalpy change when one mole of O<sup>-</sup>(g) ions forms one mole of O<sup>2-</sup>(g) ions. As shown in the Born-Haber cycle, Ca<sup>2+</sup>(g) + O<sup>-</sup>(g) + e<sup>-</sup> becomes Ca<sup>2+</sup>(g) + O<sup>2-</sup>(g).
> 
> Examiners often ask about the second electron affinity of oxygen, so make sure you know it well.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The enthalpy changes represented by each step are:
> 
> - (**B**) enthalpy change of atomisation (of calcium) **[1 mark]**
> - (**D**) first ionisation energy (of calcium) **[1 mark]**
> - (**F**) first electron affinity (of oxygen) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The enthalpy change of atomisation is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from an element in its standard state, for example Ca (s) → Ca (g).
> 
> The first ionisation energy is the enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is removed from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous ions with a single positive charge, for example Ca (g) → Ca<sup>+</sup>(g).
> 
> The first electron affinity is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms gains one mole of electrons to form one mole of gaseous ions with a single negative charge, for example O (g) → O<sup>-</sup>(g).
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The enthalpy change of atomisation is always endothermic because:
> 
> - Energy must be absorbed to break the bonds in the element **[1 mark]**
> - No bond forming occurs in this process, so no energy is released **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The enthalpy change of atomisation is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from an element in its standard state, for example O<sub>2</sub> (g) → 2O (g).
> 
> This process is always endothermic because bond breaking requires energy input, and no bond forming occurs to release energy.
> 
> Remember that endothermic reactions always have positive enthalpy changes and exothermic reactions have negative enthalpy changes.

>

> **[exam-tip]**

>

To calculate the second ionisation energy of magnesium:

- Write the Born-Haber equation relating all enthalpy changes:

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>f</sub> = Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub>(Mg) + Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie1</sub>(Mg) + Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie2</sub>(Mg) + Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub>(O) + E<sub>ea1</sub>(O) + E<sub>ea2</sub>(O) + Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub>(MgO) **[1 mark]**

- Rearrange and substitute values:

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie2</sub>(Mg) = –552 – 148 – 736 – 248 + 141 – 798 + 3791 **[1 mark]**

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie2</sub>(Mg) = **+1450 kJ mol**<sup>**–1**</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Rearrange the Born-Haber equation to make Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie2</sub>(Mg) the subject before substituting values. When a term moves to the other side of the equation, its sign changes.
> 
> The second ionisation energy must always be positive (endothermic), as energy is always required to remove an electron from an already positively charged ion.
> 
> Show your working clearly — if you make an arithmetic error you can still gain marks for a correct method.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> There is no enthalpy of solution data for calcium oxide because:
> 
> - Calcium oxide reacts with water to produce OH<sup>-</sup> ions in solution **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The reaction of calcium oxide with water is:
> 
> CaO (s) + H<sub>2</sub>O (l) → Ca(OH)<sub>2</sub> (aq)
> 
> This is a chemical reaction rather than a simple dissolution into ions, so the enthalpy of solution definition does not apply. The enthalpy of solution is the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound dissolves in excess water to give its constituent ions at infinite dilution.

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The lattice energy of calcium chloride is shown by the equation:
> 
> - Ca<sup>2+</sup> (g) + 2Cl<sup>-</sup> (g) → CaCl<sub>2</sub> (s) **[1 mark]**
> 
> State symbols must be correct to score the mark.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Lattice energy is the enthalpy change when 1 mole of ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions under standard conditions. The equation must show 1 mole of CaCl<sub>2</sub> being formed.
> 
> Don't forget to balance the chloride ions as 2 Cl<sup>-</sup> are needed for 1 Ca<sup>2+</sup>.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The lattice energies of calcium fluoride and calcium sulfide compared to calcium chloride:
> 
> - Both have larger lattice energies (more exothermic) than CaCl<sub>2</sub>
> **OR**
> Both lattice energies are more negative than that of CaCl<sub>2</sub> **[1 mark]**
> - F<sup>-</sup> is smaller than Cl<sup>-</sup>, so the Ca<sup>2+</sup>–F<sup>-</sup> distance is shorter and the electrostatic attraction is stronger (for CaF<sub>2</sub>) **[1 mark]**
> - S<sup>2-</sup> has a higher charge than Cl<sup>-</sup>, so the electrostatic attraction between Ca<sup>2+</sup> and S<sup>2-</sup> is greater (for CaS) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two factors affect the magnitude of lattice energy:
> 
> Charge on the ions:
> 
> - Higher charge
> - → stronger electrostatic attraction
> - → more exothermic lattice energy
> 
> Size of the ions:
> 
> - Smaller ion
> - → shorter inter-ionic distance
> - → stronger attraction
> - → more exothermic lattice energy
> 
> For CaF<sub>2</sub> vs CaCl<sub>2</sub>: same charge, but F<sup>-</sup> is smaller than Cl<sup>-</sup>, so the Ca<sup>2+</sup>–F<sup>-</sup> distance is shorter and the lattice energy is more exothermic.
> 
> For CaS vs CaCl<sub>2</sub>: S<sup>2-</sup> has a 2- charge compared to 1- for Cl<sup>-</sup>, giving stronger electrostatic attraction and a more exothermic lattice energy.
To calculate the lattice energy of CaCl<sub>2</sub>:

- Identify the relevant values and apply correct multipliers (2 Cl atoms required, so 2 × E<sub>ea</sub>(Cl)):

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub>(Ca) = +178; IE<sub>1</sub>(Ca) = +590; IE<sub>2</sub>(Ca) = +1150; Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub>(Cl<sub>2</sub>) = +244; 2 x E<sub>ea</sub>(Cl) = 2 x (–349) = –698 **[1 mark]**

- Apply Hess's Law:

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub>(CaCl<sub>2</sub>) = –796 – 178 – 590 – 1150 – 244 – (–698) **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the result:

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub>(CaCl<sub>2</sub>) = **–2260 kJ mol**<sup>**-1**</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> To calculate lattice energy, rearrange the Hess equation: Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub> = Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>f</sub> minus all other enthalpy changes in the cycle.
> 
> Be careful with the sign of the electron affinity. The electron affinity of Cl is –349 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup> (exothermic). When you subtract E<sub>ea</sub> from the rearranged Hess equation, you get: –(–349) = +349 per mole of Cl. For 2 moles of Cl, this contributes +698 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>.
> 
> Lattice energy is always exothermic (negative), as forming ionic bonds releases energy. If your answer is positive, check the signs in your calculation.

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub> (NaCl) = –411 – 108 – 121 – (–349) – 496 **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the result:

Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub> (NaCl) = **–787 kJ mol**<sup>**-1**</sup> **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Allow 1 mark for directly writing the rearranged equation: Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>latt</sub> = Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>f</sub> – Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub> (Na) – Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>at</sub> (Cl) – E<sub>ea</sub> (Cl) – Δ*H*<sup>θ</sup><sub>ie</sub> (Na)

> **[exam-tip]**
> When calculating lattice energy using a Born-Haber cycle, remember that the direct route (enthalpy of formation) must equal the indirect route (sum of all other steps). This is Hess's Law.
> 
> Draw the cycle carefully and make sure you account for the sign of each enthalpy change when substituting values.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The definition of first electron affinity:
> 
> - The enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is gained by one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous ions with a single negative charge **[1 mark]**
> - Under standard conditions **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Learning thermodynamic definitions is important for CIE IAL Chemistry. You should also be able to represent the first electron affinity with an equation, for example O (g) + e<sup>-</sup> → O<sup>-</sup>(g).
> 
> Note that the first electron affinity can be exothermic or endothermic depending on the element. For most elements in Group 16 and 17, the first electron affinity is exothermic because energy is released when an electron is attracted to the neutral atom.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The arrows point in different directions because:
> 
> - The first electron affinity of oxygen is exothermic (arrow points downwards) **[1 mark]**
> - The second electron affinity of oxygen is endothermic (arrow points upwards) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In a Born-Haber cycle, an arrow pointing downwards represents an exothermic process (negative enthalpy change), while an arrow pointing upwards represents an endothermic process (positive enthalpy change).
> 
> The first electron affinity: O (g) + e<sup>-</sup> → O<sup>-</sup>(g). This is exothermic because energy is released when an electron is attracted to the neutral atom.
> 
> The second electron affinity: O<sup>-</sup>(g) + e<sup>-</sup> → O<sup>2-</sup>(g). This is endothermic because the incoming electron is repelled by the existing negative charge on O<sup>-</sup>, requiring energy input to overcome this repulsion.
