# Mark Schemes — Homogeneous & Heterogeneous Catalysts
**26. Reaction Kinetics** · Cambridge (CIE) A-LEVEL chemistry (9701)


## Structured Questions (3 questions)


### Q1
The completed electron configuration of  `Fe presubscript 56 presuperscript 26` is:

![Electron box diagram showing the complete electron configuration of iron with argon core, 4s2 and 3d6 subshells filled](assets/002-electron-box-diagram-showing-the-complete-electr.png)

**[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for correct electron box notation for Fe

> **[exam-tip]**
> Iron has an argon core ([Ar]) with 8 additional electrons arranged as 4s<sup>2</sup> 3d<sup>6</sup>. Note that the 3d subshell is not completely filled (it can hold up to 10 electrons), so the correct configuration is [Ar] 4s<sup>2</sup> 3d<sup>6</sup>, not [Ar] 4s<sup>2</sup> 3d<sup>8</sup>.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Iron acts as a heterogeneous catalyst in the Haber process:
> 
> - Heterogeneous **[1 mark]**
> - (Because) the iron catalyst is in a different state / phase to the reactants **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> In the Haber process, the reactants nitrogen and hydrogen are gases, while the iron catalyst is a solid. Since the catalyst and reactants are in different states, iron acts as a heterogeneous catalyst.
> 
> You need to include both the identification (heterogeneous) and the explanation (different state or phase to the reactants) to score both marks.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The mode of action of a heterogeneous catalyst in a chemical reaction is:
> 
> - Reactants adsorb onto the surface of the catalyst **[1 mark]**
> - The bonds in the reactants weaken **[1 mark]**
> - Products are desorbed from the surface of the catalyst **[1 mark]**
> 
> For the first marking point, the keyword that must be seen is adsorb (not absorb)

> **[exam-tip]**
> The three key steps for heterogeneous catalysis are adsorption, bond weakening (allowing the reaction to occur), and desorption. Examiners are looking for these three specific terms.
> 
> Note that the catalyst itself is not consumed. It is regenerated when the products desorb, which is why it is classified as a catalyst.

### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Homogeneous catalysis is:
> 
> - Where the catalyst is in the same phase / state as the reactants **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> You need to know the definitions for both homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis. The key distinction is whether the catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (homogeneous) or a different phase (heterogeneous).
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The mode of action of a homogeneous catalyst is:
> 
> - The catalyst reacts with a reactant to form an intermediate (complex) **[1 mark]**
> - The intermediate provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy **[1 mark]**
> - The intermediate reacts to form the products
> **AND**
> the catalyst is regenerated **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The three key points for homogeneous catalysis are:
> 
> 1. Formation of an intermediate (between the catalyst and a reactant)
> 2. An alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy
> 3. Products form and the catalyst is regenerated
> 
> The mode of action of a heterogeneous catalyst (adsorption, bond weakening, desorption) is more commonly tested, but you should know both.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> One advantage and one disadvantage of using a homogeneous catalyst rather than a heterogeneous catalyst are:
> 
> Advantage:
> 
> Any **one** from:
> 
> - High selectivity / fewer unwanted side reactions
> - Can operate at lower temperatures / pressures, reducing energy consumption
> - Easier to control
> 
> **[1 mark]**
> 
> Disadvantage:
> 
> Any **one** from:
> 
> - Separation / purification of the products is challenging / expensive / time-consuming
> - The catalyst can become poisoned
> 
> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Although this type of question is less common in exam papers, you should know at least one advantage and one disadvantage of both homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts.

### Q3
> **[mark-scheme]**
> A suitable catalyst for catalytic cracking is:
> 
> Any **one** from:
> 
> - Aluminium oxide / alumina / Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>
> - Aluminosilicates
> - Zeolite
> - (Hot) pumice stone
> - Porous / ceramic pot
> 
> **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The most common answers are aluminium oxide (Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) and zeolite. Although cracking is covered in an earlier section of the specification, questions on the catalysts used can appear in the context of this topic on catalysis.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Catalytic cracking is an example of heterogeneous catalysis:
> 
> - Heterogeneous **[1 mark]**
> - (Because) the catalyst is in a different state / phase to the reactants **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> The catalyst (aluminium oxide, zeolite, etc.) is a solid, while the hydrocarbon reactants are passed over it as vapours in the gaseous phase. Since the catalyst and reactants are in different states, this is an example of heterogeneous catalysis.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Using a powdered solid catalyst increases the rate of the reaction because:
> 
> - It has a greater surface area **[1 mark]**
> - There are more active sites available (for adsorption of reactant molecules) **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> This question combines collision theory with the mode of action of a heterogeneous catalyst. A powdered solid has a much greater surface area than a solid block or pellet of the same material. The greater surface area means more active sites are available for reactant molecules to adsorb onto, increasing the rate of the reaction.
