### Q1
Debt factoring is selling a business's trade receivables to a specialist company **[1]**, which pays most of their value immediately and then collects the money from the customers itself **[1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Define requires a clear statement of the meaning of the term
> 
> An example alone is not sufficient for full marks but can support a partial definition for 1 mark
> 
> **Indicative content**
> 
> - Debt factoring means selling amounts owed by customers to a factor, which advances most of the value straight away
> - Debt factoring is the transfer of a business's unpaid invoices to a third party in exchange for immediate cash
> - Debt factoring allows a business to receive most of the value of its receivables at once rather than waiting for customers to pay

> **[exam-tip]**
> The business gets its money sooner but receives less than it is owed, and that discount is the whole cost of the arrangement. A definition saying the factor simply "pays the business" misses why any firm would hesitate to use it. Getting the idea of most rather than all into your sentence is usually what earns the second mark.

### Q6

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Define requires a clear statement of the meaning of the term

>

> - The difference between a business's current assets and current liabilities, representing the short-term financial resources available to run the business

### Q7
DB's actual profit for the last six months was only \$2,000, far lower than budgeted, partly due to a \$10,000 adverse labour cost variance **[Ap]**. A bank loan of \$80,000 at 8% would cost DB \$6,400 a year in interest **[K]**, which looks very difficult to afford given DB's current low profit **[An]**. Kofi's offer of 30% of profits would currently cost DB much less than this, since 30% of a small profit is a small amount, making it a safer option for DB right now **[E]**

However, Dayo is opening a third bakery location to grow the business, and if profit rises significantly, giving Kofi 30% every year could end up costing DB more  **[K]**than the fixed \$6,400 loan interest **[Ap]**. Kofi would also become a permanent partner, meaning Dayo gives up some control over decisions and a share of profits for good, whereas a bank loan ends once it is repaid **[An]**

Overall, I would recommend Dayo accepts Kofi's offer for now **[E]**. DB's current profit is too low and unpredictable to safely commit to fixed loan repayments, especially with ongoing recruitment and training costs for skilled bakers **[Ap]**. If DB's profit grows strongly once the new location is established, Dayo could consider replacing Kofi's investment with a bank loan later, once DB is in a stronger financial position to manage the fixed repayments **[E]**

> | **1** | - Analysis based on one reason why Dayo should/should not accept Kofi's offer to invest as a business partner rather than taking out a bank loan |
> 
> **Indicative content **
> 
> - A bank loan of \$80,000 at 8% interest would cost DB \$6,400 a year in fixed interest payments
> 
>   - DB made only \$2,000 profit in the last six months, well below what would be needed to comfortably cover this, so committing to fixed loan repayments right now could put real strain on DB's cash flow.
> - Kofi's offer involves no fixed interest payments, instead taking 30% of DB's annual profits
> 
>   - Since DB's current profit is low, 30% of it would currently cost DB much less than \$6,400 a year, making Kofi's offer the cheaper and less risky option in the short term while DB's profitability remains weak.
> - However, DB's breakeven point between the two options is where 30% of annual profit equals \$6,400
> 
>   - This works out at an annual profit of around \$21,300 (6,400 ÷ 0.3); if the new bakery location helps DB's profits grow beyond this figure, giving Kofi 30% every year would end up costing DB more than the fixed loan interest, making the bank loan cheaper in the long run.
> - Taking on Kofi as a partner means giving up 30% of profits permanently and sharing control over decisions, whereas a bank loan is a temporary commitment that ends once repaid
> 
>   - This matters for Dayo, who has run DB alone for five years and may value keeping full ownership and control as the business grows to a third location.
> - Kofi may also bring useful skills or experience to DB, beyond just the \$80,000 investment, which could help Dayo manage the challenges of expansion, such as DB's existing difficulty recruiting and training skilled bakers.
> 
> **Evaluation**
> 
> - The key question is whether DB's profitability recovers and grows once the new location opens, or stays as low as it currently is.
> - Given DB's current position - just \$2,000 profit in six months, against a \$10,000 adverse labour cost variance — committing to \$6,400 a year in fixed loan interest looks risky right now, so Kofi's offer reduces DB's financial risk in the short term.
> - However, if the new location is successful and profit grows well beyond around \$21,300 a year, Kofi's 30% share becomes permanently more expensive than the loan, meaning Dayo would be giving away increasing amounts of profit for a one-off \$80,000 investment.
> - Overall judgement should weigh DB's current fragile profitability and cash flow against Dayo's long-term goals for growth and control.
> 
> Accept any other valid response.

> **[exam-tip]**
> "Evaluate" questions at this level require you to do more than simply list advantages and disadvantages. You must build an argument on each side, apply it to the specific business context (e.g. DB's actual profit figures, the interest rate, Kofi's 30% share), and then reach a clear, justified conclusion.
> 
> A common mistake is to describe both options without ever making a judgement. Make sure your final paragraph clearly states which option you recommend and why, referencing the data in the case study. For example, use the actual profit of \$2,000 over six months to demonstrate whether DB can realistically afford the loan repayments.
> 
> Another pitfall is ignoring the long-term perspective. Strong answers consider both the short-term financial risk and the long-term cost implications — giving away 30% of profits forever could be far more expensive than a loan that is eventually repaid.
> 
> Avoid generic statements such as "a loan is risky" without linking them to DB's specific financial data. The examiner is looking for context throughout your answer, not just in the conclusion.
