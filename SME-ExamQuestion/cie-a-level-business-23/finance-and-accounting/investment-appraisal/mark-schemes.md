### Q1
Investment appraisal produces figures from forecasts, so its usefulness depends on how sound those forecasts are **[K]**. Nayland's net present value of \$410 000 rests on revenue growing 5% a year for ten years **[Ap]**. That rate matches the last three years, so it is not invented, but two of the four largest contracts are with the government and are re-tendered every three years, meaning roughly half the revenue base is re-competed three times during the project's life **[An]**. A single lost tender would break the growth assumption early, and because net present value compounds those flows across ten years the error would widen rather than stay contained **[An]**

The discount rate is a second weakness **[K]**. The appraisal uses 8%, but the bank has indicated borrowing costs will rise, and at 11% the net present value becomes negative **[Ap]**. The project therefore sits close to the boundary between adding and destroying value, and what decides which side it falls on is a rate Nayland does not control **[An]**. A net present value of \$410 000 on a \$2.4 million investment is a thin margin for a ten-year commitment, and it disappears on a three-point movement in a single input **[An]**

Nayland cannot rely on this appraisal as it stands **[E]**. The problem is that both key assumptions are fragile in the same direction, and a payback of 6 years 2 months on technology expected to be superseded within about eight leaves almost no margin if either moves **[E]**.
