### Q4
Financial incentives include commission, bonuses, profit sharing, and performance-related pay. In retail, individual performance can often be measured through sales figures, making commission straightforward to implement — staff who sell more earn more, directly linking effort to reward. This is consistent with Taylor's economic man theory, which argues workers are primarily motivated by financial gain. For full-time sales staff in higher-value retail environments such as electronics or furniture stores, the prospect of a meaningful bonus can be a powerful driver of performance and focus.

However, Herzberg's two-factor theory challenges this view. Herzberg argued that pay is a hygiene factor — sufficient pay prevents dissatisfaction but does not create genuine motivation. Once retail employees earn enough to meet their basic needs, additional bonuses are unlikely to make them significantly more engaged. True motivators are intrinsic to the job: recognition, responsibility, achievement, and development opportunities. A retail employee given responsibility for training new staff or managing a product range is more likely to feel genuinely motivated than one who receives a modest monthly bonus.

Commission can also undermine teamwork and service quality in retail by encouraging staff to prioritise their own individual sales over helping colleagues or building customer relationships. Many retail workers are part-time, and for them flexible hours and a positive team environment may be more motivating than financial incentives — a point supported by Mayo's human relations theory.

Overall, financial incentives are most effective for sales-focused roles where individual performance is measurable and employees are primarily income-driven. For the broader retail workforce — particularly part-time and customer service staff — non-financial motivators are likely to be equally or more effective. The most successful approach combines fair base pay to prevent dissatisfaction with non-financial motivators that address higher-order needs, creating genuine long-term engagement rather than short-term performance gains alone
