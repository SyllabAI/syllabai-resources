# Mark Schemes — Vectors
**Vectors** · Edexcel IGCSE Further Pure Maths (4PM1)


## Q1 — medium — 5 marks · exam-questions

### 3((a)) — 3 marks
$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ is not given, so build it from the two vectors that are

Travelling from $A$ to $B$ means subtracting the vector to $A$ from the vector to $B$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = \overset{\rightarrow}{OB} - \overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$

Subtract the $i$ components and the $j$ components separately

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A B} = \left(8 - 4\right) \mathbf{i} + \left(p - 3\right) \mathbf{j}`

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A B} = 4 \mathbf{i} + \left(p - 3\right) \mathbf{j}`

**[B1]**

$i$ and $j$ are perpendicular unit vectors, so Pythagoras gives the magnitude

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = \sqrt{4^{2} + \left(p - 3\right)^{2}}`

Set that equal to the magnitude the question gives you

`\sqrt{4^{2} + \left(p - 3\right)^{2}} = 2 \sqrt{13}`

Squaring both sides clears the square root, and `\left(2 \sqrt{13}\right)^{2} = 4 \times 13`

`16 + \left(p - 3\right)^{2} = 52`

Expand the bracket and collect everything on one side

$16 + p^{2} - 6 p + 9 = 52$

$p^{2} - 6 p - 27 = 0$

**[M1]**

Factorise. Two numbers multiplying to $- 27$ and adding to $- 6$ are $- 9$ and $3$

`\left(p - 9\right) \left(p + 3\right) = 0`

The question asks for the possible values, so give both roots

$p=9,p=-3$

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **B1**: A correct vector for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{BA}$, so `\pm \left(4 \mathbf{i} + \left(p - 3\right) \mathbf{j}\right)`.
> 
> **M1**: Forms an equation in $p$ by setting the magnitude of your vector equal to $2 \sqrt{13}$, or the sum of the squares of its components equal to 52.
> 
> **A1**: Both $p = 9$ and $p = - 3$. One value alone does not earn this mark.
> 
> There is a single accuracy mark for the pair of values, not one mark for each, so a solution stopping at $p = 9$ scores nothing here even though that value is correct.
> 
> Solving the quadratic by the formula or by completing the square is equally acceptable.
> 
> The official scheme writes the magnitude as $\sqrt{52}$ rather than $2\sqrt{13}$. Those are the same number, and either form may be used.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Subtract in the order the letters run.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ goes from $A$ to $B$, so it is the far end minus the near end, $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB} - \overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$
> - Getting it the wrong way round reverses every sign, and here it happens to give the same two values of $p$, so it will not always warn you
> 
> Square the whole of $2\sqrt{13}$, not just the surd.
> 
> - `\left(2 \sqrt{13}\right)^{2} = 2^{2} \times 13`, which is 52, and forgetting to square the 2 gives 13 and a quadratic with no whole-number roots
> - A quadratic that refuses to factorise is often a sign that a magnitude was squared carelessly
> 
> Expect two answers whenever a magnitude leads to a quadratic.
> 
> - `\left(p - 3\right)^{2}` is the same for $p - 3 = 6$ and $p - 3 = - 6$, which is why there are two positions for $B$
> - One mark covers both values here, so writing only the positive one loses it
> 
> Part (b) tells you which value it wants.
> 
> - The condition $p > 0$ is there to pick out $p = 9$, so keep both values to hand rather than discarding one now

### 3((b)) — 2 marks
Part (a) gave two possible values of $p$, and only one of them is positive

$p = 9$

Put that value into the expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ from part (a)

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A B} = 4 \mathbf{i} + \left(9 - 3\right) \mathbf{j}`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = 4 i + 6 j$

**[B1]**

A unit vector in the same direction is the vector divided by its own magnitude

The question already tells you that magnitude, and part (a) used it: `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = 2 \sqrt{13}`

`\text{unit vector} = \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{13}} \left(4 \mathbf{i} + 6 \mathbf{j}\right)`

**[B1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **B1**: Selects $p = 9$ and writes $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = 4 i + 6 j$.
> 
> **B1**: Divides your vector by its magnitude, giving `\frac{1}{2 \sqrt{13}} \left(4 \mathbf{i} + 6 \mathbf{j}\right)`, or equivalent.
> 
> Equivalent forms all earn the mark, including the cancelled `\frac{1}{\sqrt{13}} \left(2 \mathbf{i} + 3 \mathbf{j}\right)`, the rationalised `\frac{\sqrt{13}}{13} \left(2 \mathbf{i} + 3 \mathbf{j}\right)`, and the components written out separately as $\frac{2}{\sqrt{13}} i + \frac{3}{\sqrt{13}} j$. No simplifying is required.
> 
> Allow follow-through from your own value of $p$ and your own vector, provided you divide by the magnitude of that vector.

> **[exam-tip]**
> A unit vector is just the vector shrunk to length 1, so divide by the length you already have.
> 
> - The magnitude was handed to you in part (a) as $2 \sqrt{13}$, so there is nothing new to calculate
> - Dividing by a magnitude never changes the direction, which is what keeps the answer parallel to $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$
> 
> Use the condition $p > 0$ before substituting, not after.
> 
> - $p = - 3$ would give $4 i - 6 j$, a vector pointing the other way, so picking the wrong root gives the wrong direction
> - Two marks for two lines of work here, so the condition is doing real work
> 
> You are not asked to simplify.
> 
> - `fraction numerator 1 over denominator 2 square root of 13 end fraction open parentheses 4 bold i plus 6 bold j close parentheses` is a complete answer, and cancelling the 2 to reach `fraction numerator 1 over denominator square root of 13 end fraction open parentheses 2 bold i plus 3 bold j close parentheses` is optional
> - If you do rationalise, `fraction numerator square root of 13 over denominator 13 end fraction open parentheses 2 bold i plus 3 bold j close parentheses` is the form the other questions on this paper use

## Q2 — medium — 11 marks · exam-questions

### 10((a)) — 3 marks
A quadrilateral is a parallelogram when both pairs of opposite sides are equal in length and parallel

Reading the vertices in order, the opposite sides of $A B C D$ are $A B$ with $D C$, and $B C$ with $A D$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AC}$ is a diagonal rather than a side, and it is what lets you reach $C$

Travel from $B$ back to $A$ and then out to $C$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{BA} + \overset{\rightarrow}{AC}$

Reversing a vector reverses its sign, so `\overset{\rightarrow}{B A} = - \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right)`

`\overset{\rightarrow}{B C} = - \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right) + 7 \mathbf{a} + 9 \mathbf{b}`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = 4 a + 5 b$

**[B1]**

Now do the same for the other pair, travelling from $D$ back to $A$ and then out to $C$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{DC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{DA} + \overset{\rightarrow}{AC}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{D C} = - \left(4 \mathbf{a} + 5 \mathbf{b}\right) + 7 \mathbf{a} + 9 \mathbf{b}`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{DC} = 3 a + 4 b$

**[B1]**

Compare each vector you have found with the one opposite it

$\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AD}$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{DC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$

Two equal vectors have the same magnitude and the same direction, so each of those pairs of sides is equal in length and parallel

**Final answer:** **Both pairs of opposite sides are equal and parallel, so the quadrilateral is a parallelogram**

**[B1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **B1**: A correct vector for $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{CB}$.
> 
> **B1**: A correct vector for $\overset{\rightarrow}{DC}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{CD}$.
> 
> **B1**: A complete conclusion, which must both compare the two pairs of opposite sides and state that $A B C D$ is therefore a parallelogram. Allow follow-through from your own vectors, including the reverse directions.
> 
> The conclusion mark needs the word parallelogram and a reason. Showing the two vectors and stopping does not earn it.
> 
> The official scheme also allows an argument from side lengths, marked in the same way: the first mark for `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A D}\right| = \left|\overset{\rightarrow}{B C}\right|`, the second for `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = \left|\overset{\rightarrow}{D C}\right|`, and the third for concluding that opposite sides are equal in length so the quadrilateral is a parallelogram. Note that putting numbers to those lengths needs $a$ and $b$ to be perpendicular unit vectors, which this question does not say, so the comparison of vectors above is the safer argument.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Work out which lines are actually the sides before you start.
> 
> - The vertices are given in the order $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, so the sides are $A B$, $B C$, $C D$ and $D A$
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{AC}$ joins opposite vertices, so it is a diagonal, and treating it as a side is the quickest way to go wrong here
> 
> Build a missing vector by walking a path through points you know.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{BA} + \overset{\rightarrow}{AC}$ works because the middle letters match and cancel
> - Every vector in this question is measured from $A$, so every path goes back through $A$
> 
> Equal vectors say more than equal lengths.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AD}$ gives you parallel and equal in one statement, because a vector carries both direction and magnitude
> - Equal lengths alone would not be enough to name the shape, so quoting the vectors is the stronger answer
> 
> A show that question is marked on the reason, not the answer.
> 
> - The last mark is for the sentence, so write it out: both pairs of opposite sides are equal and parallel, therefore $A B C D$ is a parallelogram

### 10((b)) — 8 marks
$F$ divides $C D$ in the ratio $1 : 2$ measured from $C$, so $C F$ is one third of the whole of $C D$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{CD}$ is $\overset{\rightarrow}{DC}$ reversed, and part (a) found $\overset{\rightarrow}{DC} = 3 a + 4 b$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{CF} = \frac{1}{3} \overset{\rightarrow}{CD}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{C F} = - \frac{1}{3} \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right)`

**[B1]**

Reach $F$ from $A$ by going out to $C$ and then on to $F$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AF} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AC} + \overset{\rightarrow}{CF}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A F} = 7 \mathbf{a} + 9 \mathbf{b} - \frac{1}{3} \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right)`

Collect the $a$ terms and the $b$ terms separately

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AF} = 6 a + \frac{23}{3} b$

**[B1]**

$E$ lies on two different lines, so write $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$ in two different ways and make them agree

$A$, $F$ and $E$ are collinear, so $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$ is a multiple of $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A E} = \mu \left(6 \mathbf{a} + \frac{23}{3} \mathbf{b}\right)`

**[M1]**

$E$ also lies on the line through $B$ and $C$, so reach it by going out to $B$ and then along $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$

Part (a) found $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC} = 4 a + 5 b$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AE} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AB} + λ \overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A E} = 3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b} + \lambda \left(4 \mathbf{a} + 5 \mathbf{b}\right)`

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A E} = \left(3 + 4 \lambda\right) \mathbf{a} + \left(4 + 5 \lambda\right) \mathbf{b}`

**[M1]**

$a$ and $b$ point in different directions, so the only way the two expressions can describe the same vector is for the $a$ parts to match and the $b$ parts to match

$6 μ = 3 + 4 λ$

$\frac{23}{3} μ = 4 + 5 λ$

**[M1]**

Eliminate $λ$ by multiplying the first equation by 5 and the second by 4, so that both carry $20 λ$

$30 μ = 15 + 20 λ$

$\frac{92}{3} μ = 16 + 20 λ$

Subtract the first of those from the second

$\frac{92}{3} μ - 30 μ = 1$

$\frac{2}{3} μ = 1$

**[M1]**

$μ = \frac{3}{2}$

**[A1]**

Substitute back into the expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$ along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{A E} = \frac{3}{2} \left(6 \mathbf{a} + \frac{23}{3} \mathbf{b}\right)`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AE} = 9 a + \frac{23}{2} b$

**[B1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **B1**: A correct vector for $\overset{\rightarrow}{CF}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{DF}$, so `- \frac{1}{3} \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right)` or `\frac{2}{3} \left(3 \mathbf{a} + 4 \mathbf{b}\right)`. This may be embedded in your working for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$.
> 
> **B1**: A correct vector for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$, using your $\overset{\rightarrow}{CF}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{DF}$.
> 
> **M1**: One expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$, using your $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$ and a parameter.
> 
> **M1**: A second expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$ by a different path, using your own vectors and a second, different parameter.
> 
> **M1**: Sets your two expressions equal and compares the $a$ and $b$ components to form two equations in the two parameters. This mark depends on both earlier method marks.
> 
> **M1**: Solves your two equations by any method to find a value for one parameter. This mark depends on all three earlier method marks.
> 
> **A1**: $μ = \frac{3}{2}$, or $λ = \frac{3}{2}$.
> 
> **B1**: $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE} = 9 a + \frac{23}{2} b$, written in the form the question asks for. Allow follow-through from your own $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$ and parameter, provided your value of $λ$ lies between 1 and 2.
> 
> The official scheme also allows a route through $\overset{\rightarrow}{BE}$, marked in the same way. The first two marks are unchanged. In place of the second expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{AE}$, write `\overset{\rightarrow}{B E} = - 3 \mathbf{a} - 4 \mathbf{b} + \mu \left(6 \mathbf{a} + \frac{23}{3} \mathbf{b}\right)` for the second method mark, then use the fact that $\overset{\rightarrow}{BE}$ is a multiple of $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$ to form $\frac{6μ-3}{\frac{23}{3}μ-4} = \frac{4}{5}$ for the third, and solve that for $μ$ for the fourth.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two expressions for the same point is the standard method whenever two lines meet.
> 
> - Being on line $A F$ gives you one expression, being on line $B C$ gives you another, and setting them equal is what pins $E$ down
> - Use two different letters for the two unknown multiples, or the algebra collapses to nothing
> 
> Read the ratio carefully: $1 : 2$ makes three parts, not two.
> 
> - $CF:FD=1:2$ means $\overset{\rightarrow}{CF}=\frac{1}{3}\overset{\rightarrow}{CD}$, and writing $\frac{1}{2}$ is the commonest error in this question
> - Add the two numbers in the ratio to get the number of parts, then count how many of them you need
> 
> Watch which direction the ratio is measured in.
> 
> - $C F$ is measured from $C$, so the journey runs along $\overset{\rightarrow}{CD}$, which is $\overset{\rightarrow}{DC}$ with every sign reversed
> - Using $\overset{\rightarrow}{DC}$ by mistake puts $F$ outside the quadrilateral
> 
> Both parameters coming out as $\frac{3}{2}$ here is a coincidence.
> 
> - They measure different things, one along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AF}$ and one along $\overset{\rightarrow}{BC}$, so do not use one as a check on the other
> - Substitute your value into the right expression, and if in doubt substitute into both and confirm you get the same vector
> 
> Use $λ$ to check your answer is even possible.
> 
> - $λ = \frac{3}{2}$ is greater than 1, which puts $E$ beyond $C$, exactly as $B C$ extended requires
> - A value below 1 would place $E$ between $B$ and $C$, so it would contradict the question

## Q3 — medium — 12 marks · exam-questions

### 8((a)) — 3 marks
**(i)**

There is no direct route from $A$ to $B$ in the information given, so travel through $O$

Go backwards along $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$ and then forwards along $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AO} + \overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$

Reversing a vector reverses its sign, so `\overset{\rightarrow}{A O} = - \left(4 \mathbf{a} + 5 \mathbf{b}\right)`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = - 4 a - 5 b + 8 a - b$

**[M1]**

Collect the $a$ terms and the $b$ terms separately

$\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = 4 a - 6 b$

**[A1]**

**(ii)**

$a$ and $b$ are perpendicular unit vectors, so the two components are at right angles and Pythagoras gives the length

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = \sqrt{4^{2} + ( - 6 )^{2}}`

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = \sqrt{52}`

The question asks for the simplest form, and $52 = 4 \times 13$

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = 2 \sqrt{13}`

**[B1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct vector path to find $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ or $\overset{\rightarrow}{BA}$, so `\pm \left(- 4 \mathbf{a} - 5 \mathbf{b} + 8 \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\right)`.
> 
> **A1**: $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = 4 a - 6 b$.
> 
> **B1**: The exact magnitude in its simplest form, $2 \sqrt{13}$.
> 
> $\sqrt{52}$ on its own does not earn the final mark, because the question asks for the simplest form.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Build any missing vector by walking a path through points you do know.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = \overset{\rightarrow}{AO} + \overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$ works because the middle letters match up and cancel
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{AO}$ is not given, but it is just $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$ with every sign reversed
> 
> Watch the sign on the single $b$.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB} = 8 a - b$ contributes $- b$, and $- 5 b - b = - 6 b$
> - A coefficient of 1 written invisibly is one of the easiest things to drop
> 
> Simplify the surd before you stop.
> 
> - $52 = 4 \times 13$, so the surd simplifies to $2 \sqrt{13}$, and the mark here is specifically for the simplified form
> - Look for the largest square factor rather than the first one you spot
> 
> Keep $\sqrt{52}$ to hand for part (b).
> 
> - It is one of the three side lengths of the triangle, so you will want it again in a moment
> - Using the unsimplified form there keeps the cosine rule arithmetic tidier

### 8((b)) — 4 marks
The area formula $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} a b \text{sin} C$ needs two sides and the angle between them

The two sides from $O$ are $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$ and $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$, so find their lengths first

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O A}\right| = \sqrt{4^{2} + 5^{2}}`

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O A}\right| = \sqrt{41}`

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O B}\right| = \sqrt{8^{2} + ( - 1 )^{2}}`

`\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O B}\right| = \sqrt{65}`

**[M1]**

Part (a) already gives the third side, `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{A B}\right| = \sqrt{52}`

Three sides and no angle means the cosine rule, with `\angle A O B` opposite $A B$

`( \sqrt{52} )^{2} = ( \sqrt{41} )^{2} + ( \sqrt{65} )^{2} - 2 \times \sqrt{41} \times \sqrt{65} \times \text{cos} \angle A O B`

**[M1]**

Rearrange for the cosine, and note the squares simply undo the square roots

`\text{cos} \angle A O B = \frac{41 + 65 - 52}{2 \times \sqrt{41} \times \sqrt{65}}`

`\angle A O B = 58 . 465 \ldots \circ`

Now use the area formula with the two sides that enclose this angle

$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \sqrt{41} \times \sqrt{65} \times \text{sin} 58 . 465 \dots \circ$

**[M1]**

$\text{Area} = 22$

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct expression, simplified or not, for `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O A}\right|` or `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O B}\right|`.
> 
> **M1**: A correct substitution into the cosine rule to find any angle of the triangle.
> 
> **M1**: A correct substitution into $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} a b \text{sin} C$, using your own lengths and angle.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to 22.
> 
> Any of the three angles may be used, provided the two sides in the area formula are the two that enclose it. Using `\angle O A B = 72 . 349 \ldots \circ` with $\sqrt{41}$ and $\sqrt{52}$, or `\angle A B O = 49 . 184 \ldots \circ` with $\sqrt{65}$ and $\sqrt{52}$, both reach the same area.
> 
> The determinant method is equally acceptable, and is marked like this. A correct statement of the area as $\frac{1}{2}$ times the determinant, listing the points in order and repeating the first at the end, earns the first mark. A correct evaluation of the determinant, ignoring the $\frac{1}{2}$, earns the second, and multiplying by $\frac{1}{2}$ earns the third, which a correct answer implies. The accuracy mark then requires the exact value 22 rather than a rounded one.
> 
> Heron's formula is also equally acceptable, and is marked like this. A correct length for `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O A}\right|` or `\left|\overset{\rightarrow}{O B}\right|` earns the first mark, finding half the perimeter earns the second, substituting into a correct Heron's formula earns the third, and the exact value 22 earns the fourth.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Vectors and trigonometry meet here, and the trigonometry is ordinary triangle work.
> 
> - Once you have the three side lengths, the vectors have done their job
> - Three sides and no angle is the standard cosine rule situation, exactly as in a non-vector question
> 
> Do not round the angle before using it.
> 
> - $58 . 465 \dots \circ$ carried in full gives exactly 22, while $58 . 5 \circ$ gives $22 . 01$
> - The area here is a whole number, which is a strong hint that no rounding should have crept in
> 
> The squares and square roots cancel in the cosine rule, so the arithmetic is tidier than it looks.
> 
> - $( \sqrt{41} )^{2} = 41$, so the numerator is just $41 + 65 - 52$
> - Leave the lengths as surds rather than converting them to decimals first
> 
> There is a much shorter route worth knowing.
> 
> - With $a$ and $b$ as perpendicular unit vectors, $\overset{\rightarrow}{OA}$ and $\overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$ behave like coordinates
> - The area is then `\frac{1}{2} \left|4 \times \left(- 1\right) - 8 \times 5\right|`, which gives 22 exactly in one line

### 8((c)) — 5 marks
$C$ lies on two different lines, so write $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC}$ in two different ways and make them agree

$O$, $C$ and $D$ are collinear, so $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC}$ is a multiple of $\overset{\rightarrow}{OD}$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{O C} = \lambda \left(15 \mathbf{a} + 10 \mathbf{b}\right)`

**[M1]**

$C$ also lies on $A B$, so reach it by going out to $A$ and then part of the way along $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$

Part (a) gives $\overset{\rightarrow}{AB} = 4 a - 6 b$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{O C} = 4 \mathbf{a} + 5 \mathbf{b} + \mu \left(4 \mathbf{a} - 6 \mathbf{b}\right)`

Collect the $a$ terms and the $b$ terms

`\overset{\rightarrow}{O C} = \left(4 + 4 \mu\right) \mathbf{a} + \left(5 - 6 \mu\right) \mathbf{b}`

**[M1]**

$a$ and $b$ point in different directions, so the only way two expressions can be equal is for the $a$ parts to match and the $b$ parts to match

$4 + 4 μ = 15 λ$

$5 - 6 μ = 10 λ$

Eliminate $λ$ by multiplying the first equation by 2 and the second by 3, making both right-hand sides $30 λ$

$8 + 8 μ = 15 - 18 μ$

**[M1]**

$26 μ = 7$

$μ = \frac{7}{26}$

**[A1]**

Substitute back into the expression for $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC}$ along $A B$

`\overset{\rightarrow}{O C} = \left(4 + 4 \times \frac{7}{26}\right) \mathbf{a} + \left(5 - 6 \times \frac{7}{26}\right) \mathbf{b}`

$\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = \frac{66}{13} a + \frac{44}{13} b$

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: Writes $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = λ \overset{\rightarrow}{OD}$, or the equivalent with any letter.
> 
> **M1**: Writes $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{OA} + μ \overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ and collects it into the form `\left(4 + 4 \mu\right) \mathbf{a} + \left(5 - 6 \mu\right) \mathbf{b}`.
> 
> **M1**: Compares the $a$ and $b$ components to form two equations and solves them simultaneously. This mark depends on both earlier method marks.
> 
> **A1**: $μ = \frac{7}{26}$, or $λ = \frac{22}{65}$.
> 
> **A1**: $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = \frac{66}{13} a + \frac{44}{13} b$, or equivalently $5 \frac{1}{13} a + 3 \frac{5}{13} b$. The answer must be fully correct.
> 
> Substituting $λ = \frac{22}{65}$ into `\lambda \left(15 \mathbf{a} + 10 \mathbf{b}\right)` reaches the same vector and is equally acceptable.
> 
> Going the other way round the triangle, $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{OB} + μ \overset{\rightarrow}{BA}$, is also equally acceptable.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two routes to the same point is the standard method for an intersection.
> 
> - Being on $O D$ gives you one expression, being on $A B$ gives you another, and setting them equal is what pins the point down
> - Use two different letters for the two unknown multiples, or the algebra will collapse
> 
> Comparing components only works because $a$ and $b$ are independent.
> 
> - They point in different directions, so no amount of $a$ can ever make up a shortfall in $b$
> - That is what turns one vector equation into two ordinary simultaneous equations
> 
> Be careful about where the parameter starts from.
> 
> - $\overset{\rightarrow}{OC} = \overset{\rightarrow}{OA} + μ \overset{\rightarrow}{AB}$ means $μ$ measures the fraction of the way from $A$ to $B$
> - Writing $μ \overset{\rightarrow}{OB}$ by mistake is the commonest error, and it describes a completely different point
> 
> Do not stop at the value of the parameter.
