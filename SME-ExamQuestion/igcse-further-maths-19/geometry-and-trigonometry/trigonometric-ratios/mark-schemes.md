# Mark Schemes — Trigonometric Ratios
**Geometry & Trigonometry** · Edexcel IGCSE Further Pure Maths (4PM1)


## Q1 — medium — 15 marks · exam-questions

### 10((a)) — 3 marks
In a right pyramid the apex sits directly above the centre of the base, so start by locating that centre

Let $M$ be the point where the diagonals of the square cross, so $O M$ is the vertical height $h$

The diagonal of the square is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with two sides of 8

$A C = \sqrt{8^{2}+8^{2}}$

$A C = 8 \sqrt{2}$

**[M1]**

$M$ is the midpoint of that diagonal, so $A M$ is half of it

$A M = 4 \sqrt{2}$

Triangle $O M A$ has a right angle at $M$, with $O A = 12$ as its hypotenuse

$h = \sqrt{12^{2}-(4\sqrt{2})^{2}}$

**[M1]**

$h = \sqrt{112}$

$112 = 16 \times 7$, so the surd simplifies

$h = 4 \sqrt{7}$

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct method for the diagonal of the base, or for half of it, so $8 \sqrt{2}$ or $4 \sqrt{2}$.
> 
> **M1**: A correct use of Pythagoras in a right-angled triangle containing $h$, $O A$ and half the diagonal.
> 
> **A1**: $h = 4 \sqrt{7}$.
> 
> The question asks for the exact value, so $10 . 58$ does not earn the accuracy mark.
> 
> Working with the diagonal $B D$ instead of $A C$ is equally acceptable, since they are the same length and cross at the same point.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The first move in every pyramid question is to find the foot of the vertical height.
> 
> - For a right pyramid on a square base that is the centre of the square, where the diagonals cross
> - Sketching the single right-angled triangle $O M A$ separately, out of the three-dimensional picture, makes the rest straightforward
> 
> Half the diagonal is the length you need, not the side.
> 
> - The sloping edge $O A$ runs to a corner, so the horizontal distance is $A M$, not 4
> - Using 4 by mistake gives $h = \sqrt{128}$, which is the commonest wrong answer here
> 
> Keep the surds exact all the way through.
> 
> - $( 4 \sqrt{2} )^{2} = 32$ exactly, so $h^{2}$ is $144 - 32$, which is $112$ with no rounding at all
> - Simplify $\sqrt{112}$ by looking for the largest square factor, which is 16

### 10((b)) — 2 marks
The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow on that plane

$O$ is directly above $M$, so the shadow of $O A$ on the base is $M A$, and the angle wanted is `\angle O A M`

That is the angle at $A$ in the same right-angled triangle used in part (a)

The height is opposite it and $A M$ is adjacent to it, so use the tangent

`\text{tan} \angle O A M = \frac{4 \sqrt{7}}{4 \sqrt{2}}`

**[M1]**

`\angle O A M = 61 . 8744 \ldots \circ`

`\angle O A M = 61 . 9 \circ \textrm{ } \left(1 d . p .\right)`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct trigonometric statement for the angle between $O A$ and the base, using your $h$ and half the diagonal.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to $61 . 9 \circ$.
> 
> Any equivalent trigonometry is acceptable, so `\text{cos} \angle O A M = \frac{4 \sqrt{2}}{12}` or `\text{sin} \angle O A M = \frac{4 \sqrt{7}}{12}` both earn the method mark.

> **[exam-tip]**
> A line meets a plane at the angle between the line and its shadow.
> 
> - Shine a light straight down: $O A$ casts the shadow $M A$, so the angle is at $A$ between $O A$ and $M A$
> - That phrasing turns every "line and plane" question into an ordinary right-angled triangle
> 
> You already have the triangle from part (a), so do not build a new one.
> 
> - $O M A$ has the right angle at $M$, height $4 \sqrt{7}$, base $4 \sqrt{2}$ and hypotenuse 12
> - All three sides are known, so any of sine, cosine or tangent will work
> 
> Use the exact values rather than the rounded ones.
> 
> - $\frac{4\sqrt{7}}{4\sqrt{2}}$ typed straight into the calculator avoids carrying $10 . 58$ through
> - On a 1 decimal place answer that rarely matters, but the habit protects you when it does

### 10((c)) — 2 marks
Two planes meet along a line, and the angle between them is measured in the direction perpendicular to that line

Plane $A O B$ and the base meet along $A B$, so work at the point of $A B$ closest to the centre

Let $N$ be the midpoint of $A B$. Then $O N$ is perpendicular to $A B$, and so is $M N$

So the angle between the planes is `\angle O N M`

$M N$ runs from the centre of the square to the middle of a side, which is half the side length

$M N = 4$

Triangle $O M N$ has its right angle at $M$, with the height opposite `\angle O N M`

`\text{tan} \angle O N M = \frac{4 \sqrt{7}}{4}`

**[M1]**

`\angle O N M = 69 . 2951 \ldots \circ`

`\angle O N M = 69 . 3 \circ \textrm{ } \left(1 d . p .\right)`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct trigonometric statement for the angle between the two planes, using your $h$ and a horizontal distance of 4.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to $69 . 3 \circ$.
> 
> Any equivalent trigonometry is acceptable. Working through the slant height $O N = \sqrt{128}$ and using `\text{cos} \angle O N M = \frac{4}{\sqrt{128}}` reaches the same angle.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The hard part of a plane-to-plane angle is choosing where to measure it.
> 
> - Find the line where the two planes meet, here $A B$, then move perpendicular to it in each plane
> - $O N$ and $M N$ are both perpendicular to $A B$, which is exactly what makes `\angle O N M` the right angle to find
> 
> The horizontal distance changes between parts (b) and (c), and the height does not.
> 
> - Part (b) runs to a corner, so the horizontal distance is $4 \sqrt{2}$
> - Part (c) runs to the middle of a side, so it is only 4, which is why this angle is the larger of the two
> 
> Check that your two answers are consistent.
> 
> - A shorter horizontal distance with the same height must give a steeper angle
> - $69 . 3 \circ$ being bigger than $61 . 9 \circ$ is a quick confirmation that neither has been mixed up

### 10((d)) — 4 marks
$P$ and $Q$ are at different heights, so split $P Q$ into a vertical part and a horizontal part

$P$ is the midpoint of $O A$, and $O$ is directly above the centre $M$

So $P$ sits directly above $S$, the midpoint of $A M$, at exactly half the height of the pyramid

$P S = \frac{4\sqrt{7}}{2}$

$P S = 2 \sqrt{7}$

**[M1]**

Everything else happens in the horizontal base, so set up coordinates there

Take $A$ as the origin, with $A B$ along one axis and $A D$ along the other

Then $A$ is `\left(0 , 0\right)`, $B$ is `\left(8 , 0\right)`, $C$ is `\left(8 , 8\right)` and the centre $M$ is `\left(4 , 4\right)`

$S$ is the midpoint of $A M$, so $S$ is `\left(2 , 2\right)`

$B Q : Q C = 3 : 1$ and $B C = 8$, so $B Q = 6$ and $Q$ is `\left(8 , 6\right)`

$S Q^{2} = ( 8 - 2 )^{2} + ( 6 - 2 )^{2}$

$S Q^{2} = 52$

**[A1]**

$P S$ is vertical and $S Q$ is horizontal, so they are at right angles and Pythagoras applies

$P Q^{2} = ( 2 \sqrt{7} )^{2} + 52$

**[M1]**

$P Q^{2} = 80$

$P Q = 4 \sqrt{5}  \text{cm}  \text{as required}$

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: Finds the height of $P$ above the base as half of $h$, so $2 \sqrt{7}$.
> 
> **A1**: A correct horizontal distance from the point below $P$ to $Q$, so $S Q^{2} = 52$ or $S Q = \sqrt{52}$.
> 
> **M1**: Uses Pythagoras with a vertical and a horizontal distance to reach $P Q$.
> 
> **A1**: Reaches the given answer $4 \sqrt{5}$ with no errors.
> 
> The answer is given, so the working must be complete.
> 
> Any equivalent system of right-angled triangles is acceptable. Dropping a perpendicular from $P$ to the diagonal $A C$ and then a second perpendicular across to $B C$ reaches $P Q$ in two steps and earns the same marks.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Any distance in three dimensions splits into one vertical piece and one horizontal piece.
> 
> - Find the point directly below the higher end, then work out the horizontal distance from there
> - One application of Pythagoras then finishes it, no matter how awkward the two points look
> 
> Coordinates on the base are worth the thirty seconds they take.
> 
> - $S$ at `\left(2 , 2\right)` and $Q$ at `\left(8 , 6\right)` makes the horizontal distance a routine calculation
> - Trying to chase the distance round the square with separate triangles is where errors creep in
> 
> Halving works all the way down for a midpoint.
> 
> - $P$ is halfway along $O A$, so it is at half the height and half the horizontal distance from $A$
> - That is what puts $S$ at the midpoint of $A M$ rather than anywhere else
> 
> Read the ratio carefully.
> 
> - $B Q : Q C = 3 : 1$ means $Q$ is three quarters of the way from $B$ to $C$, so $B Q = 6$
> - Reading it the other way round gives $B Q = 2$ and the proof will not close

### 10((e)) — 4 marks
Triangle $P Q A$ has no right angle, so aim to find all three sides and then use the cosine rule

$A$ and $Q$ both lie in the base, so $A Q$ is a horizontal distance and the coordinates from part (d) give it directly

$A Q = \sqrt{8^{2}+6^{2}}$

$A Q = 10$

**[M1]**

$P$ is the midpoint of $O A$, and $O A = 12$

$P A = 6$

Part (d) gives the third side, $P Q = 4 \sqrt{5}$

The angle wanted is at $Q$, so $P A$ is the side opposite it

`\text{cos} \angle P Q A = \frac{10^{2} + ( 4 \sqrt{5} )^{2} - 6^{2}}{2 \times 10 \times 4 \sqrt{5}}`

**[M1]**

`\text{cos} \angle P Q A = 0 . 80498 \ldots`

**[A1]**

`\angle P Q A = 36 . 39124 \ldots \circ`

`\angle P Q A = 36 . 4 \circ \textrm{ } \left(1 d . p .\right)`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct method for $A Q$, giving 10.
> 
> **M1**: A correct substitution into the cosine rule for the angle at $Q$, using your three side lengths.
> 
> **A1**: A correct value or expression for `\text{cos} \angle P Q A`.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to $36 . 4 \circ$.
> 
> Follow through from your own value of $P Q$ in part (d).
> 
> Splitting triangle $P Q A$ with a perpendicular and using right-angled trigonometry is equally acceptable, provided the final angle is the one at $Q$.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The two previous parts have already done most of the work.
> 
> - $P Q = 4 \sqrt{5}$ comes from part (d), and $P A = 6$ is half of the given $O A$
> - Only $A Q$ is new, and it is a flat distance in the base
> 
> Three sides and an angle to find is always the cosine rule.
> 
> - Rearranged, it is $\text{cos} C = \frac{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}}{2ab}$
> - The side that goes on top as $c$ is the one opposite the angle you want, here $P A$
> 
> Getting the wrong angle is the easiest mark to lose here.
> 
> - `\angle P Q A` is at $Q$, so $P A$ is the opposite side
> - Putting $P Q$ on top instead gives the angle at $A$, which is a perfectly good angle but the wrong one
> 
> Squares of surds are exact, so nothing needs rounding until the end.
> 
> - $( 4 \sqrt{5} )^{2} = 80$ exactly, so the numerator is $100 + 80 - 36 = 144$
> - Only the final division and the inverse cosine need the calculator at all

## Q2 — medium — 15 marks · exam-questions

### 9((a)) — 3 marks
`\angle A F B` sits in triangle $A B F$, and the other two angles of that triangle are both given

The three angles of a triangle add to $180 \circ$

`\angle A F B = 180 \circ - 60 \circ - 45 \circ`

`\angle A F B = 75 \circ`

$75 \circ$ is not one of the standard exact angles, but it is the sum of two that are

Write it as $45 \circ + 30 \circ$ and use `\text{sin} \left(A + B\right) = \text{sin} A \text{cos} B + \text{cos} A \text{sin} B` from the formulae sheet

$\text{sin} 75 \circ = \text{sin} 45 \circ \text{cos} 30 \circ + \text{cos} 45 \circ \text{sin} 30 \circ$

**[M1]**

Now put in the exact values

$\text{sin} 75 \circ = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$

**[M1]**

$\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{6}$, so the two terms both end up over 4

$\text{sin} 75 \circ = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{4} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}$

`\text{sin} \angle A F B = \frac{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{6}}{4} \textrm{ } \text{as required}`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: Identifies `\angle A F B = 75 \circ` and uses a correct addition formula to split it. Accept $45 \circ + 30 \circ$, $120 \circ - 45 \circ$, or $60 \circ + 15 \circ$ with the half-angle values.
> 
> **M1**: Substitutes the correct exact values, shown explicitly.
> 
> **A1**: Reaches the given result with no errors.
> 
> The answer is given, so every step has to be shown. A calculator value of $0 . 9659$ earns nothing.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The exact angles are $30 \circ$, $45 \circ$ and $60 \circ$, and anything built from them is fair game.
> 
> - $75 \circ = 45 \circ + 30 \circ$, and $15 \circ = 45 \circ - 30 \circ$ works the same way
> - Whenever a question asks for a surd answer at an unusual angle, look for a sum or difference of two standard ones
> 
> Find the missing angle of the triangle before anything else.
> 
> - The question gives `\angle B A F = 60 \circ` and `\angle A B F = 45 \circ`, so `\angle A F B` follows in one line
> - It is easy to start reaching for the sine rule and miss that the angle is simply $180 \circ$ minus the other two
> 
> Write the exact values in before multiplying.
> 
> - Showing $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ explicitly is worth a mark of its own here
> - Jumping straight to $\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4}$ risks the examiner not seeing where it came from

### 9((b)) — 5 marks
$B F$ is opposite the $60 \circ$ angle and $A B$ is opposite the $75 \circ$ angle, so both are paired with a known angle

That is exactly what the sine rule needs

$\frac{BF}{\text{sin}60\circ} = \frac{24}{\text{sin}75\circ}$

**[M1]**

Substitute the exact values, taking $\text{sin} 75 \circ$ from part (a)

$\frac{BF}{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}} = \frac{24}{\frac{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}}{4}}$

**[M1]**

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal

$B F = \frac{48\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}}$

**[A1]**

A surd in the denominator has to be cleared, so multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}$

`B F = \frac{48 \sqrt{3} \left(\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}\right)}{\left(\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}\right) \left(\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}\right)}`

**[M1]**

The denominator is a difference of two squares, so it becomes $6 - 2$

$B F = \frac{48\sqrt{18}-48\sqrt{6}}{4}$

$\sqrt{18} = 3 \sqrt{2}$, so the first term is $144 \sqrt{2}$

$B F = \frac{144\sqrt{2}-48\sqrt{6}}{4}$

`B F = 12 \left(3 \sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}\right) \textrm{ } \text{cm} \textrm{ } \text{as required}`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct sine rule statement pairing $B F$ with $60 \circ$ and 24 with $75 \circ$.
> 
> **M1**: Substitutes the exact values for both sines, using part (a).
> 
> **A1**: Reaches $\frac{48\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}}$ or an equivalent unrationalised form.
> 
> **M1**: Multiplies numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}$ to rationalise.
> 
> **A1**: Reaches the given result with no errors.
> 
> The question says the calculator must not be used, so every surd manipulation has to be shown. A decimal such as $21 . 5$ appearing anywhere loses the final mark.
> 
> Rationalising by $\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}$ and adjusting the signs at the end is equally acceptable.

> **[exam-tip]**
> "Without using a calculator" tells you the answer will be a surd and the working is being marked.
> 
> - Every simplification has to appear on the page, including the difference of two squares in the denominator
> - A correct final answer with no visible surd work will not score the method marks
> 
> Part (a) is there so that you can substitute an exact value here.
> 
> - $\text{sin} 75 \circ = \frac{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}}{4}$ is exactly what turns this into a surd calculation
> - Questions that prove a value and then use it are almost always signalling this
> 
> Rationalise by changing the sign between the two surds.
> 
> - `\left(\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}\right) \left(\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}\right) = 6 - 2`, which is 4, and that is what clears the denominator
> - The order the two surds are written in does not matter, only that the sign flips
> 
> Watch $\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{6}$.
> 
> - It is $\sqrt{18}$, which simplifies to $3 \sqrt{2}$, and that 3 is where the $3 \sqrt{2}$ in the answer comes from
> - Leaving it as $\sqrt{18}$ means the final form never matches the one printed in the question

### 9((c)) — 3 marks
Plane $A E B$ and plane $A B C D$ meet along $A B$, so the angle between them is measured perpendicular to $A B$

Let $X$ be the point on $A B$ directly below $F$, and $Y$ the matching point on $D C$

Then $F X$ is the height of the triangular end above $A B$, and the required angle is `\angle E X Y`

Find $F X$ first, from the right-angled triangle $B F X$ with its $45 \circ$ angle at $B$

`F X = 12 \left(3 \sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}\right) \text{sin} 45 \circ`

**[M1]**

$F X = 36 - 12 \sqrt{3}$

$E$ sits directly behind $F$, so $E Y$ is the same height, and $X Y$ runs across the base with $X Y = E F$

Triangle $E X Y$ has its right angle at $Y$, with $E Y$ opposite the $65 \circ$ and $X Y$ adjacent to it

$\text{tan} 65 \circ = \frac{36-12\sqrt{3}}{EF}$

**[M1]**

$E F = \frac{36-12\sqrt{3}}{\text{tan}65\circ}$

$E F = 7 . 09505 \dots$

`E F = 7 . 1 \textrm{ } \text{cm} \textrm{ } \left(2 s . f .\right)`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **M1**: A correct method for the perpendicular height of $F$ above $A B$, giving $F X = 36 - 12 \sqrt{3}$, which is $15 . 2153 \dots$ as a decimal.
> 
> **M1**: A correct trigonometric statement linking your height, the $65 \circ$ angle and $E F$.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to $7 . 1$ cm.
> 
> Finding $F X$ from the area of triangle $A B F$ is equally acceptable, since $\frac{1}{2} \times 24 \times F X$ must equal $\frac{1}{2} \times 24 \times B F \times \text{sin} 45 \circ$.
> 
> Using $A F$ and the $60 \circ$ angle at $A$ instead reaches the same height and is equally acceptable.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The angle between two planes is measured along a line perpendicular to where they meet.
> 
> - The planes meet along $A B$, so both arms of the angle must be at right angles to $A B$
> - $X Y$ lies in the base and $X E$ lies in the plane $A E B$, which is what makes `\angle E X Y` the one you want
> 
> The height of the triangular end is the quantity that links every part of this question.
> 
> - $F X = 36 - 12 \sqrt{3}$ comes straight out of part (b) and is used again in part (d)
> - Store it in your calculator rather than rounding to $15 . 2$
> 
> $E F$ is the depth of the prism, not a slant length.
> 
> - $E F$ equals $A D$ and $B C$, and it lies flat in the base, which is why it is the adjacent side here
> - The prism is right, so every one of those edges is perpendicular to the triangular ends
> 
> Two significant figures is a low precision, so do not round early.
> 
> - The unrounded $7 . 09505$ rounds cleanly to $7 . 1$
> - Rounding $\text{tan} 65 \circ$ or the height first can push a borderline answer the wrong way

### 9((d)) — 4 marks
The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow on that plane

$X$ is the point directly below $F$, so the shadow of $C F$ on the base is $C X$

**Final answer:** **The required angle is **`\angle F C X`

**[B1]**

To use that angle, find $C F$ first

$C$ is directly behind $B$ by the depth of the prism, and that depth is perpendicular to $B F$

So $B F$ and $E F$ are two sides of a right angle, with $C F$ as the hypotenuse

`C F = \sqrt{[ 12 \left(3 \sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}\right) ]^{2} + 7 . 09505 \ldots^{2}}`

**[M1]**

$C F = 22 . 6573 \dots$

Triangle $F X C$ has its right angle at $X$, with $F X$ opposite the angle at $C$ and $C F$ as the hypotenuse

`\text{sin} \angle F C X = \frac{15 . 2153 \ldots}{22 . 6573 \ldots}`

**[M1]**

`\angle F C X = 42 . 186 \ldots \circ`

`\angle F C X = 42 . 2 \circ \textrm{ } \left(1 d . p .\right)`

**[A1]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> **B1**: Identifies the required angle as `\angle F C X`, where $X$ is the point on $A B$ directly below $F$.
> 
> **M1**: A correct method for $C F$, using Pythagoras with your $B F$ and your $E F$.
> 
> **M1**: A correct trigonometric statement for the angle, using your height and your $C F$.
> 
> **A1**: Accept answers which round to $42 . 2 \circ$.
> 
> Follow through from your value of $E F$ in part (c).
> 
> Working through the horizontal distance $C X$ instead is equally acceptable, giving `\text{tan} \angle F C X = \frac{15 . 2153 \ldots}{16 . 7883 \ldots}` and the same angle.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Name the angle before you calculate anything.
> 
> - There is a mark here purely for identifying `\angle F C X`, and it is available even if the arithmetic goes wrong
> - Dropping the perpendicular from $F$ to the base is what creates the right-angled triangle you need
> 
> $C F$ is a diagonal across the prism, so it takes two steps.
> 
> - $B F$ is the sloping edge of the triangular end, and $B C$ is the depth, and the two are perpendicular
> - So $C F^{2} = B F^{2} + B C^{2}$, with $B C = E F$ from part (c)
> 
> The height is the same one you found in part (c).
> 
> - $F X = 36 - 12 \sqrt{3}$ has now been used three times, in parts (b), (c) and (d)
> - Recognising a repeated quantity saves time and keeps the rounding consistent
> 
> Choose the ratio that uses the lengths you already have.
> 
> - $F X$ and $C F$ are known, and they are opposite and hypotenuse, so the sine is the natural choice
> - Working out $C X$ first and using the tangent also works, but it is an extra calculation for no gain

### 4() — 6 marks
The plane $D C B A$ is the rectangular face of the prism, containing $D C$ and the length $D A$

The two triangular cross sections are identical, so $F$ sits directly opposite $E$ and $B$ directly opposite $C$

`\angle A B F = 90^{\circ}`, so $F B$ is perpendicular to $A B$, and the prism is a right prism, so $F B$ is perpendicular to its length as well

`\angle B D F = 22.68 \ldots^{\circ}`

`\angle B D F = 22.7^{\circ}`

**[A1]**
