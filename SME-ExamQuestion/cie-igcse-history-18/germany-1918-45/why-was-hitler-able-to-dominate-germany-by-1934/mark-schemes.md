# Mark Schemes — Why was Hitler Able to Dominate Germany by 1934?
**Germany: 1918–45** · Cambridge (CIE) IGCSE history (0470 & 0977)


## Exam Questions (20 questions)


### Q1
The SA was created in August 1921 with a few hundred men and destroyed in June 1934 with three million. In between it was the Nazi Party's most visible asset and its most persistent problem [R].

The first facet is what the SA did for the party in its early years. Formed as the NSDAP's private army and nicknamed the 'Brownshirts', it recruited the unemployed, students and ex-soldiers by offering them money and a daily structure [R]. Hitler used it to control crowds at meetings, start fights in the streets, disrupt the meetings of opponents such as the KPD, and strengthen the party's image [R]. This mattered in the conditions of Weimar Germany, where political violence was normal and most parties had a paramilitary force: a party without one could not hold a meeting safely, so the SA was the price of existing at all [E]. It grew from 800 men in 1922 to 400,000 by 1925 [R].

The second facet is its role in the Nazi seizure of power. During June and July 1932 fighting between the SA and the KPD's Red Front Fighters left over 100 people dead and 7,000 injured, which created exactly the disorder that made Germans want a strong government [R]. After the Reichstag Fire the SA used the emergency decree to justify violence and intimidation against opponents, helping deliver nearly 44% of the vote on 5 March 1933, and it then intimidated Reichstag members before the Enabling Act vote on 24 March [R]. The SA was therefore directly involved in producing both the election result and the majority that ended German democracy [E]. It had been given the same role a decade earlier, when Rohm was made responsible for securing the army and police headquarters in Munich during the putsch of November 1923, though on that occasion it failed [R].

However, the SA repeatedly damaged the party it served. Many middle- and upper-class Germans regarded the Brownshirts as violent thugs, which was a serious obstacle once Hitler decided to seek power by election rather than by force [R]. By 1925 Hitler was losing trust in an organisation of 400,000 men that Rohm had strengthened while Hitler was in prison, so he sent Rohm abroad and created the much smaller and better-disciplined SS under Himmler, which had 3,000 members by 1930 [R]. A party trying to look respectable was being held back by its own army [A]. The SS wore black uniforms from 1932, conducted itself far less violently than the SA, and was made up of men Hitler had selected and trusted personally, which shows exactly what he thought the SA lacked [R].

The SA was also a political threat in its own right. Rohm led around three million men, roughly 60% of them unemployed by 1933, wanted the SA merged with the army, and demanded a 'second revolution' focused on the working class, which conflicted directly with Hitler's need for the support of the rich [R]. Hindenburg threatened martial law if Hitler could not control it [R]. At Bad Wiessee on 30 June 1934 Rohm and around 400 SA members were arrested and shot [E].

Its destruction was as useful as its existence. Killing the SA leadership removed the army's greatest fear and secured its loyalty, which was decisive when Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934 and every soldier swore an oath of loyalty to Hitler personally [R]. Other rivals were killed alongside the SA leadership, including von Schleicher, Gregor Strasser and von Kahr, and the party told the public that Rohm had been planning to replace Hitler [R]. The SA's final service to Hitler was to be sacrificed [A].

The SA was extremely important, but its importance changed completely over time. In the 1920s it was indispensable, because without it the NSDAP could not have operated in the street politics of Weimar Germany, and in 1932 and 1933 its violence and intimidation shaped both the March election and the Enabling Act [A]. Once Hitler was in office, however, the same organisation became the main obstacle to the army's support, which he needed more. The SA was essential to getting Hitler into power and an obstacle to keeping him there, and Hitler's willingness to destroy his own three-million-strong movement in a single night is the clearest evidence of how much the army mattered more [A].

### Q2
Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933, fourteen years after he joined a party with 23 members. How much of that was his own doing, and how much was done for him by circumstances and by other men, is the central question about his rise [R].

The first facet is that Hitler built the party himself. He joined the DAP on 19 September 1919, became head of propaganda in January 1920, co-wrote the Twenty-Five Point Programme, forced a leadership contest in July 1921 which he won against Drexler, and at the conference of January 1922 persuaded members to surrender their right to elect a leader [R]. He also created the SA in August 1921 and appointed his own inner circle of Hess, Goering, Streicher and Rohm [R]. This matters because none of it was inevitable: the DAP was one of many small right-wing parties, and it was Hitler's oratory and organising that made it different [E].

The second facet is that he learned from failure. The Munich Putsch of November 1923 collapsed because Ludendorff released the Bavarian officials and the army stayed loyal, and Hitler was sentenced to five years for high treason [R]. He turned the trial into publicity by representing himself, became a national celebrity, wrote Mein Kampf in prison, and concluded that violent putsches did not work [R]. He then rebuilt the party during the Lean Years, dividing Germany into 35 regions each under a gauleiter, creating the SS under Himmler, and settling the party's direction at the Bamberg Conference of 1926, where he defeated the socialist wing by branding it communist and promoted Goebbels to gauleiter of Berlin [R]. He also secured funding from corporations such as Thyssen, Krupp and Bosch [R]. That preparation is why the NSDAP was ready to exploit the Depression when it came, and it was entirely his own work [A].

However, the conditions that made him electable were not of his making. The Nazis won roughly 3% in May 1928 and were the seventh largest party, using the same leader and the same methods that later won 38% [R]. What changed was the Wall Street Crash, the recall of American loans and unemployment reaching 6.1 million by January 1933 [R]. Hitler did not cause the Depression, and without it the reorganised party would have remained a well-run fringe movement [A].

The weaknesses of the Weimar system also helped him. Proportional representation made majorities almost impossible, chancellors repeatedly abused Article 48, and Hindenburg's willingness to govern by decree had already turned many Germans away from democracy [R]. Bruning's harsh policies from 1930 to 1932 failed to control unemployment and cost him the support of the Reichstag [R]. Hitler inherited a discredited democracy rather than discrediting it himself [E]. Nor did he win the presidency when he contested it: Hindenburg beat him in April 1932 despite Hitler raising his share to 36% [R].

Most decisively, other politicians handed him office. Hindenburg disliked Hitler and twice refused to appoint him, keeping von Papen in July 1932 and choosing von Schleicher in December [R]. Hitler's vote had actually fallen to 33% by November 1932 [R]. What changed Hindenburg's mind was von Schleicher's request to suspend the constitution and rule as a military dictator, and von Papen's warning on 30 January 1933 that a coup was coming, together with his offer to serve as co-chancellor and keep Hitler 'in their hand' [R]. Hitler was given power at his weakest electoral moment by men who thought they were using him [A]. Hindenburg's own conduct mattered too: he had never fully supported the Republic, and his readiness to allow the abuse of Article 48 weakened the Reichstag long before Hitler could [R].

Hitler's own actions were the most important single factor, but they were necessary rather than sufficient. He created the party, survived its greatest failure, and rebuilt it into the only organisation capable of profiting from a crisis, and no one else could have done that [A]. Yet he never won an election outright, and the last and most important step was taken by other people for their own reasons. Hitler made himself the obvious beneficiary of a collapse he did not cause, and his real achievement was to be ready when Germany's politicians decided they needed him [A].

### Q3
The Nazi Party was one of dozens of extremist groups in Weimar Germany, and in May 1928 it held just 12 of the Reichstag's seats. Five years later Hitler was Chancellor. Propaganda is one explanation for that transformation, but it is not the only one [R].

The first facet is that propaganda was built into the party from the start. Hitler became the DAP's head of propaganda in January 1920, and it was in that role that he pushed for the Twenty-Five Point Programme so the party had a clear statement of its aims [R]. He gave 31 speeches in his first year, practised a distinctive style of oration that built to a frantic crescendo, rehearsed and photographed his hand gestures, and produced images of himself speaking [R]. Membership rose from 1,000 in June 1920 to 3,000 by the end of the year [R]. This mattered because a party of 23 members had no money, no seats and no reputation: publicity was the only asset it could generate for itself, and Hitler's speaking was what turned a Munich discussion group into a movement [E].

The second facet is Goebbels and the mass campaigns after 1929. Goebbels organised Hitler's campaigns in 1930 and 1932, using funding from big businesses to hire aeroplanes so Hitler could tour the country, to stage marches and rallies, and to produce posters, radio broadcasts and pamphlets attacking the government's handling of the Depression [R]. The party had also been publishing the Volkischer Beobachter since December 1920, distributing 17,000 copies within a year [R]. The results were dramatic: 18.3% in 1930, 30% for Hitler in the March 1932 presidential election, 36% in April, and 38% in July 1932 [E]. Hitler became a national celebrity, and his oratory converted many who had been sceptical of Nazi policies [R]. The money came from big corporations such as Thyssen, Krupp and Bosch, who believed Hitler's influence over workers would limit the power of trade unions, so the scale of the campaigns depended on backers the party had cultivated rather than on propaganda alone [A].

However, propaganda only worked because conditions had changed. In the prosperous years the same techniques achieved almost nothing: the Nazis won roughly 3% of the vote in May 1928 and were the seventh largest party in the Reichstag [R]. What changed between 1928 and 1930 was not Nazi communication but German unemployment, which reached 6.1 million by January 1933 [R]. Propaganda amplified a message that hardship had made people willing to hear; it did not create the willingness [A].

There is also a limit visible in the numbers themselves. Even at their peak the Nazis never won a majority, and in November 1932 their share fell to 33% [R]. Hindenburg twice refused to appoint Hitler, and he was appointed in January 1933 at a moment when Nazi support was declining rather than rising [R]. Propaganda could not deliver the 50% that would have made the chancellorship unavoidable [A]. It had also failed once before at the decisive moment: the Munich Putsch of 1923 collapsed despite Hitler's reputation as a speaker, because publicity is no substitute for the support of the army and the police [R].

The decisive factor was the conduct of other politicians. Von Papen and von Schleicher ignored election results in order to govern for the wealthy, von Schleicher asked Hindenburg to suspend the constitution so he could create a military dictatorship, and von Papen then warned Hindenburg on 30 January 1933 that a coup was coming [R]. Von Papen's solution was to make Hitler chancellor with himself as co-chancellor, believing they had Hitler 'in their hand' [R]. No amount of propaganda produced that decision [A].

Propaganda was essential but not sufficient, and the distinction matters. Without it the Nazi Party would have remained a Munich fringe group, because it had no other way of reaching a national audience, and Hitler's personal appeal is the single most important reason the party grew at all [A]. But propaganda's effect depended entirely on circumstances it did not control: it failed in 1928 and succeeded in 1932 with the same methods and the same speaker. Propaganda made the Nazis the largest party, the Depression made that possible, and the miscalculations of Hindenburg, von Papen and von Schleicher turned the largest party into a government [A].

### Q4
Hitler's route to the chancellorship began with the collapse of the German economy. After the Wall Street Crash the USA recalled its loans, German banks lost heavily and called in loans of their own, and businesses cut production or closed [R]. Unemployment reached 4.3 million by September 1931 and 6.1 million by January 1933 [R]. Chancellor Heinrich Bruning, leader of the Centre Party, raised taxes and cut benefits to the unemployed, and by April 1932 had lost the support of the Reichstag; he resigned on 30 May 1932 [R].

The Nazi vote rose alongside the crisis. The NSDAP won 18.3% and 107 seats in 1930, and in the presidential elections of 1932 Hitler took 30% in March and 36% in April, though Hindenburg was re-elected [R]. Goebbels organised the campaigns, funding posters, radio broadcasts and pamphlets and hiring aeroplanes so that Hitler could speak across the country [R]. In the Reichstag election of July 1932 the NSDAP won 38% of the vote and 230 seats to become the largest party, after street fighting between the SA and the KPD's Red Front Fighters left over 100 dead and 7,000 injured [R].

Hindenburg refused to appoint Hitler. He disliked him, regarding a former corporal as unsuitable, so he kept von Papen as chancellor and called another election in November 1932, gambling that Nazi support would fall [R]. Von Papen's government was nicknamed 'the Cabinet of Barons', because the upper classes controlled its inner circle and its policies did not benefit the lower classes; it had no majority and passed policy mainly through Hindenburg's presidential powers, and Hitler had agreed to support it only if the ban on the SA was lifted [R]. Nazi support did fall: the NSDAP's share dropped to 33%, while the KPD rose to 17%, which alarmed moderate and right-wing politicians [R]. Von Schleicher then abandoned the coalition and persuaded Hindenburg that von Papen could not continue, and von Papen resigned [R].

The final stage was a struggle between the men who thought they could manage Hitler. Hindenburg appointed von Schleicher chancellor on 2 December 1932, out of desperation to avoid a Nazi government [R]. Von Schleicher had no majority in the Reichstag and had not been elected, and the Nazis opposed him at every opportunity [D]. He asked Hindenburg to suspend the Weimar Constitution so that he could form a military dictatorship, and Hindenburg refused [R]. Von Papen learned of the plan and contacted Hindenburg on 30 January 1933, warning that von Schleicher would otherwise seize power in a military coup [R]. Von Papen proposed instead that Hitler be made chancellor with himself as co-chancellor, and Hindenburg agreed [R]. Hindenburg and von Papen believed they had Hitler 'in their hand' and would make the real decisions [D]. Hitler became Chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933 [R].

### Q5
Anton Drexler, a railway mechanic from Munich, founded the German Workers' Party, or DAP, in February 1919 [R]. It was a right-wing party that targeted the working classes, and in September 1919 it had just 23 members [R]. Hitler came to it by accident: the Weimar government, worried about the threat new parties posed to public safety, employed him to spy on political parties in Munich, work he needed because the Treaty of Versailles had cut the army to 100,000 men and left most of Germany's soldiers unemployed [R]. He attended his first DAP meeting on 12 September 1919, found that its ideas matched his own, and joined as a member on 19 September, against his government assignment [R].

Hitler rose quickly because of his skill as a speaker. His speeches impressed Drexler, who put him on the party's organising committee and then made him head of propaganda in January 1920 [R]. Hitler practised a distinctive style of oration, increasing his tempo to a frantic and aggressive crescendo, rehearsed his hand gestures and photographed himself doing so [D]. He delivered 31 speeches in his first year as a member, and membership rose from 1,000 in June 1920 to 3,000 by the end of that year [R].

The party was then rebuilt around him. In January 1920 Hitler and Drexler wrote the Twenty-Five Point Programme, which mixed nationalist demands such as Anschluss and the exclusion of Jewish people from citizenship with socialist ones such as jobs for German citizens and a ban on private land ownership [R]. The party was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party to appeal to several groups at once, adopted the swastika and the straight-armed salute to distinguish itself from other nationalist parties, opened a headquarters in Munich, and from December 1920 published a newspaper, the Volkischer Beobachter, distributing 17,000 copies within a year [R].

Hitler completed his takeover in two steps. In July 1921 he triggered a leadership contest and his popularity allowed him to defeat Drexler and take control of the NSDAP [R]. He then appointed his own inner circle, with Rudolf Hess as his deputy, Hermann Goering commanding the SA, Julius Streicher producing the newspaper Der Sturmer and Ernst Rohm linking the SA to the army [R]. The SA itself was created in August 1921 as the party's private army, nicknamed the 'Brownshirts', and numbered 800 men by 1922 [R]. Because the SA was not fully under his control, Hitler also formed the Stosstrupp, or 'Shock Troop', from Brownshirts he selected personally as bodyguards [D]. At a party conference in January 1922 Hitler spoke for two and a half hours and persuaded members to surrender their right to elect the leader, which effectively made the NSDAP a dictatorship [R]. By November 1923 it had 50,000 members [R].

### Q15
**The first facet is that Hindenburg's death removed the last constitutional check on Hitler.** Paul von Hindenburg had been President since 1925, and under the Weimar Constitution the President appointed the chancellor, could dismiss him, and controlled the emergency powers of Article 48. This was not a theoretical restraint. As late as 1934 Hindenburg had threatened to impose martial law if Hitler could not bring the SA under control, and Hitler took that threat seriously enough to act on it. While Hindenburg lived there was one man in Germany with the legal authority to remove Hitler from office. His death on 2 August 1934, from lung cancer at the age of 86, ended that [E].

**The second facet is that it allowed Hitler to merge the two offices.** Rather than allow a new presidential election, Hitler combined the posts of president and chancellor in himself and took the title Führer. This was then put to the public, and 90 per cent voted in favour. The importance here is as much presentational as legal: Hitler could claim that absolute personal rule had been granted by the German people rather than seized, which mattered to a regime that had always been careful to take power through apparently lawful means [E].

**The third facet, and the genuinely new one, is the army oath.** Every soldier was required to swear an oath of loyalty to Hitler personally rather than to the state or the constitution. The Reichswehr was the only institution left in Germany with the physical capacity to overthrow the regime, and this bound it to Hitler as an individual. It also completed a bargain begun five weeks earlier: in the Night of the Long Knives on 30 June 1934 Hitler had had Röhm and around 400 SA members arrested and shot, along with von Schleicher, Gregor Strasser and von Kahr. By destroying the SA leadership, which had wanted to absorb the army, Hitler had bought the generals' goodwill; the oath was what he took in return [E].

**The fourth facet works against the question, because much of the dictatorship was already built.** The Enabling Act had become law on 24 March 1933 by 444 votes to 94, allowing Hitler's cabinet to pass laws without the Reichstag and to overrule the constitution itself. By August 1934 Germany was already a one-party state with no independent parliament, unions or local government. On this reading Hindenburg's death confirmed Hitler's power rather than created it [E].

**Judgement.** Weighing these against one another, the death was less important as a cause of the dictatorship than as its completion, because the decisive legal break had come with the Enabling Act seventeen months earlier [A]. But of the four facets the army oath is the one that added something genuinely new, and it is therefore the most important. Every other source of opposition had already been dismantled; the armed forces had not, and the oath closed the last door through which Hitler could have been removed [A].

The speed of events supports that judgement. The merger of offices and the oath followed within hours of an 86-year-old man's death from a known illness, which indicates the response had been prepared in advance. Hindenburg's death is best understood not as a turning point that changed Hitler's position but as the final scheduled step in a takeover that had been under way since March 1933 [A].
