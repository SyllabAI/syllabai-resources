# Mark Schemes — What was it Like to Live in Nazi Germany?
**Germany: 1918–45** · Cambridge (CIE) IGCSE history (0470 & 0977)


## Exam Questions (20 questions)


### Q1
The Nazis promised to protect German farmers and small businesses, two groups that had voted for them in large numbers. Both got something, and both discovered what Nazi protection cost [R].

The first facet is what farmers gained, which was real. The Nazis believed in 'Blood and Soil', and Hitler spoke of true Aryans having farming ancestry, so farmers were valued far more highly than urban workers [R]. The Reich Food Estate regulated the food market and offered farmers guaranteed customers at fair prices, while the Reich Entailed Farm Law protected them by preventing banks from repossessing property if they fell into debt [R]. This mattered because farming had been the worst-hit sector of the Weimar economy, with agricultural incomes 44% below the national average by 1929, so guaranteed prices and protection from repossession addressed the two things that had ruined farmers [E]. Autarky gave the countryside a further political importance, since a Germany that fed itself could not be starved by a naval blockade as it had been in the First World War [R].

The second facet is that the same measures took away farmers' independence. The Nazis increased their control and told farmers what produce to grow, which caused anger in farming communities [R]. The protection from repossession also made banks reluctant to lend to farmers at all, so a law meant to help them cut off their access to credit [A]. Farming communities remained poor, and many farmers' children moved to towns and cities for better wages [R]. From 1936 the Four Year Plan added a further layer of direction, with Goering re-establishing the Reich Food Estate to guarantee prices while pressing for higher farming output in pursuit of autarky [R]. A farmer by 1939 had a guaranteed price and almost no say in what he grew [A].

The third facet is what businesses gained. The banning of trade unions and strikes removed the threat of workers turning to communism and made the workforce more productive [R]. Rearmament then created enormous opportunities, with the government offering large contracts for war goods such as steel, and armaments spending rising from 3.5 billion marks in 1933 to 26 billion by 1939 [R]. For any business connected to rearmament this was transformative, which is why corporations such as Thyssen, Krupp and Bosch had funded the party in the first place [E].

Businesses also lost their independence. The Nazis increased their control over what businesses produced, setting production targets and taking charge of iron ore, coal mining, steel and armaments under the Four Year Plan from 1936 [R]. Goering, who had no experience in economics, replaced Schacht as Minister of the Economy, and many Nazis and business leaders did not support the plan [R]. Firms outside rearmament suffered, because fewer businesses were producing consumer goods [A]. The pursuit of autarky pushed industry towards ersatz substitutes, using coal to make rubber and acorns to make coffee, and business leaders objected that excessive rearmament was lowering Germany's standard of living in what became known as a 'guns, not butter' economy [R]. By 1944 a quarter of the workforce was enslaved labour drawn from concentration camps and occupied territory, so even the firms that prospered did so on terms set entirely by the state [E].

Small businesses were also straightforwardly deceived. The Nazis had promised small business owners that they would restrict the larger department stores, and the government simply did not do it, so large businesses continued to dominate the market [R]. The group that had been most attracted by Nazi promises was the group whose promise was broken [A]. Small shopkeepers had no organisation left to complain through either, since the banning of unions and parties in 1933 removed every channel of protest [E].

Farmers and businesses benefited to a limited extent, and the pattern is the same for both. Each received genuine material help, guaranteed prices for farmers and government contracts for industry, and each paid for it with the loss of the right to make its own decisions [A]. The difference lies in who was inside the rearmament economy: heavy industry did extremely well, while farmers, small shopkeepers and consumer-goods manufacturers did not. Nazi rule benefited whoever was useful to rearmament and left everyone else with control instead of prosperity [A].

### Q2
Nazi Germany surrendered in May 1945, twelve years after Hitler became Chancellor, having lost a war it started. Whether the war caused the collapse or merely delivered it is one of the genuine disagreements among historians [R].

The first facet is military defeat, which is the most obvious cause. Germany defeated France in six weeks and forced the British army out of Europe, and support for the Nazi Party remained high even in 1941 [R]. Operation Barbarossa then failed through poor tactics and planning, and the defeat at Stalingrad in 1942 showed that this would not be the quick war Germans had been promised [R]. By 1944 Germany faced a war on two fronts it could not win, and in April 1945 Hitler and Goebbels killed themselves [R]. This matters because Nazi legitimacy rested on success: a regime that had justified dictatorship by restoring German strength could not survive visible defeat [E].

The second facet is the collapse of support at home. Rationing was extended and black markets appeared, Britain's bombing caused high casualties and falling living standards, and during 'Total War' women were forced to work, children were evacuated and entertainment and luxury goods were stopped [R]. Goebbels ran a propaganda campaign in 1943 and it failed to halt the decline, and an increasing number of Germans began to oppose the regime [R]. The July 1944 plot came from inside the army itself [A]. In the final stage Hitler created the Volkssturm, a Home Guard of conscripted young and elderly men which failed in battle, and the bombing of Dresden in February 1945 caused very heavy civilian casualties [R]. A regime reduced to conscripting twelve-year-olds had already lost the consent it began with [E].

The third facet is that the German economy was never built for the war it fought. Germany had not achieved autarky and still imported one-third of its raw materials in 1939, and Albert Speer only converted the country to a war economy in 1942, by which point it was too late [R]. There were shortages of fuel, coal and food, Germany relied on plundering occupied countries, and by 1944 a quarter of the workforce was enslaved labour [R]. During Operation Barbarossa there were not even enough supplies to use blitzkrieg tactics properly against the USSR, and battles steadily consumed what war supplies remained [E].

However, some historians argue the regime was already failing before 1939. On this view Germany's economy was in trouble by 1939, causing discontent, and Hitler brought forward his plans to invade Poland precisely in order to win back public support [R]. Rearmament had produced a 'guns, not butter' economy that lowered living standards, business leaders had not supported the Four Year Plan, and by 1939 food prices had risen 20% so unskilled workers were worse off, while the Volkswagen scheme took five marks a week from workers who never received a car [R]. If that is right, the war was a symptom of the regime's weakness rather than its cause [A].

Against this, the evidence of pre-war strength is substantial. Four million people joined the Nazi Party between 1933 and 1939, unemployment fell from 4.8 million in 1933 to 300,000 by 1939, and 90% voted for Hitler to become Fuhrer in 1934 [R]. Other historians therefore argue that support was strong before the war and that only the experience of total war weakened the regime [A]. There was also no organised opposition capable of removing the Nazis in 1939: parties and unions had been banned in 1933 and the Night of the Long Knives had destroyed internal rivals [R].

The Second World War was decisive, but the two explanations are less far apart than they look. The regime was not on the point of collapse in 1939: it had popular support, low official unemployment and no organised opposition capable of removing it [A]. Yet its economy was already distorted by rearmament and dependent on aggression to sustain itself, which meant the war it needed was also the war it could not survive. The Nazi state was built in a way that made war necessary and defeat fatal, so the war did not simply end the regime, it completed a contradiction that had been there from the beginning [A].

### Q3
Nazi policy towards women had two clear aims: to remove women from work and to raise the birth rate. By 1939 the regime had partly achieved both, and by 1944 it had abandoned the first entirely [R].

The first facet is marriage and the birth rate, where the results were genuinely positive for the Nazis. The Law for the Encouragement of Marriage of 1933 offered loans of 1,000 marks, roughly eight months' wages, to women who married and left work, with a quarter cancelled for each child [R]. Around 700,000 couples took out marriage loans within four years, marriages rose from 500,000 in 1932 to 750,000 by 1934, and the birth rate rose from 15 per 1,000 in 1933 to 20 per 1,000 in 1939 [R]. This was a real success because the birth rate had been the Nazis' central demographic worry, and the policy reversed a decline rather than merely slowing it [E].

The second facet is the removal of women from employment, which worked at first. Women were banned from being teachers, doctors and other professionals in 1933, and around 19,000 female civil servants were sacked; in 1936 they were barred from becoming judges or lawyers; and in 1937 schools preparing girls for university were closed, so 11,000 fewer women were starting university by 1939 [R]. Around 360,000 women had left their jobs by 1934 [R]. The Nazis were explicit that this was the point: women were to stay at home and care for the family while the man earned the money [R]. Measured against the aim, this was effective: the Nazis wanted women at home and large numbers of women went home [E].

The third facet is the organisations, which reached enormous numbers but were resented. The German Women's Enterprise had six million members and 1.7 million women attended its courses in childcare, cooking and sewing, and three million women were eligible for the Mother's Cross in 1939 [R]. Yet many women disliked Scholtz-Klink and found the DFW demeaning [A]. Reach is not the same as enthusiasm, and a policy that produces compliance while generating resentment has only half succeeded [A]. The regime also prescribed how women should look, expecting blonde plaited hair, athleticism, no make-up, flat heels and no smoking, and Goebbels declared that 'the mission of the woman is to be beautiful and bring children into the home' [R]. Judged as an attempt to remake women's lives around the 'three Ks' of Kinder, Kirche and Kuche, the policy went far beyond employment law and reached into appearance and behaviour, which is why resentment matters as evidence [E].

Several policies also failed outright. Only around 7,000 children were born through the Lebensborn Programme, despite it running from 1935 [R]. Few mothers had more than two children, so the Mother's Cross rewarded a minority rather than changing behaviour [R]. The divorce laws of 1938, which let husbands divorce wives who would not have children, increased the divorce rate and so contradicted the Nazis' own rhetoric about family unity [A]. A regime that claimed to be restoring the family had written a law that broke families up [E].

Most damagingly, the central policy was reversed. By 1937 Germany faced a national shortage of workers, and women holding marriage loans were allowed to work after all [R]. In 1944 the government forced women into war work, 2.4 million returned to their jobs, and over 500,000 served in the Wehrmacht as nurses or in other non-combat roles [R]. Some resented the loss of the professional roles they had been pushed out of a decade earlier [E]. Even before the war women's employment rates never returned to their 1930 levels, so the reversal did not restore what the bans had removed [R].

Nazi policies towards women succeeded only to a limited extent, and the limits were self-inflicted. On the birth rate and on marriage the Nazis achieved measurable change, and in the peacetime years they largely got the domestic role they wanted [A]. But the employment policy was never sustainable, because a regime committed to rearmament and then to total war could not also spare half its workforce. The Nazis were forced to choose between their ideology about women and their ambitions abroad, and they chose the ambitions. That reversal is the clearest evidence that the policy failed on its own terms [A].

### Q4
Reducing unemployment was one of the Nazis' first priorities. Around six million Germans were unemployed by 1932 because of the Great Depression, and five million were still out of work when Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933 [R]. The Nazis regarded the unemployed as a political danger, because they might support parties such as the KPD, and as a group contributing nothing to the economy [D].

Public works came first. The National Labour Service was set up in 1933 to provide paid work repairing roads and planting trees, and from 1935 it was compulsory for unemployed young men aged 18 to 25 to serve six months; it had 422,000 members in 1935, although pay was poor and workers complained about the food, the uniforms and the conditions [R]. Hitler personally started the autobahn project in September 1933, intending a network of around 11,000 kilometres; it employed 125,000 workers by 1935 and around 3,500 kilometres had been built by 1938 [R]. Spending on public works, including roads, bridges and the Berlin Olympic Stadium, more than doubled from 18 billion marks in 1933 to 38 billion by 1938 [R].

The economy was then reorganised. Hitler appointed Dr Schacht, a respected banker who had helped resolve the hyperinflation crisis of 1923, as Minister of the Economy [R]. Schacht's New Plan of 1934 cut welfare spending, invested in industry and created trade deals with countries such as Hungary, and by 1936 the economy had recovered [R].

Rearmament provided the largest number of jobs. Hitler announced conscription in 1935 in breach of the Treaty of Versailles, and by 1939 there were around 1.4 million men in the German armed forces [R]. Spending on armaments rose from 3.5 billion marks in 1933 to 26 billion by 1939, and employment in aircraft construction rose from 4,000 people in 1933 to 72,000 by 1935 [R]. Official unemployment fell year by year, from 4,800,000 in 1933 to 2,700,000 in 1934, 2,100,000 in 1935, 1,500,000 in 1936, 900,000 in 1937, 500,000 in 1938 and 300,000 by 1939 [R]. From 1936 Goering's Four Year Plan took the process further, setting production targets for industry and creating the Hermann Goering Works as centres for heavy industry [R].

The figures were not what they appeared. The Nazis excluded whole groups from the statistics, a practice known as invisible unemployment: part-time workers were counted as fully employed, and the armed forces, prisoners, Jewish people forced out of their jobs and women who had left work were all left out of the count [R]. The schemes were also expensive and hard to sustain as long-term policies, and rearmament only created jobs so long as Germany was preparing for war [D]. Wages rose for most workers through the 1930s, but so did working hours, and by 1939 food prices had risen by 20%, so unskilled workers were worse off despite being counted as employed [R].

### Q5
Nazi policy towards women aimed to reverse the changes of the Weimar Republic and to raise the birth rate, which had fallen to one million births a year by 1933 [R]. Goebbels said that 'the mission of the woman is to be beautiful and bring children into the home', and the Nazis expected women to marry 'pure' Aryan men, raise large families, stay at home while the man earned, and appear blonde, athletic and without make-up [R]. A woman's life was said to revolve around the 'three Ks' of Kinder, Kirche and Kuche [D]. Gertrud Scholtz-Klink was appointed leader of the National Socialist Women's League and oversaw all policies relating to women [R].

Organisations were unified and then used to train women. All women-related organisations had to join the German Women's Enterprise, and any that refused were banned [R]. The DFW enrolled members on courses in childcare, cooking and sewing, and by the late 1930s it had six million members, of whom 1.7 million had attended its courses [R]. Around 360,000 women had left their jobs by 1934, embracing the domestic role the Nazis wanted [R].

A series of laws encouraged marriage and childbirth. The Law for the Encouragement of Marriage of 1933 offered loans of 1,000 marks, about eight months' wages, to women who married and either quit their jobs or stayed unemployed, with a quarter of the loan cancelled for each child, so a mother of four repaid nothing [R]. Himmler began the Lebensborn Programme in 1935, pairing SS men with single women of clear Aryan ancestry to produce children the Nazis regarded as genetically pure [R]. New divorce laws in 1938 let husbands divorce wives who failed or refused to have children or who had abortions, and the Mother's Cross, awarded on Mother's Day from 1938, honoured women with four or more children, with gold medallists receiving a public salute from the Hitler Youth [R]. Families with ten children had to name Hitler as godfather to the tenth child, and by 1939 three million women were eligible for a Mother's Cross [R].

Employment was restricted at the same time. In 1933 women were banned from being teachers, doctors and other professionals, and around 19,000 female civil servants were sacked [R]. In 1936 they were banned from becoming judges or lawyers or from serving on juries, and in 1937 schools preparing girls for university were banned, so around 11,000 fewer women were starting university by 1939 [R]. ### Q6
The Nazis wanted control of Germany's young people because Hitler intended a Third Reich that would last a thousand years. Adults held mixed opinions and were difficult to change, whereas control of the young would guarantee support for generations [R]. Boys were to be raised fit and healthy to support the economy and fight in wars, and girls fit and healthy so that they could be good wives and healthy mothers [D].

Membership was built up quickly and then made compulsory. Other youth groups were banned and membership of the Nazi organisations was eventually required, so numbers rose from 100,000 in 1932 to nine million by 1939 [R]. ### Q15
**The first facet is the loss of independent rights.** Hitler distrusted trade unions because they supported left-wing parties and organised strikes that disrupted the economy, and he used the powers of the Enabling Act to ban them all in May 1933. In their place came the Labour Front, or DAF, which controlled workers' rights, hours and pay. The consequences were immediate: workers lost the right to negotiate over wages and conditions, the maximum working week was extended by six hours, and strikes were forbidden, with those who disrupted production punished. Whatever else happened to German workers after 1933, it happened to them without their having any means of objecting [E].

**The second facet is the compensation the regime offered in return.** The Nazis knew that abolishing the unions would cause resentment, so they created organisations within the DAF to raise living standards. Strength Through Joy, founded in 1933, provided subsidised leisure, trips and holidays and had 35 million members by 1936. Beauty of Labour, founded in 1934, improved workplace facilities such as toilets, changing rooms, showers and canteens, and around 34,000 companies had upgraded by 1938. Yet both schemes reveal the limits of the bargain. Workers were expected to build and decorate the new facilities themselves, unpaid and outside working hours. And the Volkswagen scheme, under which workers paid five marks a week towards a car costing 999 Reichsmark, ended with the factories switching to armaments after 1938 and not a single worker receiving a car [E].

**The third facet is employment itself, which is the strongest argument in the regime's favour.** Unemployment stood at 4.8 million in 1933 and had fallen to around 300,000 by 1939. The National Labour Service provided paid public work such as road repair and tree planting, becoming compulsory for unemployed men aged 18 to 25 from 1935 and reaching 422,000 members. The autobahn programme, begun by Hitler in September 1933, employed 125,000 workers by 1935 and had completed some 3500 kilometres by 1938, while spending on public works rose from 18 billion marks in 1933 to 38 billion by 1938. For a man who had been out of work in 1932, this was a transformation [E].

**The fourth facet is the gap between wages and prices, which divided workers sharply.** Most workers did earn more under the Nazis, and skilled men in the armaments industry did particularly well. But food prices rose by 20 per cent by 1939, and for the unskilled the wage increases did not keep pace. The impact of Nazi rule therefore depended a great deal on what job a worker did: the same policies that enriched a skilled engineer left an unskilled labourer worse off than before [E].

**The fifth facet is that the headline achievement was partly manufactured.** Nazi unemployment figures excluded several large groups: part-time workers were counted as fully employed, as were the armed forces and those making armaments, while prisoners, Jewish people forced out of their jobs and women pushed out of work were left out of the statistics altogether. This is invisible unemployment, and it means the fall from 4.8 million to 300,000 overstates what was achieved [E].

**Judgement.** The most important impact was the first: the removal of any independent means of representation [A]. Everything else follows from it. Wages, hours and conditions all became things the regime granted rather than things workers could win, and the Volkswagen scheme demonstrates exactly what that meant in practice, since men who had paid in for years had no way of recovering their money when the state decided the factories were needed for something else.

The employment gains were nevertheless real and should not be dismissed, even allowing for invisible unemployment [A]. The deeper problem is what they rested on. Rearmament created jobs only because Germany was preparing for war, and armaments spending rose from 3.5 billion marks in 1933 to 26 billion by 1939, with employment in aircraft construction rising from 4,000 in 1933 to 72,000 by 1935. The impact of Nazi rule on workers was therefore a trade: secure work and modest improvements in daily conditions, bought with the loss of every right to bargain, and underwritten by an economy that had to go to war to keep paying [A].
