### Q1
The March 1917 Revolution succeeded because popular anger, an army that defected, and an organised alternative to the tsar all arrived together [R].

Popular support was genuine and widespread. The war had caused severe fuel and food shortages, and huge casualties made people less patriotic and less willing to support the tsar as head of the army [R]. Industrial workers were less religious, so belief in the tsar's divine right weakened, and more turned to Marxist parties, while Tsarina Alexandra was mockingly called the 'German Princess' [R]. This mattered because the tsar had lost legitimacy before a single soldier disobeyed an order [E].

However, popular anger alone had failed before. The 1905 Revolution also began with mass strikes and popular fury, yet Nicholas survived by using the army to suppress the general strike [A]. Popular support alone could not remove an autocrat who still controlled the means of force [A].

Military support proved decisive in 1917. Nicholas had over-relied on the army as in 1905, but the war had turned it against him, since soldiers no longer respected his leadership after the defeats he had personally commanded [R]. On 12 March soldiers ordered to stop the protests joined the strikers instead, and many joined the Soviets [R]. Without the army, the tsar lost the one tool that had saved him twelve years earlier [E].

The Petrograd Soviet then gave the revolution somewhere for power to go. It took control of supplies on 12 March, and after the abdication a system of Dual Power emerged, in which the Provisional Government needed the Soviet's influence over the army, while the Soviet needed the Government's influence over the middle classes [R]. The revolution did not simply remove the tsar; it had an organisation ready to replace him [A].

Military support was the most important factor, because it was the missing piece that had let the tsar survive an identical crisis in 1905. Popular anger created the pressure and the Soviet gave it an organised outlet, but it was the army joining the crowds on 12 March that made the tsar's position impossible [A]. Every other factor had been present in some form in 1905 and had not been enough alone.

### Q3
In 1913 the Romanov dynasty appeared secure, celebrating three hundred years of rule. By March 1917 it had collapsed entirely. The First World War sits at the centre of every explanation of that collapse, and the question is how much weight it should carry alongside Russia's older problems [R].

The first facet is military failure, and its scale was extraordinary. Russia mobilised 5.3 million men within ten days, yet 800,000 lacked rifles and factories could produce only 13,000 rounds of ammunition a day [R]. Defeats at Tannenberg and Masurian Lakes in 1914 cost over 150,000 casualties between them, and by 1916 over 1.7 million Russians had died with more than a million deserting [R]. This mattered because Nicholas took personal command of the army in 1915 despite having no military experience, so every subsequent defeat attached directly to him rather than to a general who could be dismissed [E]. Even the one clear success, the Brusilov Offensive of 1916, destroyed 1.5 million Austro-Hungarian troops but cost Russia 500,000 of its own men, so it turned opinion against the war rather than reviving support for it [R].

The second facet is the economic collapse the war produced. Russia had entered the war with the largest gold reserve in Europe, but inflation reached 200%, real wages fell, and millions of conscripted peasants left farming production to collapse [R]. The railway system could not distribute what food there was, so it rotted at stations while cities starved, producing over 1,300 strikes by 1917 [R]. A population that could not eat had little reason left to remain loyal [E]. Even government support for widows and orphans of soldiers largely failed to materialise, adding a further sense of betrayal among families who had already lost the most [R].

The third facet is the vacuum in government that Nicholas created himself. By commanding the army in person he left Tsarina Alexandra in charge of Petrograd, and she relied on Rasputin's advice, dismissing ministers erratically and scandalising the Orthodox Church, one of the tsar's own 'pillars' of support [R]. Aristocrats murdered Rasputin in December 1916 to save the monarchy, but the damage to its reputation could not be undone by then [A].

However, some historians argue the war only dictated timing rather than cause. On this view Tsarist Russia had unresolvable problems regardless of the war: an economy that relied on backward peasant agriculture, a geography spanning 5,000 kilometres with only 40% of the population ethnically Russian, and a political system with no elected parliament worth the name after the Dumas were rigged [R]. These structural weaknesses would have produced a revolution eventually, and the war simply accelerated it [A].

The events of 1905 support this reading. Russia had already experienced a near-revolution over economic hardship, military humiliation and a discredited government, and Nicholas survived only through the October Manifesto and repression, both of which he later undermined by rigging the Duma [R]. A regime that had already needed rescuing once, and had wasted the rescue, was fragile before a single shot was fired in 1914 [A].

Against this, the war's specific damage is hard to explain away. The tsar's personal command of the army was his own decision, not a structural inevitability, and it directly transferred the blame for military failure onto the monarchy in a way 1905 never had [R]. Rasputin's influence, Alexandra's German background and the murder of Rasputin by the tsar's own supporters were all products of the war years specifically, and none of them existed as active grievances before 1914 [E]. Nicholas's decision to rename St Petersburg to Petrograd in 1914, distancing the dynasty from its own capital's Germanic name, shows how directly the war reshaped what Russians associated with their rulers [R].

The war was decisive, but decisive in combination with what came before it. Russia's structural weaknesses, its backward economy, its rigid social hierarchy and its rigged Duma, meant the regime had no reserves of trust or competence left to draw on once war exposed its failures [A]. The First World War did not create the fault lines in Tsarist Russia; it applied a pressure the regime's own history had already made unbearable. The war was the immediate and necessary cause of the collapse in 1917, but it succeeded only because thirty years of political failure had left nothing else standing [A].

### Q4
Peter Stolypin governed Russia from 1906 to 1911 using what historians call a 'carrot and stick' approach: reward loyalty, punish resistance. Whether that combination actually secured the tsarist regime is a genuine historical debate [R].

The first facet is the 'carrot': land reform aimed at the peasantry. Stolypin introduced reforms intended to increase peasant support, offering loans through the Peasant Land Bank so more peasants could buy land and become kulaks, wealthier independent farmers [R]. He reduced the power of the mirs over individual peasants and required them to reallocate vacant land within the commune [R]. This mattered because peasants made up 80% of the population, so winning their loyalty, or at least their acquiescence, was essential to any government hoping to survive [E]. Stolypin's reforms followed directly from the unrest of 1905, when peasants had set fire to landowners' properties in what became known as the Years of the Red Cockerel, so the 'carrot' was designed specifically to answer that grievance [R].

The second facet is the 'stick': repression of opponents. Stolypin harshly punished those who resisted the tsar, executing so many through military field courts that people called the noose 'Stolypin's Necktie', with over 1,000 people hanged and 20,000 exiled [R]. He also used the Okhrana, the secret police, to stop riots and investigate revolutionary movements [R]. This was significant because it demonstrated that reform under Stolypin was never intended to be a genuine transfer of power: consent was to be bought where possible and forced where it could not [E]. Stolypin's repression fell within a wider pattern set by the tsar himself, since the Fundamental Laws of 1906 had already reasserted Nicholas's right to rule by decree, so Stolypin was applying a philosophy of control that the monarchy had already committed itself to [R].

However, the land reforms achieved very little. Historians note that only a small number of peasants ever became kulaks, and poorer peasants, who could not afford the Peasant Land Bank's loans, only became poorer relative to them, deepening resentment within peasant communities rather than easing it [R]. A policy meant to create a loyal class of prosperous farmers instead created a new grievance among those left behind [A].

The repression also failed to produce lasting stability. Workers' conditions did not improve: their pay after the 1905 Revolution was lower in real terms than it had been in 1903, and strikes continued regardless of the risk of execution or exile [R]. In 1912, a year after Stolypin's own assassination, the government brutally repressed strikes at the Lena Goldfields, showing that force alone had not ended labour unrest [R]. If repression worked, such a strike, and such a violent response to it, would not have been necessary years later [A]. Trade unions remained illegal throughout the period, and the government still had no laws governing working hours, pay or conditions, so the underlying causes of labour unrest were never addressed at all, only their outward expression [R].

Stolypin's approach also could not survive contact with the tsar's own instincts. Stolypin himself recognised that Russia needed twenty years of peace to complete his reforms, yet Nicholas's government pursued an assertive foreign policy that contributed to the crisis of 1914, cutting off the time Stolypin's programme needed [R]. A long-term social policy was undermined by decisions taken entirely outside its control [A]. Stolypin was himself assassinated in 1911, three years before the war began, which meant the reforms lost their principal architect even before external events overtook them [R].

Stolypin's methods therefore secured the regime only in the short term and failed in the long term, as historians generally agree. The 'carrot' created a small class of kulaks while leaving the majority of peasants no better off, and in some cases worse, and the 'stick' suppressed individual acts of resistance without addressing why they kept recurring [A]. Neither method resolved the underlying problem that Russia's peasants and workers had genuine, unaddressed grievances, so unrest persisted after Stolypin's assassination in 1911 and resumed on a larger scale once the pressures of the First World War were added. Stolypin bought the tsarist regime time rather than security, and when that time ran out in 1914, nothing durable had been built to replace it [A].

### Q6
Russian society under the tsar was rigidly hierarchical. Power ran from the tsar, through the Orthodox Church and army officers, down to the bourgeoisie and finally the proletariat, who had the least power of all [R].

Peasants made up around 80% of the population and were poorly educated [R]. The Emancipation Edict of 1861 had freed them from serfdom and allowed some to buy land through the Peasant Land Bank, but only a small number, known as kulaks, could afford to do so, and many poorer peasants resented them [R]. Land passed down through generations by inheritance, so by 1905 family plots were often too small or scattered around the mir, and peasants lacked the equipment or techniques to farm efficiently [D]. More than 60% of Russians were illiterate, compared with only 3% in Britain, and the empire relied on peasants as soldiers for the army [R].

Industrial workers were far fewer, at less than 5% of the population, but lived in some of the worst conditions in the empire [R]. Russian cities offered substandard housing and poor sanitation, so diseases such as cholera spread rapidly [D]. There were no laws governing working hours, pay or conditions, and trade unions were illegal, so workers had almost no way to improve their situation through legal means [R].

Russia's industry itself was underdeveloped: unlike Britain and Germany, which had industrialised in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and by 1905 produced railways, motorised tractors and alloy steel, Russia mostly produced pig iron and its workers lacked the skills to match Western production [R]. The bourgeoisie, industrialists and business owners, made up less than 2% of the population but were a growing class [R]. They had a great deal of money but very little political power, since the tsar's government gave no formal role to businessmen [D]. This mismatch between their economic importance and political powerlessness pushed many towards liberal ideas and, eventually, towards demanding constitutional reform through the zemstva [R].

The empire's geography deepened these divisions further. It stretched nearly 5,000 kilometres and only 40% of the population was ethnic Russian, with large Ukrainian, Polish, Jewish and other minority populations, many of whom wanted independence or resented policies such as Russification [R]. The Ural Mountains divided the industrialised west, where Nicholas lived in St Petersburg, from Siberia in the east, which had limited transport links to the rest of the empire [R]. Communication and legal enforcement were difficult across such distances, so the Orthodox Church, which taught that the tsar was God's representative on Earth, became essential for enforcing the tsar's laws among a largely illiterate population, with priests reading new laws aloud to their congregations [R]. In theory a government of an Imperial Council, a Cabinet of Ministers and a Senate sat beneath the tsar, but none of these bodies held real power: their members were appointed by the tsar himself and existed only to advise or administer, since there was no elected parliament until 1906 [D].
