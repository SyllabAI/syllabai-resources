### Q1
Lenin is often credited as the single figure most responsible for the Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917, but several other factors also mattered a great deal in explaining exactly why the Bolsheviks succeeded when they did [R].

The first facet is Lenin's ideological leadership. Although he had been exiled and missed the March Revolution, he returned to Russia in April 1917, funded by Germany and travelling on the sealed train, and immediately delivered the April Theses, demanding peace, the end of rationing, land redistribution and 'All power to the Soviets'. These slogans gave the Bolsheviks a clear and popular message at a time when other parties still supported continuing the war, and they were chanted by workers during the July Days. This mattered because it gave the party a distinct identity that set it apart from its rivals months before October arrived, at a time when the Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries still shared power with the Provisional Government and offered no such clear alternative [E].

The second facet is Lenin's personal insistence on timing. Even after the Bolsheviks won a majority in the Petrograd Soviet by September, other leading Bolsheviks, including Zinoviev and Kamenev, argued against an armed uprising, fearing it was too risky and premature. Lenin returned to Petrograd in disguise on 7 October and personally persuaded the Central Committee to commit to a seizure of power on 10 October. Zinoviev and Kamenev were so opposed to the plan that they later criticised it openly once it succeeded, showing genuine division within the leadership that only Lenin's insistence overcame. Without his pressure, the Bolsheviks might have delayed and let the opportunity created by the Kornilov Affair slip away entirely [E].

The third facet is Trotsky's organisational role, which was arguably just as important. As chairman of the Petrograd Soviet from September, Trotsky reported that fifteen of eighteen army units in the city supported the Bolsheviks, and it was his Military Revolutionary Committee that coordinated the actual seizure of the bridges, the post office and the telephone exchange on the night of 24 to 25 October. Lenin provided the ideology and the decision to act, but Trotsky provided the machinery that carried the plan out, coordinating the Military Revolutionary Committee that actually directed soldiers and Red Guards to their targets across the city that night [E].

The fourth facet is the weakness of the Provisional Government itself. Kerensky's decision to arm the Bolshevik Red Guard during the Kornilov Affair in September gave the party weapons it never returned, and his government's failures over land, the war and Dual Power had already destroyed its support among workers, soldiers and peasants long before October. When Kerensky tried to shut down Bolshevik newspapers on 23 October, loyal soldiers simply ignored his order, showing how little authority the government retained by that point, only a month after Kerensky had himself armed the very Red Guard now defying him [E].

These facets are closely connected. Lenin's April Theses had built the popular support that made soldiers willing to defect to the Bolsheviks, but that support only became real power because Trotsky turned it into an organised force and because Kerensky's own mistakes had left the government almost undefended by October. Lenin's leadership was necessary to create the conditions for October, but it was not sufficient on its own to explain the eventual outcome, since a popular message and a determined leader could still have failed against a government capable of defending itself [A].

On balance, Lenin's leadership was important but not the most important single factor [A]. His insistence on acting on 10 October was decisive in ensuring the Bolsheviks moved when they did rather than hesitating further, but the government's own collapse after the Kornilov Affair meant that Petrograd was left almost undefended regardless of exactly who led the Bolsheviks that autumn. Lenin decided that the moment should be seized, and his refusal to let the party hesitate after Zinoviev and Kamenev's objections was genuinely decisive. Yet it was the Provisional Government's accumulated weakness, exploited skilfully by Trotsky's organisation on the ground, that made seizing power in Petrograd so easy to achieve with so little bloodshed [A].

### Q2
The Provisional Government came to power in March 1917 as a temporary solution while Russia moved towards democracy, but by October it had lost all authority. Several linked problems combined to destroy it [R].

The first facet is Dual Power. From the start the government had to share authority with the Petrograd Soviet, which issued Soviet Order No.1 telling the army to obey the Soviet rather than the government. As extremist parties like the Bolsheviks gained representation within the Soviet, it became an increasingly hostile rival, encouraging strikes and undermining every decision the government tried to take. This mattered because it meant the government could never govern with full authority, since real power in the capital was split between two competing bodies from the very beginning, and every reform depended on a rival institution's cooperation rather than the government's own decisions [E].

The second facet is the government's failure to act on land and elections. Peasants wanted landowners' estates redistributed, but the government feared taking such a major decision before it had a democratic mandate, and it never set a date for the elections that would give it one. By mid-1917 peasants stopped waiting and began seizing land illegally across the countryside. This was damaging because the peasantry made up the great majority of Russia's population, and their patience with a government that promised elections and reform but delivered neither eventually ran out entirely, undermining any claim it had to represent ordinary Russians [E].

The third facet is the government's decision to continue the First World War. It needed British and French investment and hoped a victory would strengthen its position, but this put it directly at odds with the Petrograd Soviet, which declared in March that it could no longer support the war. Kerensky's June Offensive against Austrian troops failed badly, producing heavy casualties and rising desertion, and soldiers turned increasingly to Bolshevik propaganda rather than their own government. A government that kept an unpopular war going while failing to win it lost the loyalty of the very army it depended on for its survival [E].

The fourth facet is the July Days. Food shortages, the failure of the June Offensive and the resignation of ministers combined to spark mass protests in Petrograd between 3 and 6 July, joined by the Kronstadt sailors and chanting Bolshevik slogans. Although Lenin refused to support the unrest and fled to Finland once the government cracked down and arrested key Bolsheviks, including Trotsky, the protests showed how fragile the government's support had already become only four months after the Tsar's abdication, and its violent response alienated many workers still further [E].

The fifth facet is the Kornilov Affair. By September, with the government failing on every front, Kerensky appointed General Kornilov to command the army, but when Kornilov marched towards Petrograd, Kerensky panicked, freed the Bolsheviks his own government had imprisoned in July, and armed the Red Guard to defend the city. The Bolsheviks helped stop Kornilov by persuading his troops to desert, and although Kornilov was arrested, the Red Guard never returned its weapons. This was the most damaging single episode because Kerensky's own decision handed his most determined enemies both freedom and firearms in the space of a few days, a mistake none of the government's other failures came close to matching in its consequences [E].

These facets connect closely. The government's failures on land and the war had already convinced peasants and soldiers, many of whom were peasants in uniform, that it could not be trusted, which is part of why so many were willing to accept Bolshevik rifles once Kornilov threatened Petrograd, and why the Red Guard felt no obligation to disarm itself afterwards [A].

On balance, the Kornilov Affair was the most important reason for the collapse of the government's authority [A]. Dual Power, the war and the land question had all steadily eroded its support over many months, but none of them put weapons directly into Bolshevik hands. It was Kerensky's own panic in September that armed the Red Guard and destroyed what remained of his authority over the army, converting a slow, months-long decline into the specific military weakness that let the Bolsheviks seize power only seven weeks later [A].

### Q5
Historians widely agree that the October Revolution looked nothing like the popular uprising the Bolsheviks later claimed it to be [R]. In 1928 the Bolsheviks commissioned Sergei Eisenstein to make a film showing crowds storming the gates of the Winter Palace under heavy gunfire. In reality the gates were unguarded and open, and the remaining ministers of the Provisional Government offered almost no resistance at all to the Bolshevik takeover of the capital [E].

The events themselves support the idea of a coup rather than a revolution [R]. By September the Bolsheviks already held a majority in the Petrograd Soviet, with Trotsky as chairman, and small, organised groups seized the bridges, the post office and other key buildings overnight on 24 and 25 October. Only around six people died taking the Winter Palace. This was a carefully planned seizure by a committed and well-organised minority, not thousands of ordinary Russians spontaneously rising up together in the streets, as they had during the popular revolution of March 1917 [A].

There is also a case for calling the October Revolution more popular, however [R]. Lenin's April Theses, delivered in April 1917, demanded peace, bread, land and 'All power to the Soviets', and these slogans won genuine mass support among workers and soldiers exhausted by the war and food shortages. They were chanted by protesters during the July Days, showing that Bolshevik ideas already commanded a real popular following months before October arrived [E].

That popularity continued to grow through the autumn [R]. The Kornilov Affair in September forced Kerensky to arm the Bolshevik Red Guard to defend Petrograd, and the party's role in helping stop Kornilov's advance won it further credibility with workers and soldiers who feared a right-wing takeover. The Bolsheviks could not have acted so confidently in October without knowing that resistance to their takeover would be minimal, and Tsereteli, the Menshevik leader, publicly predicted that Bolshevik power would not last three weeks, which shows that even the government's own opponents saw it at the time as a small, temporary putsch rather than a genuine mass movement [E].

On balance, the statement is true to a large extent [A]. The Bolsheviks did not have control of the whole of Russia in October 1917 and relied on genuine wider support to survive the months that followed, but the seizure of Petrograd itself was the disciplined, planned action of a minority party rather than a spontaneous popular rising, which is exactly why most historians today choose to describe October 1917 as a coup [A].
