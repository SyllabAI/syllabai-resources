# Mark Schemes — Was the Treaty of Versailles Fair?
**The 20th Century: International Relations from 1919** · Cambridge (CIE) IGCSE history (0470 & 0977)


## Exam Questions (17 questions)


### Q1
Both men left Paris having won something and lost something. The difference is that Lloyd George came looking for a compromise and Clemenceau came looking for security, and only one of those was available [R].

Clemenceau had real successes. Alsace-Lorraine was returned under Article 51, France gained the Saar coalfields for fifteen years under Article 45, Germany's army was cut to 100,000 men, and Article 231 forced Germany to accept blame, with reparations set at 132 billion gold marks [R]. These were his central demands, so on territory, blame and payment he delivered what French opinion required [E].

Yet his central aim failed. He wanted the Rhineland under French control and Germany broken into separate states; instead the Rhineland was only demilitarised, Germany remained whole, and he thought neither the army limits nor the reparations went far enough [R]. This is decisive, because his motive was preventing a third German invasion, and a demilitarised zone can be remilitarised while a divided Germany could not have been reunited [A]. Marshal Foch said openly that the treaty did not protect France [R].

Lloyd George got what he came for. Germany's navy was cut to six battleships with no submarines, restoring British naval supremacy; Britain took Germany's colonies as mandates under Article 119; and the terms were harsh enough to satisfy the voters he had promised to 'make Germany pay' [R]. Germany also survived as a trading partner, which was his other main aim [E].

His disappointments were smaller. He worried about Germans placed under French and Polish rule, and reluctantly accepted the independence of eastern European states [R]. These were concerns about the future rather than failures of his own programme, and he had deliberately positioned himself between Wilson and Clemenceau, so he never expected everything [A].

Lloyd George was therefore more satisfied. Clemenceau won more of the headline terms but lost the one that mattered to him, and said so at the time [A]. Lloyd George wanted a treaty harsh enough to please British voters and mild enough to leave Germany trading, and that is what the settlement delivered. Satisfaction must be judged against what each man was trying to achieve, and the leader who wanted the middle ground was always the likelier to accept a compromise [A].

### Q2
Whether the treaty was too harsh depends on what it is measured against: the terms other countries had accepted, or what Germany could actually bear [R].

The military terms left Germany defenceless. Article 160 cut the army to 100,000 men, Article 181 limited the navy to six battleships and banned submarines, Article 198 forbade an air force, and Article 42 demilitarised the Rhineland [R]. This was harsh because Germany lost the ability to defend its own borders while its neighbours did not disarm at all, as the Ruhr showed in January 1923, when France occupied Germany's industrial heartland and Germany, capped at 100,000 troops by the treaty, could only respond with passive resistance, not military force [E].

The economic terms were harsher still, because Germany could not meet them. Reparations were set at 132 billion gold marks, Article 45 gave France the Saar coalfields for fifteen years, costing 15% of Germany's coal, and Article 119 transferred all eleven colonies to Britain and France [R]. The treaty demanded an enormous payment while removing the resources needed to make it, which is why Germany could not pay by November 1922 [E].

However, Germany had recently imposed far worse. At Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 it forced Russia to give up around one million square miles and over a third of its population [R]. The Allies were applying a standard Germany itself had set a year earlier, so the terms were not exceptional for their time [A].

Many contemporaries thought Versailles too lenient. Some in Britain and France argued that while Germany existed at all it remained a threat, and Marshal Foch complained that the treaty did not protect France [R]. The Allies then softened it, cancelling Austria's and Hungary's reparations and reducing Germany's [R]. Authors of a crushing settlement do not spend the next decade easing it [A].

The statement is true to a large extent, but not for the usual reason. The terms were not unusually severe by the standards of 1919, and Brest-Litovsk proves it [A]. What made them too harsh was their effect: they removed the means of payment while still demanding it. A treaty is too harsh when the country it is imposed on cannot live with it [A].

### Q3
One reason was that the armistice had settled nothing beyond the fighting. Germany surrendered on 11 November 1918 and the German politicians of the new Weimar Republic signed the armistice, but this only ensured a temporary peace [R]. An official peace document was still required in which the Allies agreed the terms of defeat for Germany, Austria-Hungary, Turkiye and Bulgaria, which is why five separate treaties were planned [R]. This created a need for treaties because an armistice is only an agreement to stop shooting: without a settlement, no borders, no reparations and no limits on armed forces had been fixed, and the war could simply have resumed [E].

A second reason was the scale of the destruction, which created a demand for punishment that had to be dealt with formally. Historians estimate the conflict caused around 8.5 million military deaths and around 13 million civilian casualties, and the fighting on the Western Front destroyed hundreds of factories, railways and roads in France and Belgium [R]. In Britain around 750,000 soldiers died, and Lloyd George had won the election of December 1918 on a promise to 'make Germany pay' [R]. This mattered because Allied leaders had promised their voters that Germany would be punished, and only a treaty could deliver reparations and territorial losses in a form that was legally binding on Germany [E].

### Q4

### Q7
Three sources support the statement and three work against it, and the balance depends on what test of fairness is applied.

Source A supports it most directly. Clemenceau presents the treaty as 'the weighty settlement of our accounts' after a war 'imposed on' the Allies, claiming only 'the legitimate satisfactions which are our due' [S]. Since the Western Front had been fought largely across France, the demand for repair was real [R]. Source F states the same case as law, Article 231 making Germany accept responsibility for 'all the loss and damage' caused by 'the aggression of Germany and her allies' [S]. Source D supports it more sharply still: Germany had imposed on Russia territories that 'will no longer be under her sovereignty' and a boundary 'determined by the contracting powers' [S], so if Versailles was unfair, Brest-Litovsk was far more so, and the French used exactly that comparison [R].

Against the statement, Source C provides the measure. Wilson had promised 'open covenants of peace, openly arrived at' and armaments 'reduced to the lowest point' for all states, yet Germany was excluded from the negotiations and disarmed alone [S]. Source B presses the same objection from the German side, conceding partial responsibility but insisting that to admit sole guilt 'would be a lie' [S]. Source E is the most damaging because it comes from inside the Allied delegation: Keynes reports 'no provisions for the economic rehabilitation of Europe' [S], and Source F concedes his point, since Article 232 admits German resources 'are not adequate' to meet the bill the treaty imposes [S].

Overall the sources do not provide convincing evidence that the settlement was fair, though they show it was defensible [S]. On the narrow test of entitlement to compensation, A, D and F make a strong case. But fairness also means keeping the terms on which the war was ended and demanding what can be met, and on both of those C and E convict the treaty out of the Allies' own mouths, with F supplying the admission. It was justifiable as a settling of accounts and unfair as a peace [R].

### Q8
Keynes's account can be trusted on the economics and much less on the motives he attributes to individuals.

His strongest claim to trust is that he was there. As the British Treasury's official representative at the conference, when he says 'no arrangement was reached at Paris for restoring the disordered finances of France and Italy' he is reporting what he watched happen from inside the negotiations [S]. His central claim, that the Treaty contained no provisions for the economic rehabilitation of Europe, is about what the document does and does not contain, so it can be checked rather than merely believed [S].

His purpose cuts the other way. Keynes resigned on 7 June 1919 because he had concluded the terms would not be modified, and wrote the book to argue the settlement was an economic disaster [S]. That is not a neutral observer but a man who had lost the argument and left, and who would naturally present the conference as ignoring the very problems he had urged it to solve.

The portraits of the Council of Four are where trust should be weakest. Calling Clemenceau's aim 'to crush the economic life of his enemy' and Lloyd George's 'to do a deal and bring home something which would pass muster for a week' is judgement of motive, not evidence of it [S]. There is knowledge behind it, since Clemenceau did want Germany too weak to invade and Lloyd George was elected on a promise to make Germany pay [R], but Keynes states as fact what he cannot have known.

So trust the observation and treat the verdicts with care [S].

### Q9
