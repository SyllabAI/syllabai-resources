### Q1
American pressure helped create the crisis in the USSR. Only a Soviet leader could decide not to answer it with tanks, and that decision ended Soviet control [R].

Gorbachev removed the means of control. Perestroika reduced military spending, made the Red Army smaller and ended the arms race with the USA, and by 1988 Soviet troops were being withdrawn from Eastern Europe [R]. Soviet control had always rested on a permanent garrison, so a policy that shrank the army and pulled it out removed the only thing that had held Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 [E].

Glasnost then removed the fear. Censorship was relaxed, citizens could criticise the government, opposition was to be allowed and elections were discussed in 1988, while the Soviet government publicly admitted that the Hungarian Uprising and the Prague Spring had been peaceful attempts at reform [R]. That admission told every satellite state that the invasions had been wrong and would not be repeated, which is why the revolutions of 1989 met no resistance [A].

The USA, however, had created the economic pressure. Reagan called the USSR an 'evil empire' in March 1983, the Reagan Doctrine supported anti-communist groups trying to overthrow communist governments, and the Strategic Defense Initiative was designed to force the USSR to collapse its economy by overspending on defence [R]. Since the USSR already spent up to 20% of its GDP on the military, this deepened the exact problem perestroika was invented to solve [E].

Reagan then made reform safe. Summits between 1985 and 1989 improved relations, and in December 1987 the superpowers signed the INF treaty abolishing land missiles between 500km and 5,500km [R]. Gorbachev could only cut the Red Army if he was confident the West would not exploit it, and American diplomacy gave him that [E].

Gorbachev's reforms did more. The USSR's deepest problems were self-inflicted: the economy had stopped growing by 1980, Afghanistan had been invaded in 1979 and had proved unwinnable, and living standards were falling regardless of American policy [A]. Reagan sharpened a crisis that already existed. But economic weakness alone had never freed anybody in Eastern Europe before, and the difference in 1989 was that the tanks stayed at home [A].

### Q2
Moscow's response to Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 was almost identical. What differed was the provocation it faced and the way each people reacted [R].

The method was the same in both cases. Khrushchev sent troops and tanks into Hungary to stop Nagy's reforms, and Brezhnev did the same in Czechoslovakia twelve years later against Dubcek's [R]. Both leaders were removed and replaced with pro-Soviet leaders, and both countries remained members of the Warsaw Pact [R]. Two different Soviet leaders, facing two different reform movements, reached for the same instrument [E].

The outcome abroad was also the same. The West condemned the USSR's actions on both occasions but made no attempt to stop them, accepting that both countries lay within the Soviet sphere of influence [R]. This mattered because it told Moscow that force inside the Eastern Bloc carried no international cost, which is precisely why the same solution was available in 1968 [E].

However, the violence involved was very different. The Hungarian Uprising produced two weeks of fighting, 30,000 deaths and 200,000 refugees [R]. The Czechoslovakians did not fight back at all, using peaceful protest instead, standing in front of tanks and giving Soviet troops flowers [R]. A country crushed after a war and a country occupied without resistance did not experience the same event [A].

The Soviet attitude beforehand also differed. Khrushchev had encouraged the formation of Nagy's government and wanted some reform to make communism look better to the world, whereas Brezhnev was resistant from the start [R]. Nagy also went further than Dubcek, demanding free elections and Hungary's removal from the Warsaw Pact, while Dubcek was a devout communist who always intended Czechoslovakia to remain a member [R]. The USSR was therefore answering a much greater challenge in 1956 [A].

The statement is true to a large extent. In everything Moscow controlled, the tanks, the removal of the leader, the pro-Soviet replacement and the absence of Western intervention, 1968 repeated 1956 [A]. The differences lie in what each country did, not in what the USSR did to it. Moscow's answer to reform did not change in twelve years, which is why Brezhnev could publish a doctrine making it official [A].

### Q6
