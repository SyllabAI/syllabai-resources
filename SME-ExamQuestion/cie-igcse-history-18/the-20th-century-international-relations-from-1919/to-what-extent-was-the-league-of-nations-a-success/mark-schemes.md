## Exam Questions (12 questions)


### Q1
The Depression explains why the League faced the challenges of the 1930s. It does not explain why it proved unable to meet them [R].

The Depression created the aggressors. Japan had few natural resources, unemployment rose by 30% and silk, its main export, lost its value, so Manchuria's raw materials became worth seizing [R]. Italy had also suffered and wanted Abyssinia's mineral resources and fertile land [R]. Both crises that destroyed the League were attempts to escape economic collapse through conquest, and without the Depression neither invasion had an obvious motive [E].

It also produced governments with no interest in the League. People blamed their governments for the crash and extremist parties offered radical alternatives, so by 1939 Europe had four dictators in Mussolini, Stalin, Hitler and Franco [R]. Extremists cared only about their own countries, while militarism appealed because rearmament created jobs and restored national pride [R]. The League depended on members wanting to co-operate, and the Depression replaced co-operative governments with ones built on national self-interest [E].

However, the League was already failing before 1929. It had no army, the USA had never joined, four permanent members could veto Council decisions, and the Assembly met once a year and required unanimity [R]. Poland defied it over Vilna in 1920 and Mussolini overturned its ruling on Corfu in 1923, both in years of prosperity [R]. The weaknesses exposed in the 1930s were built into the design in 1920 [A].

The decisive failures were also choices. Britain and France chose to negotiate the Hoare-Laval Pact, chose not to close the Suez Canal and chose to leave oil, iron and steel out of the sanctions on Italy [R]. None of that was forced on them by the Depression; it followed from wanting Mussolini as an ally under the Stresa Front [E]. A poorer Britain still had a navy and still controlled the Suez Canal [A].

The statement is true only to a limited extent. The Depression matters enormously and explains why Japan and Italy invaded when they did [A]. But an organisation built to keep the peace has to survive being tested, and this one could not, because it could never compel a great power and its leading members preferred their own interests to collective security [A].

### Q2
Manchuria showed that the League could not act quickly enough; Abyssinia showed that its own leading members did not want it to act at all. The second is the more serious charge [R].

Manchuria exposed how slow the League was. Japan staged an attack on the South Manchurian Railway in September 1931 and controlled the whole province by March 1932, yet the League only considered the Lytton Report in February 1933, eighteen months later [R]. No sanctions followed, and Japan ignored the report and left the League in March 1933 [R]. A determined aggressor could therefore complete a conquest before the League had finished investigating it [E].

Manchuria also set a precedent others copied. Germany and Italy were already looking to expand, and the crisis told Hitler and Mussolini that the League would not stop them [R]. It wrecked the World Disarmament Conference running at the same time, since no country would disarm having watched Japan act unopposed, and Hitler left the League in November 1933 [R]. The damage was not confined to Asia: it changed how every aggressive government in Europe calculated its risks [A].

Abyssinia, however, discredited the League's own leaders. In December 1935 the Hoare-Laval Pact, a secret Anglo-French deal to hand most of Abyssinia to Mussolini, became public, and Britain and France refused to close the Suez Canal because they valued the Stresa Front with Italy more [R]. In 1931 the League had merely been too weak to stop an aggressor; in 1935 the powers running it were caught helping one [A].

The sanctions confirmed it. Economic sanctions were imposed but excluded oil, iron and steel, partly because the USA was selling oil to Italy [R]. Italy kept what it needed, took Addis Ababa in May 1936 and then formed the Rome-Berlin Axis with Germany [R]. The League had failed to protect a member and lost the ally it sacrificed that member to keep [E].

Abyssinia did the greater damage. Manchuria came first and was the more shocking failure, but it could be explained away: Japan was distant and the USA was absent [A]. Abyssinia allowed no such excuse. It happened on Europe's doorstep, against a fellow member, and the League's own founders chose the aggressor over the victim [A].

### Q3
The League settled some disputes and failed at others, and the pattern is consistent: it succeeded when it was invited to judge and failed whenever military or economic intervention was expected [R].

Its clearest success was the Aaland Islands, 1920-21. The islands were historically Finnish but their population wanted to join Sweden, and both countries approached the League to decide [R]. It ruled that they remain Finnish but be demilitarised, and both accepted [R]. This worked because both sides had chosen to bring the case to the League and had agreed in advance to be bound by the result, so no enforcement was needed [E].

Upper Silesia in 1921 shows what happened when a settlement was accepted rather than enforced. Germany and Poland both claimed the region, so the League held a plebiscite, which Germany won with 60% of the votes, then gave Poland the industrial districts [R]. When the arrangement ended in 1925 relations worsened, Poland complaining it had received only a third of the land and Germany that it had lost a quarter of its coal mines [R]. A compromise that satisfies nobody lasts only while both sides choose to keep it [A].

Vilna in 1920 exposed the limit directly. Poland sent an army to capture the city, Lithuania appealed, and the League ordered Poland to withdraw [R]. Poland refused, and Vilna became Polish. The pledge of collective security in Article 10 was not honoured, because France did not want to upset a potential ally and Britain would not act alone [E].

Corfu in 1923 was worse, because the ruling was reversed. After an Italian general was murdered, Mussolini demanded 50 million lire and invaded Corfu, and the League ordered him out [R]. He insisted the Conference of Ambassadors decide instead, and it ordered Greece to pay Italy [R]. The League's decisions were therefore not final [A].

The League was successful only to a limited extent. Aaland was a genuine achievement, but it succeeded only where both parties had already agreed to accept its judgement [A]. Wherever a country refused, at Vilna or Corfu, it could not compel compliance. It worked as a court of arbitration for the willing, not as the guarantee of collective security it was created to be [A].
