### Q1
The Berlin Blockade did not create the division of Germany, but it turned a disagreement about how to govern it into a confrontation the two sides never came back from [R].

It produced the first direct clash between the superpowers. In June 1948 the USSR closed the road, rail and canal links into West Berlin, a city with food for only 36 days and no fuel or medicine [R]. The West had three options, all bad: withdrawing handed Stalin the capital and destroyed containment, supplying by land made the West the aggressor, and only flying supplies in avoided both [R]. For the first time each side had to weigh a decision that might mean war, which no earlier quarrel had required [E].

Its consequences were long-lasting and far-reaching. NATO was created in April 1949, the Federal Republic of Germany followed in May, the German Democratic Republic in October, and the Warsaw Pact in April 1955 [R]. Germany's temporary occupation zones therefore became two states with two rival military alliances behind them, which is the shape the Cold War kept for forty years [E].

However, the division was already well advanced. Bizonia merged the British and American zones in January 1947, the Truman Doctrine followed in March, Trizonia formed in March 1948 and the Deutschmark arrived that June [R]. Stalin blockaded Berlin because of those steps, so the blockade was a reaction to a division the West had already built [A].

It also changed nothing on the ground. Stalin lifted the blockade on 12 May 1949 having gained nothing: West Berlin stayed Western, the Deutschmark stayed, and the USSR had been humiliated because it could not stop the airlift without shooting down supply planes and starting a war [R]. A crisis the aggressor abandons on the original terms has not altered the balance of power [A].

The blockade was therefore very important to a large extent, though more as a consequence rather than a root cause. It settled nothing that was in dispute in 1948 [A]. What it did was convert an argument between former allies into two armed camps with a fortified border, and make that permanent [A].

### Q2
Both superpowers acted out of fear, so the useful test is not who was more frightened but who broke what had been agreed [R].

The strongest case against the USSR is that Stalin broke the Yalta agreement. He had accepted that free elections would be held in Eastern Europe, but the Red Army remained and communist governments were installed instead [R]. In Poland a campaign of violence and intimidation gave the communists 80% of the vote in 1947; in Hungary Rakosi seized the secret police and imprisoned opponents; in Czechoslovakia Gottwald took power by coup in 1948 [R]. These were not defences against an American threat but the destruction of governments the USSR had promised to allow [E].

The Berlin Blockade goes further. In June 1948 the USSR closed the road, rail and canal links into West Berlin, a city with only 36 days of food, to force the Western powers out of the capital [R]. Attempting to starve a city into surrender is not the behaviour of a state that merely wants to be left alone, and Stalin created the first direct confrontation [A].

However, the USA moved first and moved secretly. Truman replaced Roosevelt, took a tougher approach, concealed the Manhattan Project from an ally and used the bomb partly to demonstrate American power [R]. Kennan's Long Telegram of February 1946 already committed the USA to containment [R]. Since the satellite states were not complete until 1948, the American decision that co-operation was impossible came before the takeover it supposedly answered [A].

American policy also looked expansionist from Moscow. The Truman Doctrine of March 1947 sent \$400 million to Greece and Turkey, and the Marshall Plan gave \$13 billion to 16 countries, which Stalin called 'dollar imperialism' [R]. Trizonia followed in March 1948 and the Deutschmark that June, without Soviet agreement [R]. A rebuilt, separately governed West Germany was the exact threat the buffer zone existed to prevent [E].

The statement is true to a large extent. Both sides acted from fear, and the American atomic monopoly gave Stalin real grounds for it [A]. But the USA broke no agreement in 1945, whereas Stalin promised free elections at Yalta and then denied them, and it was the USSR that turned a quarrel into a confrontation at Berlin [A].

### Q9
The atomic bomb did serious damage [R]. Stalin learned that the Manhattan Project had succeeded while the Big Three were still at Potsdam in July 1945, and the bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki the following month killed around 120,000 Japanese civilians. Alongside the official justifications for using them, the USA wanted to show the Soviet Union how technologically advanced and powerful it was, and Truman hoped this would persuade Western Europe to back the USA and encourage Eastern Europe to resist Soviet expansion.
