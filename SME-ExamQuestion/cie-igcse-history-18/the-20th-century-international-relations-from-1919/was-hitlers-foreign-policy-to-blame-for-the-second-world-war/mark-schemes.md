### Q1
The League failed to stop Hitler; appeasement handed him what he wanted. There is a real difference between not preventing an aggressor and assisting one [R].

The League's failures taught Hitler what he could get away with. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and faced no consequences, and when Italy invaded Abyssinia in 1935 the League imposed sanctions but did nothing about Mussolini's use of chemical weapons [R]. It was slow, taking eighteen months over Manchuria, and weak because the USA was absent [R]. Every refusal to act damaged its reputation, and Hitler concluded that he could act without being stopped [E].

The League also failed at the one moment Hitler could still have been halted cheaply. When German troops entered the demilitarised Rhineland in 1936 it did not respond at all, being preoccupied with Abyssinia while Britain believed Germany had a right to the land [R]. Hitler then fortified the French border with the Siegfried Line, and many historians regard this as the last chance to stop him without a world war [R]. A failure that forecloses every later cheap option is more serious than one that merely looks bad [A].

Appeasement, however, gave Hitler physical resources rather than mere confidence. Anschluss in March 1938 brought him 100,000 soldiers and raw materials such as steel and iron, and left Czechoslovakia surrounded on three sides [R]. Munich then gave him the Sudetenland, with its fortifications and the Skoda armaments factory, agreed at a conference to which Czechoslovakia was not invited [R]. Each concession made Germany militarily stronger, so appeasement did not merely fail to stop Hitler, it armed him [E].

Appeasement also caused the final miscalculation. Having been allowed similar invasions, Hitler judged Britain and France weak and did not believe Britain was prepared for war, which is why he was surprised on 3 September 1939 [R]. Had he expected resistance over Poland he might not have invaded [A].

Appeasement was therefore more important. The League's failures came first and made appeasement thinkable [A]. But the League never had the power to stop a great power, having no army and no American support, so its failure was close to predictable. Britain and France did have choices, and at Munich they chose to sign away another country's territory [A].

### Q2
Hitler ordered every invasion that led to war, so the immediate blame is his. Whether he could have acted at all without Versailles, the League's failures and appeasement is a different question [R].

Hitler's aims made conflict likely from the start. In *Mein Kampf* he set out *Lebensraum*, expansion eastwards to destroy communism, *Grossdeutschland*, the uniting of all German speakers, and the recovery of land lost at Versailles [R]. He then built the means: armaments spending rose from 3.5 billion marks in 1933 to 26 billion by 1939 [R]. Aims of that kind could not be met by negotiation, because they required other countries to surrender territory, so the rearmament was preparation rather than a bargaining position [E].

His actions followed the same pattern. Germany left the League in November 1933, the Rhineland was remilitarised in 1936, Austria was absorbed in March 1938 and the Sudetenland in October [R]. Each was defended as correcting Versailles or uniting Germans, but Prague in March 1939 could be defended as neither, and Poland followed six months later [R]. The sequence shows escalation driven from Berlin rather than responses to events [A].

However, Versailles created the grievances Hitler used. Its terms caused severe political and economic problems, resentment of it helped bring him to power in 1933, and Lloyd George had warned they would cause a future conflict [R]. The Polish Corridor and Danzig, over which the war began, were both creations of the settlement, so the flashpoint of 1939 had been built in 1919 [A].

Britain and France also gave Hitler what he wanted. Appeasement handed him Austria and the Sudetenland, and the League's inaction over Manchuria and Abyssinia had shown that aggression carried no consequences [R]. This encouraged increasingly outrageous demands and explains his miscalculation: he was surprised Britain declared war, having been allowed similar invasions before [E].

The statement is true to a large extent. The aims were Hitler's, the rearmament was his, and the order to invade Poland on 1 September 1939 was his alone [A]. But blame is not only about who moved first. Versailles supplied the grievances, the League's failures the confidence, and appeasement both the territory and the belief that Britain would not fight [A].
