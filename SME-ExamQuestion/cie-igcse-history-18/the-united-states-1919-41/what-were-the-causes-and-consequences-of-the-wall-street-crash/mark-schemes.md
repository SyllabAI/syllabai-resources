### Q1
In November 1932 the Democratic candidate Franklin D. Roosevelt decisively defeated the incumbent Republican President Hoover in the largest landslide victory recorded in American history up to that point, winning 42 of the 48 states overall and around 7 million more individual votes than his rival [R]. Such a lopsided result invites historical explanation, since it reflects far more than a simple shift in party political preference among the electorate. This essay will discuss the reasons behind Roosevelt's remarkable victory.

The first facet is Roosevelt's own skilful and energetic campaign throughout the entire course of 1932. He was widely regarded as an excellent, persuasive public speaker, and he travelled around 20,000 kilometres by train right across the country to deliver speeches to ordinary voters in person, town after town, a level of tireless, personal campaigning that contrasted sharply with Hoover's far more remote and reserved public image during a deepening national crisis that demanded visible leadership [R].

The second facet is the specific, concrete content of Roosevelt's political message itself, as distinct from his personal manner and style on the campaign trail. He repeatedly promised to help the 'forgotten man' and offered a comprehensive 'New Deal', including specific government schemes designed to create new jobs, revive both struggling industry and struggling farming across the country, help those on low incomes or without work, improve worker protection in every industry, and end the now widely unpopular policy of nationwide Prohibition [R]. This gave millions of desperate voters across every region of the country a hopeful, specific policy reason to vote for him, rather than leaving them with merely a vague, negative reason to vote against Hoover instead, since a positive programme is generally a stronger basis for a landslide than protest alone [E].

The third facet, quite different in character from the first two, is Roosevelt's genuine personal relatability to ordinary struggling Americans across the country. Having been left disabled by polio at the relatively young age of 39, Roosevelt was widely seen by many ordinary Americans as someone who understood hardship first-hand, in sharp contrast to Hoover's own background as a self-made millionaire businessman, which many struggling, ordinary voters found genuinely difficult to relate to during the depths of such a severe and prolonged economic depression [E].

The fourth facet is Hoover's own steadily accumulated mistakes and growing unpopularity throughout his presidency. His early and firm belief in 'rugged individualism' meant the federal government did far too little as the Depression steadily worsened around him, and his notorious public declaration in January 1930 that 'we have now passed the worst' proved embarrassingly wrong within only a matter of months [R]. His controversial and widely condemned decision to use the US Army against the Bonus Marchers camped outside the White House in 1932, an action that tragically killed two protestors and a baby, horrified a country that held its war veterans in particularly high regard, and it confirmed for many ordinary Americans across the political spectrum that Hoover neither understood nor cared about their widespread suffering during the worst economic crisis the country had ever faced, an impression that proved impossible for him to shake off before the election [E].

These four facets connect closely to one another rather than operating as separate, independent influences on the final result of the election. Roosevelt's promise of active, compassionate government help resonated with voters precisely because Hoover's own record of prolonged inaction and, ultimately, outright force against suffering American citizens had already convinced millions of voters that the old approach to limited government had failed them [A].

On balance, having weighed all four facets against one another, Hoover's own accumulated mistakes were the single most important reason for the eventual result, even though Roosevelt's campaign was skilful and his political message was appealing to millions of struggling voters. The sheer scale of the landslide, 42 states to six and around 7 million votes, is difficult to explain through Roosevelt's qualities alone; it points far more to a sweeping national rejection of Hoover's presidency and its handling of the crisis, with Roosevelt himself providing the most credible, most readily available political alternative for a country that was, by the end of 1932, understandably desperate for lasting, visible change after years of hardship [A].

### Q2
The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 did not remain confined to Wall Street and the stock market itself; its effects spread rapidly, unevenly and devastatingly across the whole of the American economy over the several years that followed the initial collapse, reaching sectors that had seemed entirely unconnected to the stock market at first. This essay will discuss which of these effects mattered most.

The first facet is the severe, sustained impact on American businesses generally. Share prices continued falling for three years after the initial Crash itself, and industrial production had fallen by 45% by 1932, while car production specifically, one of the great symbols of 1920s prosperity, had fallen by an even greater 80% over the same period. Over 100,000 individual businesses went bankrupt between 1929 and 1932, showing that the damage extended far beyond the financial sector alone into almost every part of American manufacturing, retail and commerce more broadly, touching firms that had no direct connection to Wall Street speculation at all [E].

The second facet is the severe impact on the banking industry specifically. Many banks had lent heavily to investors buying shares 'on the margin' in the years before the Crash, and once those substantial loans could no longer be repaid following the collapse, banks began failing in large numbers, with as many as 659 individual banks collapsing in 1929 alone. Panicked savers across the country then rushed to withdraw their remaining money, which only accelerated further bank failures, and the surviving banks that remained open became far less willing to extend credit to businesses that badly needed it to keep operating from month to month, tightening the wider economic squeeze still further [E].

The third facet is the severe impact on American agriculture. Farmers across the whole country had never shared in the prosperity of the 1920s boom in the first place, yet they were still hit hard by the Crash and its aftermath [E]. Food prices fell sharply throughout the early 1930s, export markets remained badly damaged by existing tariff policy from earlier in the decade, and rising interest rates between 1929 and 1932 made farmers' already existing loan repayments more expensive at the worst possible time for them, resulting in around 750,000 farming families across the whole country losing their land by the mid-1930s, a crisis that had begun before the Crash but was made considerably worse by it [R].

The fourth facet is the severe impact on ordinary American workers across the whole country. As businesses across virtually every sector of the economy closed their doors permanently, unemployment rose, eventually reaching around one in every four American workers, roughly 14 million people in total, by the depths of 1933 [R]. With no welfare or unemployment benefit system in place at the time, many families lost their homes to eviction or bank repossession, and some travelled the country desperately looking for any available work as so-called 'hobos', a term that itself became loaded with social stigma, while sharply falling demand for coal, as overall industrial activity across the country slowed, closed many mines permanently and cost still more miners their jobs, wages and livelihoods too, adding yet another region of the country to the growing list of communities in visible distress [E].

Rather than acting as isolated problems within the economy, these four facets fed into one another. The collapse of the banking sector cut off the credit that ordinary businesses depended on to function, directly accelerating business failures across the country, which in turn produced the mass unemployment that then reduced consumer demand for agricultural produce further, showing that the Crash's damaging impact spread through the whole economy as a single connected chain of collapse [A].

On balance, having weighed all four facets against one another, the impact on ordinary workers was the most significant of all, because mass unemployment was ultimately where every other individual economic failure eventually converged. Bank collapses, business bankruptcies and agricultural distress all eventually expressed themselves in practice as further job losses, and with no welfare system to catch those who fell through the cracks, a full quarter of the American workforce being out of work by 1933 represents the most direct human measure of how the Wall Street Crash had transformed the American economy [A].

### Q3
The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 triggered the wider Great Depression and remains one of the most studied and debated economic collapses in the whole of American history [R]. Historians continue to argue over exactly how much weight to give to speculation itself versus the deeper structural weaknesses already present in the American economy.

The first facet is speculation, particularly the widespread practice of buying shares 'on the margin'. Share prices had boomed almost continuously for most of the 1920s, and many Americans, including large numbers of working-class investors who had never bought shares before, purchased them with only around 10% of the actual price paid upfront, borrowing the remainder from banks and brokers in the confident expectation that prices would simply keep rising [R]. This mattered a very great deal indeed because it meant enormous numbers of ordinary American investors held substantial personal debts that could only realistically be repaid if share prices kept climbing indefinitely, leaving the whole stock market dangerously and precariously exposed to even a modest loss of confidence at any point [E].

The second facet is overproduction within the wider American economy as a whole. Factories across the country had been mass-producing far more consumer goods, from cars to household appliances, than American customers were actually able to afford to buy, and this gap between supply and demand meant company profits were coming under real strain even while share prices continued rising on the stock market [R]. This mattered a great deal because it showed that the ongoing boom in share prices was becoming increasingly disconnected from the genuine underlying performance and profitability of real American businesses, a gap that could not be sustained indefinitely [E].

The third facet is the persistently uneven distribution of wealth across American society throughout the decade. Much of the genuinely new prosperity of the 1920s had concentrated heavily among the already wealthy and the urban middle class, while large sections of the wider population, particularly farmers and unskilled industrial workers, had far less disposable money available to spend on the very goods that American factories were mass-producing in ever greater quantities [R]. This significantly limited how much of the growing supply of consumer goods could realistically be sold at a profit, deepening the underlying weakness already present in company profits well before the Crash itself occurred [E].

The fourth facet is weak export markets, damaged partly by American tariff policy throughout the decade. American exports remained relatively weak throughout the 1920s, limiting an alternative outlet for the surplus goods American factories could not manage to sell at home, and this meant that domestic overproduction could not simply be solved by selling the excess abroad instead, as might otherwise have been possible [E].

These four facets connect very closely to one another rather than operating as separate, unrelated causes of the eventual collapse. Overproduction, uneven wealth distribution and weak export markets together meant that company profits were genuinely under sustained pressure by 1929, and it was this real underlying economic weakness that eventually gave cautious investors a solid and rational reason to start selling their shares, triggering the wider panic that speculation on the margin then made so much worse and so much faster than it otherwise would have been [A].

On balance, having weighed all four of these facets carefully against one another, speculation was the single most important reason for the specific timing and severity of the Crash itself, even though the underlying economic weaknesses genuinely explain why market confidence eventually broke down when it did [A]. The genuine underlying economic problems had developed gradually over several years and might, left entirely on their own, have produced only a slow and reasonably manageable economic downturn rather than a sudden crisis [A]. It was instead the sheer, huge scale of margin buying across the whole market that turned the exact moment confidence broke into a sudden, catastrophic panic, as millions of individual investors were forced to sell their shares immediately in order to cover their borrowed money, producing the sudden and dramatic crash of October 1929 rather than the far gentler, more manageable economic slowdown that the underlying weaknesses alone might otherwise have produced [A].

> **Common errors**
> 
> Common errors which prevent an answer from achieving 11-15 marks include:
> 
> - Not explaining what the bonus actually was. The marchers were not asking for charity but for a payment already promised to them, due in 1945, and this is central to why public sympathy was with them.
> - Writing a general account of the Depression. Background is worth a paragraph; the marks are for the march itself.
> - Leaving out the consequences. The effect on Hoover's reputation and on the 1932 election is what makes the episode significant.
