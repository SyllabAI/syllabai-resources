### Q1
Despite the huge overall scale, cost and ambition of Roosevelt's New Deal programme, unemployment in the USA never fell below 10% throughout the 1930s, falling only from 12.8 million unemployed Americans in 1933 to still 5.6 million by 1941, an improvement but nowhere near a full recovery [R].

The first facet, and perhaps the single most immediately visible, is that many New Deal jobs were deliberately designed to be short-term and insecure rather than permanent employment [R]. Schemes such as the Civil Works Administration created around four million individual jobs specifically intended to last only a single winter season, sometimes on so-called 'boondoggles', deliberately pointless tasks invented in order to justify paying out wages, and once these temporary schemes ended, the workers who had been employed became unemployed again almost immediately [R]. This meant that the headline New Deal job-creation figures the government publicised often masked a much less stable underlying economic reality, since many of the jobs created did not represent a lasting solution to the country's unemployment problem [E].

The second facet, more structural in nature, is that ongoing improvements in manufacturing efficiency meant that fewer individual workers were needed even as overall industrial production gradually recovered across the decade [R]. As factories across the country steadily adopted more efficient methods and modern machinery, they could produce the same or even greater overall output with far fewer individual employees than they had needed before, meaning that overall economic recovery alone did not automatically translate into falling unemployment figures in quite the way earlier generations of Americans might reasonably have expected it to [E].

The third facet, and arguably the most fundamental of all, is persistently weak consumer spending across the whole of American society [R]. Unemployment remained stubbornly high and wages generally remained low throughout the whole decade, meaning many ordinary American workers and their families lacked the disposable income needed to purchase consumer goods such as cars, radios and refrigerators [E]. This reduced consumer demand in turn caused further business closures among the companies that manufactured these consumer goods, creating a self-reinforcing economic cycle in which weak spending directly caused further unemployment, and that resulting unemployment then caused weaker consumer spending, in an ongoing and difficult-to-break downward economic spiral [A].

The fourth and final facet is the continuing damage caused by existing tariffs on American goods, which limited valuable export markets abroad and reduced overseas demand for American-made products, adding a further external economic constraint on top of the already weak domestic demand that was limiting lasting economic recovery within the USA itself [E].

Far from operating as separate, unrelated economic problems, these four facets reinforced each other. Short-term, insecure jobs failed to build the lasting consumer confidence and steady income needed to sustain regular spending, manufacturing efficiency meant that whatever consumer demand did exist required fewer workers to satisfy it, and weak export markets meant American industry could not compensate for weak domestic demand by selling its surplus abroad instead, so each individual problem worsened all of the others rather than existing in isolation from one another [A].

On balance, and having weighed all four of these facets against one another, weak consumer spending was the most fundamental of these interconnected economic problems, because it explains why the New Deal's various job-creation schemes never quite produced a self-sustaining economic recovery on their own [A]. It was ultimately not any specific New Deal policy or agency but the different circumstances of the Second World War, with its huge and sudden demand for weapons, food, raw materials and military manpower, that finally eliminated almost all American unemployment by the mid-1940s, falling to a low 670,000 unemployed Americans by 1944, showing that the New Deal alone, for all its cost and ambition, had never fully solved the underlying economic problem it had originally been designed to fix [A].

### Q2
Although President Roosevelt won the 1932 election in a sweeping landslide, carrying 42 of the 48 states in total, his subsequent New Deal soon faced fierce opposition from different groups across American society, spanning the political spectrum from far right to far left [R].

The first facet, and perhaps the most institutionally powerful, is organised Republican political opposition. Republicans believed fundamentally in 'small government' that stayed well out of ordinary people's private lives, leaving individuals to succeed or fail through their own personal efforts, and they saw the New Deal as a direct attack on this long-held political principle [R]. They believed it would make Americans lazy and encourage passive dependence on government 'handouts' rather than self-reliance, and many senior Republicans also believed Roosevelt had overreached his presidential powers, becoming unconstitutionally powerful within the federal government [E].

The second facet, closely related to the first, is opposition from wealthy Americans specifically, driven primarily by increased taxation [R]. The New Deal was an expensive government programme to fund, and Roosevelt increased income taxes on wealthy Americans to help pay for it [R]. Many wealthy people resented seeing their own hard-earned money spent on government projects such as planting trees in national parks or providing direct support to the homeless, believing they were being unfairly made to fund social problems they did not personally consider to be their own responsibility to solve [E].

The third facet, more directly economic in character, is opposition from business leaders across many industries, driven by direct government interference in how their companies were permitted to operate [R]. The National Recovery Administration forced businesses to improve working conditions, pay a legally mandated minimum wage and limit working hours, and business leaders, many of whom were themselves committed Republican supporters, believed these new regulations directly reduced both their profits and their competitiveness against rivals at home and abroad [E].

The fourth and final facet is radical opposition from the opposite end of the political spectrum, led most prominently by Senator Huey Long of Louisiana [R]. Long argued the New Deal did not go nearly far enough in helping ordinary struggling Americans, proposing instead that any personal wealth over \$5 million should be confiscated by the government and redistributed fairly so that every American family could have a house, a radio and a car of their own [R]. This radical proposal appealed to millions of poorer Americans across the country who felt Roosevelt's own reforms had not gone far enough to help them [E].

These four facets are closely linked, despite emerging from opposite ends of the political spectrum. Republicans, wealthy Americans and business leaders all objected because they believed the New Deal went too far in expanding government power, spending and interference, while radicals such as Long objected for the opposite reason, that it did not go nearly far enough, meaning Roosevelt found himself paradoxically attacked from all directions for being both too radical and not radical enough by different sections of American society at the same time [A].

On balance, having weighed all four facets against one another, opposition from the political right, combining Republicans, wealthy Americans and business leaders together, was the more lasting threat to the New Deal as a whole [A]. These groups collectively held institutional power through Congress, the federal courts and their control over investment and employment across the country, giving their sustained opposition practical force that persisted throughout Roosevelt's lengthy presidency [A]. Huey Long's radical challenge was popular and concerning to Roosevelt at the time, but it was abruptly cut short by Long's assassination in September 1935, whereas organised, well-funded and institutionally embedded opposition from across the political right continued to constrain the New Deal for many years afterwards, right through to the end of Roosevelt's long presidency [A].

### Q4
When Franklin D. Roosevelt was inaugurated as president in January 1933, he faced an economic crisis so severe, with millions of Americans unemployed and thousands of banks having already failed, that he began introducing his New Deal to the American people almost immediately after taking office [R]. His policies were built around three key aims, often remembered as the 'three Rs' [R]. The first was relief, meaning providing food for people in need and implementing policies that stopped people losing their homes [D]. The second was recovery, meaning boosting the wider economy so that jobs existed for the unemployed and rebuilding both trade and industry across the country [D]. The third was reform, meaning creating a welfare state in the USA that ensured ordinary people had access to government help for unemployment, sickness, disability and old age [D]. The wide-ranging changes achieved in these early weeks of his presidency became widely known afterwards, and remain known today, as the Hundred Days [R].

One of his first actions was the Emergency Banking Act, which closed all American banks for four days while their individual accounts were inspected by government officials [R]. This decisive action restored public confidence in the wider banking system, encouraging ordinary people to reopen accounts they had emptied in panic, and it increased the money banks had available to lend to struggling businesses, since the federal government now formally backed their loans [D].

The Beer and Wine Revenue Act effectively ended Prohibition for drinks containing no more than 3.2% alcohol, well before Prohibition was formally repealed nationwide [R]. Since millions of ordinary Americans had already been openly breaking Prohibition for years, this reduced widespread law-breaking almost immediately, stopped millions of dollars from flowing to organised criminals, and raised valuable new tax revenue for the government instead [D].

The Economy Act cut the wages of all federal government employees, including Roosevelt's own presidential salary, by a full 15% [R]. While this measure created real hardship and resentment for many individual government workers, it freed up around \$1 billion that could then be redirected to help the millions of ordinary Americans who remained unemployed [D].

Roosevelt also began broadcasting his policies directly to the American people through informal radio 'fireside chats', speaking calmly and directly to the whole nation on a regular basis [R]. More than 60 million Americans are estimated to have listened to these broadcasts, a revolutionary approach to presidential communication at the time, which helped build lasting public trust and support for his wider programme of reform [D].
