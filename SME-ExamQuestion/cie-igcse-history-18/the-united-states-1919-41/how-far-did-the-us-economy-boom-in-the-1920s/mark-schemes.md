### Q1
The 1920s are often celebrated in popular memory as the 'Roaring Twenties', a decade of dazzling prosperity and endless consumer plenty across the USA, yet by 1929, at the very height of this apparent boom, 60% of American families still lived below the poverty line [R].

The first facet is that business owners and investors gained disproportionately. Rising demand for goods increased business profits, much of which was reinvested into new products, new workers and new factories, generating even greater profits and personal wealth for those who already owned businesses. Lower taxes under Republican governments let these owners keep even more of their profits, and those who could afford to buy stocks and shares benefited further as the stock market rose steadily throughout the decade [R]. This mattered because it shows that a disproportionately large share of the entirely new wealth generated by the boom flowed straight back to people who already held significant capital before the decade had even begun, reinforcing rather than reducing existing inequality [E].

The second facet is that the urban middle class experienced genuine and dramatic improvement. Mass production and hire-purchase schemes made consumer goods such as cars, washing machines, refrigerators and vacuum cleaners suddenly available and affordable, transforming how many ordinary families lived their daily lives and giving them more time for leisure [R]. This facet genuinely complicates any overly simple claim that the boom benefited only the very wealthiest Americans, since a genuinely large section of ordinary urban society did see real, tangible improvements in their day-to-day standard of living during this period [E].

The third facet is that Black Americans were largely excluded from these gains. Many who moved north from the rural South, itself suffering an agricultural slump, found only the lowest-paid jobs available, were the last to be hired and the first to be fired, and were forced into overcrowded, poor-quality housing in the most deprived urban districts [R]. This shows clearly that race, and not merely class or geographical location, was a decisive factor in determining who could actually share in the nation's new prosperity, regardless of how hard an individual worked [E].

The fourth facet is that several other groups were excluded almost entirely. Indigenous Americans remained confined to reservations with poor-quality land, little access to education or medical care, and widespread extreme poverty. Recently arrived immigrants faced frequent discrimination and were usually restricted to the lowest-paid jobs available. Most women, aside from a small number of wealthy, White, upper-class women, continued to be paid less than men for identical work while also being expected to manage their households [R]. This considerable breadth of exclusion shows that the boom's benefits were deliberately or effectively withheld from several quite distinct groups all at the same time, rather than from any single, isolated disadvantaged minority alone, which strengthens the case that exclusion was systemic rather than incidental [E].

These four facets connect very closely to one another rather than operating as separate, unconnected phenomena within the wider economy. The very same mechanisms that enriched business owners and the urban middle class, such as hiring decisions, wage rates and access to credit, were the mechanisms through which Black Americans, immigrants, Indigenous Americans and women were simultaneously excluded, since prosperity for one group and exclusion for another were simply two outcomes produced by exactly the same underlying economic and social system, rather than being two entirely separate and unrelated phenomena occurring by coincidence [A].

On balance, having weighed all four facets carefully against one another, the benefits of the economic boom were shared equally only to a limited extent, since they were distributed very unequally rather than broadly and fairly across the whole of American society [A]. The stark fact that 60% of families remained below the poverty line at the very height of the boom is genuinely difficult to reconcile with any straightforward account of the 1920s as a decade of general, widely shared national prosperity. The 'Roaring Twenties' was, for a clear majority of ordinary Americans, principally a decade in which an already comparatively privileged minority became significantly wealthier still, while women, Black Americans, Indigenous Americans and immigrants alike largely watched the celebrated boom unfold from outside its benefits.

### Q2
The 1920s are often remembered as a decade of prosperity and growth in the USA, powered above all by the new motor industry and reflected in the towering skyscrapers that rose in cities across the country, but this growth was not shared by every sector of the economy. Several long-established traditional industries declined sharply even as the wider economy boomed around them.

The first facet is competition from new synthetic materials. Companies such as Dupont developed synthetic fabrics like rayon, which consumers found highly desirable, [R] and this fashion for synthetics led to a slump in demand for traditional cotton and woollen textiles. [E] This damaged both the farmers who grew the raw cotton and wool and the mills that turned these materials into finished cloth, showing that a single fashionable new consumer product could damage an entire established regional supply chain almost overnight and with little warning. [E] Cotton, once the backbone of the southern American economy for generations, now faced direct competition from a material that had not even existed a few years earlier. [E]

The second facet is the rise of electricity as an energy source. As more American homes were connected to the electricity grid, rising from 15% in 1916 to 70% by 1927, and as households and businesses increasingly relied on electricity, gas and oil for heating, demand for coal fell sharply. [R] This led directly and swiftly to the closure of mines and the loss of thousands of jobs across coal-mining regions of the country, [E] since an entire industry, one that had powered American growth for the previous century, found its core domestic market disappearing within little more than a single decade of rapid electrification. [E]

The third facet is the end of the First World War itself. Wartime demand for ships had been extremely high throughout the conflict, as both military and merchant vessels were urgently needed, [R] but once the war ended in 1918 this demand collapsed almost immediately, and the shipbuilding industry declined sharply as a result, [E] causing further significant and lasting job losses in a sector that had only briefly boomed during the fighting itself, before demand vanished almost as quickly as it had appeared. [E]

The fourth facet is the rise of the motor car, arguably the single most disruptive new technology of the whole decade. As car ownership expanded dramatically, helped by mass production techniques that saw one Model T completed every ten seconds on Henry Ford's assembly line, reaching 23 million cars in the USA by 1929, [R] the railway industry, which had previously been essential for transporting both goods and passengers across such a vast country, went into steady decline as Americans increasingly chose to travel and transport goods by road instead. [E] The motor industry's huge and growing demand for raw materials such as steel and rubber also meant it competed directly with older sectors, including shipbuilding and the railways themselves, for exactly the same resources those traditional industries still needed to survive. [E]

These facets connect very closely to one another rather than existing as isolated, unrelated developments. [A] In every single case, it was innovation and genuine success elsewhere in the economy, in cars, in electricity and in synthetic chemistry, that directly caused decline in an older, established industry, rather than any traditional industry failing on its own terms through simple mismanagement or a general lack of demand for its products. [A]

On balance, having weighed all four facets against one another, the rise of the motor car was the single most important reason for the decline of traditional industries, because its effects reached far more widely across the whole economy than any of the other three factors considered alone. [A] The car industry did not only damage the railways directly; it also drove the construction of new roads that reduced railway usage further, and its success pulled workers, fresh investment and consumer attention away from every other form of transport, while its enormous appetite for steel and rubber also competed directly for exactly the same raw resources that traditional industries themselves still needed to survive. [A] While synthetic textiles and electricity each damaged a single, specific traditional sector in a fairly direct and predictable way, the motor car reorganised how Americans actually lived, worked, shopped and travelled on a daily basis, making its impact on traditional industry by far the broadest, deepest and most lasting of all the changes the 1920s brought. [A]
