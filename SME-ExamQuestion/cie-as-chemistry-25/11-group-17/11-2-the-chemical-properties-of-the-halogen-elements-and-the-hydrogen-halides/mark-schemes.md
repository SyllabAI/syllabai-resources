### Q6

### Q8

### Q9
**Final answer:** **The correct answer is B**

- Thermal stability depends on the strength of the H–X bond, which is determined by bond length and orbital overlap.

  - Electronegativity affects bond polarity, but polarity does not determine thermal stability.

- Thermal decomposition involves homolytic fission of the covalent bond.

  - This depends on bond enthalpy, not on the difference in electronegativity between the atoms.

**A** is incorrect as the activation energy for H–I decomposition is lower than for H–Cl, because the H–I bond is weaker. This does explain the observation.

**C** is incorrect as iodine has a larger atomic radius than chlorine, resulting in a longer H–I bond with less effective orbital overlap. The greater size of the iodine atom directly explains the lower thermal stability of HI.

**D** is incorrect as the H–Cl bond has a higher bond dissociation energy than H–I. The greater strength of the H–Cl bond means it does not break at the temperature of the platinum wire.

### Q10
**Final answer:** **The correct answer is B**

- A stronger oxidising agent will displace a weaker oxidising agent from its halide solution.

  - Oxidising ability decreases down Group 17 as atomic radius increases and electron shielding increases.

- From the table:

  - Y<sub>2</sub> displaces both X<sup>−</sup> and Z<sup>−</sup>, so Y<sub>2</sub> is the strongest oxidising agent (Cl<sub>2</sub>).
  - Z<sub>2</sub> displaces only X<sup>−</sup>, so Z<sub>2</sub> is intermediate (Br<sub>2</sub>).
  - X<sub>2</sub> displaces nothing, so X<sub>2</sub> is the weakest oxidising agent (I<sub>2</sub>).

- The reducing power of halide ions is the inverse of the oxidising power of the parent halogen.
- The order of decreasing reducing strength is therefore:

X<sup>−</sup> (I<sup>−</sup>) > Z<sup>−</sup> (Br<sup>−</sup>) > Y<sup>−</sup> (Cl<sup>−</sup>).

**A** is incorrect as this order places Y<sup>−</sup> between X<sup>−</sup> and Z<sup>−</sup>, implying Cl<sup>−</sup> is more reducing than Br<sup>−</sup>. The reverse is true.

**C** is incorrect as this represents the order of oxidising ability of the parent halogens (Cl<sub>2</sub> > Br<sub>2</sub> > I<sub>2</sub>), which is the inverse of halide reducing power.

**D** is incorrect as Z<sup>−</sup> (Br<sup>−</sup>) is not the strongest reducing agent. X<sup>−</sup> (I<sup>−</sup>) is strongest because I<sub>2</sub> is the weakest oxidising agent.

## Structured Questions (4 questions)


### Q1
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The boiling point of HF is anomalously high because:
> 
> - HF has hydrogen bonding (between molecules) **[1 mark]**
> - The other hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) only have van der Waals' forces, which are weaker than hydrogen bonds **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> All the hydrogen halides (HX) have a permanent dipole because of the difference in electronegativity between H and X. This gives them permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces. However, only HF forms hydrogen bonds, because fluorine is highly electronegative, has a small atomic radius, and acts as a hydrogen bond acceptor.
> 
> Hydrogen bonds are stronger than the van der Waals' forces in the other hydrogen halides, so more energy is needed to separate HF molecules giving it a much higher boiling point.
> 
> Note that "van der Waals' forces" is an umbrella term covering both instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id) forces and permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd-pd) forces.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The increase in boiling points from HCl to HI is due to:
> 
> - Increasing mass / size / number of electrons of the halogen atom **[1 mark]**
> - Results in stronger van der Waals' forces / London forces / instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two types of intermolecular forces are present in all the hydrogen halides:
> 
> 1. Permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd-pd) forces
> 2. Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id) forces
> 
> Going from HCl to HI, the halogen becomes less electronegative, so the H–X bond becomes less polar and pd-pd forces decrease. However, the atoms get larger and have more electrons, which strengthens the id-id forces. The increase in id-id forces outweighs the decrease in pd-pd forces, so the overall intermolecular forces increase and boiling points rise.
> 
> For these 2 marks, stating that larger atoms have stronger van der Waals' forces is sufficient. You do not need to explain the full interplay between both force types.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> HF is more thermally stable than HI because:
> 
> - The H–F bond is shorter than the H–I bond / fluorine has a smaller atomic radius than iodine, giving a shorter bond **[1 mark]**
> - A shorter bond is a stronger bond, so more energy is required to break the H–F bond / the H–F bond has a higher bond dissociation energy **[1 mark]**
> 
> The reverse argument (starting from HI being less thermally stable) is also acceptable.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Thermal stability describes how well a substance resists breaking down when heated. A thermally stable substance requires more energy to decompose.
> 
> Do not confuse this with electronegativity. While fluorine is more electronegative than iodine, electronegativity affects oxidising power, not thermal stability. The key factor here is bond length: fluorine is smaller, so the H–F bond is shorter, and shorter bonds are stronger.
> 
> A common error is to state that "HF has a longer bond than HI", which is incorrect. Fluorine is above iodine in the group and has a smaller atomic radius, so it forms a shorter H–X bond.
> 
> shorter bond = stronger bond = more thermally stable

### Q4
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The term electronegativity is defined as:
> 
> - The power / ability of an atom to attract electrons (to itself / in a covalent bond) **[1 mark]**
> 
> The trend in electronegativity down Group 17:
> 
> - Electronegativity decreases down Group 17 **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**

> 
> For the explanation, the final mark specifically requires you to refer to bonding electrons or the shared pair of electrons, not simply "outer electrons." Electronegativity is about attracting electrons in a bond, so saying "the nucleus has weaker attraction for the outer electrons" would earn marks for an ionisation energy question but will not be credited here. You must say "bonding electrons" or "shared pair."
> 
> The three-step explanation is:
> 
> 1. Atomic radius increases going down the group
> 2. Shielding by inner electron shells increases
> 3. So, the nucleus has a weaker pull on the shared electrons in the bond and electronegativity decreases.

> Iodine is a weaker oxidising agent than bromine because:
