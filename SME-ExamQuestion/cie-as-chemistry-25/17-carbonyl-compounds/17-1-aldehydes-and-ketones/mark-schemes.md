> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two isomers with formula C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>6</sub>O that can be reduced by NaBH<sub>4</sub> are:
> 
> - Propanal **[1 mark]**
> - Propanone **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> NaBH<sub>4</sub> reduces aldehydes and ketones (compounds containing a C=O group). Propanal is the only possible aldehyde with formula C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>6</sub>O, and propanone is the only possible ketone. There are exactly two correct answers here.
> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) Classes of alcohol formed:
> 
> - An aldehyde (e.g. propanal) is reduced to a primary alcohol **[1 mark]**
> - A ketone (e.g. butanone) is reduced to a secondary alcohol **[1 mark]**
> 
> ii) Explanation:
> 
> - The reduction product of propanal is propan-1-ol: the alcoholic carbon has two H atoms attached, so it cannot have four different groups and cannot be a chiral centre **[1 mark]**
> - The reduction product of butanone is butan-2-ol: the alcoholic carbon has H, OH, CH<sub>3</sub>, and CH<sub>2</sub>CH<sub>3</sub> attached / four different groups, giving a chiral centre and allowing optical isomers **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A chiral centre requires four different substituents on one carbon. Primary alcohols always have two H atoms on the alcoholic carbon, so a chiral centre is impossible. Whether a secondary alcohol has a chiral centre depends on whether the two groups on either side of the C–OH are different. For butan-2-ol they are (CH<sub>3</sub> and CH<sub>2</sub>CH<sub>3</sub>), but for propan-2-ol they are not (both CH<sub>3</sub>).
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The reduction product of a carbonyl compound cannot be a tertiary alcohol because:
> 
> - Tertiary alcohols have the formula R<sub>3</sub>COH, requiring three carbon groups on the alcoholic carbon
> **AND**
> Carbonyl compounds have the formula R<sub>2</sub>C=O, with only two carbon groups on the carbonyl carbon **[1 mark]**
> 
> - Reduction of a ketone (R<sub>2</sub>C=O) gives R<sub>2</sub>CHOH (secondary alcohol)
> **AND**
> Reduction of an aldehyde (RCHO) gives RCH<sub>2</sub>OH (primary alcohol), where the C–H bond formed means the product cannot be a tertiary alcohol **[1 mark]**

> **[exam-tip]**
> A tertiary alcohol requires three carbon substituents on the alcoholic carbon. NaBH<sub>4</sub> adds a hydride ion (H<sup>–</sup>) to the carbonyl carbon, so the alcoholic carbon always retains at least one H, making the product primary or secondary, never tertiary.
> 
> The reverse logic also works: tertiary alcohols cannot be oxidised under normal conditions; the reverse of reduction of a carbonyl would therefore be impossible.
