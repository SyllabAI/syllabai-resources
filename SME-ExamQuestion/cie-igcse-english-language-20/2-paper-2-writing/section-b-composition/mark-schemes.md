# Mark Schemes — Section B: Composition

## November 2023 (0500/21) Section B (4 questions)


### Q1
### Model answer

**The portrait**

It hung in the hallway of my grandmother’s house: a faded painting of a girl in a pale blue dress, her eyes fixed on whoever stood before her. Every summer, when we visited, I hurried past it with my head down. There was something about her gaze — steady, unblinking — that unsettled me in a way I couldn’t explain.

One afternoon, rain kept me indoors, and with nothing else to do I stopped and studied the portrait. The girl’s face was fine, her hair drawn back in a ribbon; she looked no older than sixteen. Yet her eyes seemed far older. They held secrets. At the bottom of the frame was a name, barely legible: *Clara, 1892*.

“Your great-aunt,” my grandmother told me when she caught me staring. “Died young, poor child. The story was that she had a fever.” She spoke briskly, as though reciting a well-worn line, then changed the subject. But that night I could not sleep. I kept seeing Clara’s eyes, following me in the dark.

The next day, I noticed something strange. In the dim morning light, her lips seemed slightly curved, as if the painting had changed overnight. My pulse quickened. I told myself it was only the shadows, the trick of tired eyes. But when I looked again that evening, the smile had gone.

I began to watch the portrait daily. Sometimes her expression was sorrowful, sometimes mocking, and once, just once, there was a look of pleading so desperate that it chilled me to the bone. I asked my grandmother about it again, but she replied, “Don’t meddle with things you don’t understand.” Her voice trembled.

That night the storm returned. Thunder cracked; rain battered the windows. Unable to resist, I crept into the hallway. Lightning flared, illuminating the portrait and Clara was no longer seated in her painted chair. The canvas was empty.

I turned, and in the flickering dark I thought I saw a figure at the end of the corridor: a girl in a pale blue dress, eyes fixed firmly on me.

I ran, heart hammering, and did not stop until I was back in bed, blankets pulled tight around me. When morning came, the portrait was once again complete. Clara sat, demure as ever, yet her eyes seemed brighter, her smile sharper.

And I knew then that the painting was not just a picture.

The narrative is carefully structured: it moves from curiosity, to doubt, to terror, culminating in the climactic storm scene before resolving with a chilling twist. This controlled arc ensures the story feels complete and convincing.
> 
> Vocabulary is precise and effective. Imagery is well chosen, particularly in the storm sequence where “lightning flared” and the portrait is found empty, intensifying suspense. > - Use subtle description before obvious shocks; suspense often works best through suggestion
> - Vary sentence length to reflect pace: lingering sentences for eerie atmosphere, short ones for moments of shock
> - End with a twist or haunting final image that stays in the reader’s mind

### Q2
### Model answer

The siren’s wail still echoed in my ears when I reached the corner of Alder Street. Smoke rolled across the sky like a dark tide, swallowing the stars. People were spilling out of their houses, pointing, shouting, crowding together in frightened knots. And there, at the centre of it all, stood the house I knew too well: Mrs Greene’s.

Flames were already clawing at the windows. The air shimmered with heat, and the smell was thick and bitter, like burning plastic. My chest tightened as I scanned the crowd. Mrs Greene was not among them. Neighbours muttered, their faces pale, but no one moved forward. Someone said the fire engine was on its way; someone else muttered it was too late.

And then, faint and fragile, I heard it: a cough, a cry, I couldn’t tell which. But it came from inside. My heart thudded so hard I thought it might break through my ribs. Everyone around me froze, rooted to the pavement, but I felt something shift within me. That was the moment I realised it was my responsibility. Not because I was the strongest, or the bravest, but because doing nothing would haunt me far longer than the flames themselves.

I pulled my jacket up over my mouth and ran. The heat slapped me back, but I forced myself inside. Smoke wrapped around me like a living thing, stinging my eyes and filling my throat. I stumbled, coughing, calling her name. There was no answer — only the crack and groan of the house collapsing in on itself.

Then I saw her: huddled at the bottom of the staircase, her small frame trembling, eyes wide with fear. I grabbed her arm, hoisted her up, and together we staggered towards the doorway. Each step was a battle, every breath like swallowing fire, but the light of the street seemed to drag us forward.

When we burst out into the night air, the crowd erupted. Strong hands pulled Mrs Greene from me, wrapping her in blankets. Someone pressed a bottle of water into my shaking hand. My legs buckled, but relief swept through me like a tide.

Later, as the fire engines doused the last of the flames, I stood apart, watching the smoke drift into the dawn. I knew I had not been fearless; I had been terrified. But sometimes responsibility is heavier than fear and that is what carries you through.

> ### Commentary
> 
> The response demonstrates a sophisticated ability to build suspense and convey both external action and internal emotion. The opening immediately establishes urgency with the “siren’s wail” and “smoke… swallowing the stars”, setting a vivid and unsettling scene. The narrative is carefully structured around a decisive turning point which shifts the action from observation to intervention. This gives the story a clear arc from danger to resolution, with a reflective ending that provides depth.
> 
> Vocabulary is precise and evocative, with images such as “flames clawing at the windows” and “smoke wrapped around me like a living thing” heightening the intensity. Sentence structures are effectively varied. The register is appropriate, adopting a tense yet personal tone that draws the reader into the narrator’s dilemma. Spelling, punctuation, and grammar are consistently accurate, ensuring fluency and control. Overall, this is a confident and highly effective Level 6 response.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Begin with an immediate sense of drama: sound, light, and movement can all plunge the reader straight into the action
> - Use a clear turning point (e.g. a realisation or decision) to give your story shape and direction
> - Vary sentence length to mirror pace: short sentences for urgency, longer ones for description or reflection
> - End reflectively: a narrative is most powerful when it leaves the reader with a final thought

### Q3
### Model answer

**The rehearsal**

The rehearsal room smelled faintly of polish and dust, as though the wooden floor remembered every shuffle of every dancer’s foot. The group gathered slowly: some arriving with the sleepy reluctance of early morning, others brimming with nervous energy. Tracksuits rustled, water bottles clattered to the ground, and someone stretched noisily, the pop of their joints sharp in the stillness.

At first, the atmosphere was awkward, a cluster of individuals rather than a team. Conversation was scattered: whispers about homework, laughter over a forgotten step, the anxious rustle of sheet music in a corner. The director, a woman with a clipboard clamped to her chest, raised her eyebrows as if daring anyone to waste her time. Then, with a sharp clap of her hands, silence fell.

The group took their places. A chord struck on the piano; someone tapped a pencil against a music stand in nervous rhythm. Movements were hesitant at first: voices wavered, feet stumbled, instruments squeaked. Soon the space was alive with rhythm and energy. The air throbbed with clashing harmonies and stamping feet, until each mistake melted into determination.

Faces tightened in concentration: brows furrowed, lips pursed, eyes fixed on an invisible goal. A boy in the back row counted under his breath, lips moving like a prayer; a girl marked her steps in miniature, her fingers tracing invisible lines across her palm. They were no longer individuals now but parts of a machine, each gear essential, each movement feeding into the next.

Sweat darkened T-shirts; laughter returned as someone spun the wrong way; yet even in error there was unity. Encouragement sparked: a nod from the pianist, a grin from a dancer to her partner, a quiet “you’ve got this” whispered before the next attempt. By the time the director called a halt, the group stood together in a circle, breathing heavily, cheeks flushed, eyes shining with shared effort.

The rehearsal had not been perfect, but that was never the point. In the end, it was about becoming more than the sum of their parts: finding rhythm in one another, shaping chaos into harmony, and tasting, for a moment, the thrill of something greater than themselves.

> 
> ### Commentary
> 
> The response demonstrates a sophisticated ability to convey both the physical and emotional dimensions of rehearsal. The opening immediately establishes atmosphere through sensory detail which brings the scene vividly to life.
> 
> Vocabulary is precise and purposeful. Sentence structures are varied for effect: long, flowing sentences mirror the energy of practice, while shorter sentences emphasise moments of silence. The register is consistently appropriate, maintaining a controlled tone. Accuracy in spelling, punctuation, and grammar is flawless, supporting clarity and impact. Overall, this is a confident and highly effective Level 6 response.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Establish atmosphere at the start through sensory detail (sounds, smells, textures) to ground the reader in the scene
> - Show progression: structure your description around a shift (e.g. from awkward beginnings to unity) to create development
> - Use metaphor and simile to enrich meaning, but ensure they reflect the theme of the piece rather than distract from it
> - Vary sentence lengths to mirror rhythm: flowing sentences for energy, shorter ones for silence or emphasis

### Q4
### Model answer

When the gates first swing wide, the theme park hums to life like a giant machine shaking off sleep. Music drifts on the morning air: bright, tinny tunes designed to raise spirits before the day has even begun. Children tug their parents forward, eyes wide as balloons, while teenagers swagger towards the rollercoasters, pretending not to care though their excitement spills out in restless laughter. The air smells of frying onions, buttered popcorn and spun sugar.

Everywhere, colours scream for attention. Red and yellow banners ripple overhead; neon signs flash, urging visitors towards rides with names like *The Plunge* or *Sky Slicer*. The ferris wheel, slow and dignified, turns like the hand of a clock measuring joy. Beneath it, the rollercoaster clatters and roars, a steel dragon hurtling its passengers into the sky before dragging them back down in a blur of sound and speed. Screams of fear and delight mingle until it is impossible to tell them apart. Even the queues feel alive: snaking lines of chatter and anticipation, every step closer raising the pulse.

As the sun sinks, the park takes on a new magic. Strings of lights flicker on; rides gleam like jewels against the darkening sky. The carousel spins, horses frozen in gallop, mirrors catching glimmers of laughter and light. Fireworks explode above, showering sparks of red, gold and silver, while crowds tilt their faces upwards, mouths open in wonder. The whole place seems like a kingdom of noise, colour and movement, bound together by the shared thrill of escape.

Yet once the final firework fades, the enchantment falls away. When the gates are locked, silence spreads. The music dies; the smell of popcorn lingers faintly, like a memory. The rollercoaster stands motionless, its tracks black against the moonlight, no longer a dragon but a skeleton of steel. The ferris wheel is a dark circle in the sky, unmoving, its carriages creaking faintly in the wind. Without people, the queues dissolve, leaving only painted barriers and empty walkways that echo with ghostly footsteps.

The park at night belongs to the shadows. Neon signs flicker uncertainly before going dark. Plastic horses on the carousel freeze mid-prance, eyes wide and unblinking. A place once overflowing with laughter is suddenly uncanny: still, eerie, expectant. It waits, as though holding its breath, until the gates open again and life returns.

> This is a 40-mark question. The following response would be securely placed in the top band (Level 6), as it offers a vivid, well-structured, and highly controlled piece of descriptive writing, sustaining engagement and atmosphere throughout.
> 
> ### Commentary
> 
> The response demonstrates a sophisticated ability to convey an experience and captures both the excitement and eeriness of the theme park. The description when the park is open is full of sensory detail which creates a lively, immersive scene. The contrast with the closed park is equally effective, shifting tone from exuberance to unease.
> 
> Vocabulary is precise and well chosen, with metaphors heightening the reader’s engagement. Sentence structures are varied: complex, flowing descriptions mirror the vibrancy of the open park, while shorter sentences in the final paragraph create tension and stillness. The register is consistently appropriate, adopting a descriptive style that is both evocative and controlled. Accuracy in spelling, punctuation, and grammar is strong. Overall, this is a confident and highly effective Level 6 response.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Use contrast to structure your writing: describing the same place in different states (e.g. open vs closed) gives balance and focus
> - Build atmosphere through sensory detail: combine sounds, smells, sights and textures to immerse the reader
> - Choose vocabulary with precision: metaphors and personification are most effective when they enhance the mood, not when they are overused
> - Plan for a tonal shift: moving from lively description to an eerie or unsettling atmosphere leaves a strong final impression
