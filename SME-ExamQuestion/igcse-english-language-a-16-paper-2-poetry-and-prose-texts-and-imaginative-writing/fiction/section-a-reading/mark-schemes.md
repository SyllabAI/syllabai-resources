**Model Answer**

Rose Tremain captures the reader’s interest by presenting Lev as a character full of emotions as he travels to England in search of a better life, caught between grief, fear and hope. From the opening, Lev is shown “huddled against the window”, staring out at “the land he was leaving” suggesting that he feels vulnerable and uncomfortable as he leaves behind the familiarity of home. His physical appearance is described as “grey-toned” and clutching “an old red cotton handkerchief and a dented pack of Russian cigarettes”, which reflects emotional exhaustion and personal loss. These objects symbolise his connection to home, anchoring him in his past even as he embarks on a new chapter. The unlit cigarette becomes particularly meaningful, described as “a companion – something to hold on to”, emphasising his need for comfort and stability. These conflicting emotions about his move are evident as he would “hold himself apart from other people” and that his “heart remained in his own country”, suggesting that he does not really want to belong in his new country and forget where he comes from.

The writer further sustains interest by portraying the journey itself as both physically and emotionally draining, symbolic of Lev’s uncertainty. The coach is described in basic and limited terms, as there is only an “on-board lavatory” and “the only stops the bus would make would be for gas”, which highlights the discomfort of the experience. Lev imagines that “they would have to sit for fifty hours or more,” and the repetitive structure of “side by side with their separate aches and dreams” suggests monotony and isolation. The sights from the bus window, such as “the stink of another industrial zone” and “the approach of darkness on silent marshes,” reflect Lev’s bleak outlook. The unpleasant nature of the journey mirrors the internal struggle he faces, and the emphasis on cramped conditions and sleep deprivation underlines his physical vulnerability.

In addition, the writer uses powerful contrasts in language and imagery to reflect Lev’s conflicted thoughts about leaving home and facing the unknown. His memories of home are filled with natural, colourful imagery such as “fields of sunflowers”, “quarries and rivers” and “wild garlic growing green”, all of which evoke vitality and connection. These are set in contrast with the grey, mechanical and industrial images associated with the journey and destination. Furthermore, the repetition of darkness in “darkness had always arrived in precisely the same way” and “darkness was falling outside the window” symbolises Lev’s fear and emotional numbness. The contrast between past brightness and present gloom powerfully conveys the sense of loss and disorientation he feels.

The writer engages the reader further through the introduction of Lydia, whose dialogue with Lev offers a moment of warmth and humour amidst the bleakness. Lydia’s quirky comments, such as “If only we were storks” serve to break the silence of the journey and make both characters appear more human. This interaction reveals Lev’s insecurity and determination to improve himself: “May I say some words and you can tell me if I’m pronouncing them correctly?” His desire to learn reflects his hope for a better future and gives the reader a reason to support him. Their connection offers relief from the tension, and the contrast between Lydia’s confidence and Lev’s uncertainty further builds interest in his personal development.

Finally, the writer uses structure and symbolism to deepen the reader’s interest in Lev’s journey. The passage shifts from external descriptions to internal reflections as Lev studies a British banknote. The depiction of “the frumpy Queen” and “a man, some personage from the past” invites readers to consider Lev’s outsider perspective. The note’s symbols, such as the “angel’s radiance”, ironically contrast with Lev’s current state, yet they also hint at him wanting better things. The ending is particularly impactful as Lev declares, “I’m going to their country now and I’m going to make them share it with me” demonstrating a final shift from fear to determination.

The poem “Out, Out –” by Robert Frost evokes deep sympathy for the boy through its use of language, structure and the presentation of both the boy’s and others’ reactions. Frost depicts a scene where the boy’s vulnerability is highlighted from the start, setting the scene for the shocking and tragic accident that unfolds.

From the outset, the saw is given a threatening, almost living presence: Frost describes how it “snarled and rattled” using violent verbs that suggest aggression and danger. This makes the boy seem powerless in comparison, especially as he is not even mentioned until later, which builds a sense that he is insignificant compared to the dangerous machinery he works with. Furthermore, the use of onomatopoeia “snarled” and repetition “snarled and rattled, snarled and rattled”, emphasises the menace of the saw and foreshadows the terrible disaster that follows.

Frost further builds sympathy for the boy by showing how young and innocent he is. He is only referred to as “the boy” which distances him from the adult world and reminds the reader of his youth. Despite his young age, he is “Doing a man’s work” making the reader pity his loss of childhood. His longing for a break, “Call it a day, I wish they might have said”, shows how much he wishes to escape his adult responsibilities and to give him “the half hour/That a boy counts so much when saved from work.”

When the accident happens, the moment is both sudden and shocking: “At the word, the saw... Leaped out at the boy’s hand”. Frost’s language personifies the saw, making it seem as if it acts with a will of its own, further reinforcing the boy’s helplessness. His initial response, a “rueful laugh”, shows his shock and denial. Sympathy for the boy increases when he raises his injured hand, “Half in appeal, but half as if to keep / The life from spilling”, which conveys his instinct to survive and his sudden awareness of mortality. His plea, “Don’t let him cut my hand off— / The doctor, when he comes. Don’t let him, sister!” is tragic, as the reader knows it is already too late. The poem moves swiftly between moments, sometimes using short, punchy sentences, “But the hand!” and “So”, that mimic the shock and speed of events.

The doctor’s arrival only underlines the hopelessness of the situation. Frost writes, “the hand was gone already” showing that nothing can be done. The doctor “put him in the dark of ether” removing the boy’s last bit of control. As the boy’s life fades, the “watcher at his pulse took fright” which suggests mounting panic and helplessness, until finally, “Little—less—nothing!—and that ended it”. These sentences, punctuated by dashes, slows the pace and evokes sympathy in the reader.

The reactions of those around the boy further heighten the reader’s sympathy, “And they, since they / Were not the one dead, turned to their affairs”. This abrupt return to normality after the boy’s death contrasts sharply with the reader’s empathy, leaving a bitter sense of injustice and reinforcing the tragedy of the boy’s short life.

The poem’s ending is particularly striking. The repeated negatives, “Little—less—nothing!” and the final, blunt statement, “No more to build on there”, creates a harsh sense of finality. The end-stopped lines leave the reader with a sense of abrupt closure emphasising the cruelty of the boy’s death.

Overall, Frost’s poem creates sympathy for the boy through its shocking portrayal of the accident, the helplessness of those around him and the cold, uncaring response of the world to it.

In “Whistle and I’ll Come to You” Susan Hill carefully builds tension through the narrator’s shifting thoughts and feelings, his perception of others (both real and supernatural) and her use of language and structure. This results in an atmosphere filled with uncertainty, dread and fear.

At the very start of the extract, tension is created by the narrator’s abrupt awakening: “I awoke abruptly”. This moment disturbs the peacefulness usually associated with sleep, setting a nervous tone. The narrator reflects on the worsening weather, describing how the wind has “increased greatly in force” and how the house feels “like a ship at sea” which conveys his vulnerability. The description of the house being “battered by the gale” and how the windows were “rattling everywhere” makes the narrator feel unsafe and exposed. His reflection that the house has survived storms before offers only a temporary relief, because his nostalgic memories of childhood contrast sharply with his current feelings of isolation and danger.

Tension escalates further when the narrator hears a strange “cry” coming “from somewhere, out of that howling darkness”. The fact that the narrator is unsure where the cry is coming from and even questions his own hearing by saying “Nothing” builds unease. He wonders whether he is hearing “some long-dead ghost” which suggests a supernatural presence and immediately raises the tension. His plan to comfort himself by going to make a drink and stir up the fire only increases this tension because the reader senses that something more threatening is about to occur.

The narrator’s repeated sense that he is not alone builds further as the text develops. He describes the “absolutely certain sense” that “someone” has just passed by him, even though there is no sound and no movement. The fact that the corridor leads to the “nursery whose door had been so firmly locked and then, inexplicably, opened” adds to the eerie tone. The narrator’s mind spirals into “wild, incoherent fantasies” imagining whether a living human, a reclusive woman or a “mad friend”, could be hidden in the house. But his sudden realisation that “there was no living occupant” forces the reader and the narrator to consider that whatever is present is not human.

Descriptions of others in the extract also heighten tension. The child’s “cry of desperation and anguish” echoes through the storm, making the reader imagine a child alone in the dark marsh. The narrator himself admits that “There was no child” but cannot ignore the sound, suggesting that the presence is ghostly or supernatural. This idea is reinforced by the use of phrases like “long-dead ghost” and “Rest in peace” which create a morbid, unsettling atmosphere. The narrator’s sense of hopelessness increases tension further: he reflects that “no one had been able to do anything for… how many years?” emphasising that the haunting has been occurring for a long period of time.

Hill’s use of language and structure increases the tension throughout. She frequently uses short sentences that create an abruptness and urgency: “No light came on. The torch had broken.” Adverbs like “abruptly”, “vividly” and “inexplicably” show the narrator’s heightened emotional state. Further, violent verbs, such as “battered”, “roaring” and “rattling” convey the storm’s chaotic energy, while the simile “like a ship at sea” suggests that even the house, a supposed place of safety, feels unstable. In addition, the gothic setting is heightened by the repeated descriptions of “pitch blackness” and “howling darkness”. Rhetorical questions reveal the narrator’s confusion and isolation: “How could there be?”, “and who was now in this house with me?”. Negative language, like “no one”, “nothing” and “not even heard a footstep” builds disbelief and fear.

The narrator’s growing panic is portrayed when he gropes in the dark, stumbles over Spider, and drops the torch, which smashes on the floor. The verbs “groped”, “stumbled”, “dropped” and “cursed” show his increasing desperation. His frustration and tension explode when he “drummed my fists upon the floorboards”.

The dog Spider provides both tension and relief in the text. On the one hand, Spider heightens the narrator’s sense of responsibility and fear; on the other, Spider offers a moment of comfort when she licks his hand and he hugs her “warm body”. However, the final line reintroduces dread: “again I heard that child’s terrible cry borne on the gusts towards me” leaving the reader in no doubt that the supernatural threat remains.

Overall, Susan Hill creates an atmosphere of escalating tension using the narrator’s fearful thoughts, the unsettling descriptions of ghostly presences and carefully crafted language and structure, immersing the reader in a world of gothic terror and uncertainty.

In conclusion, Moniza Alvi presents strong feelings in ‘An Unknown Girl’ by using vivid imagery, intimate language and a reflective structure to show how a brief encounter becomes a lasting symbol of connection and identity. In The Necklace, Guy de Maupassant presents money and possessions as symbols of social status, personal worth and ultimately, human folly. Through Madame Loisel’s obsession with wealth and appearance and the devastating consequences of a single lost item, the narrative explores how materialism can shape and ruin lives.

From the beginning, the narrator shows that Madame Loisel is deeply dissatisfied with her modest circumstances. Though married to a junior clerk and in a comfortable house, she feels she was “intended for a life of refinement and luxury”. Maupassant describes her constant unhappiness and longing for possessions she cannot afford: “the peeling walls, the battered chairs, and the ugly curtains” torment her, where another woman of similar status might not even notice them. This shows that, for Madame Loisel, material surroundings are not just decorative, but they define her sense of identity and happiness.

Madame Loisel’s dreams are rich with vivid imagery, reinforcing the importance she places on money and class. She imagines “tall, bronze candelabras”, “old silk” and “exquisite dishes served on fabulous china plates”. These ornate details contrast sharply with her actual life and indicate her desire to escape into a world of grandeur. Maupassant suggests that her dissatisfaction is not about need, but about perceived social worth; she doesn’t want to simply be comfortable, but she wants to be admired and envied.

Through negative language and repetition, Maupassant further reinforces her dissatisfaction and longing. The phrase “no dowry, no expectations, no means of meeting some rich, important man” uses a tricolon to emphasise all that she lacks, setting a tone of deprivation from the outset. Similarly, the list “no fine dresses, no jewellery, nothing” underscores how utterly deprived she feels of the luxuries she believes she deserves. Her constant unhappiness is captured through the repeated phrase “all the time”, showing that thoughts of wealth dominate her daily existence.

This desire for status is shown again when Madame Loisel receives an invitation to the Ministry Ball. Rather than being excited, she reacts with bitterness: “What earthly use is that to me?” The invitation is dismissed because she lacks the dress and jewellery to present herself as wealthy. This response highlights how possessions, for her, are necessary to be seen as valuable. Her husband’s practical view in suggesting that she wears flowers instead of jewellery is rejected, as she fears this would be “humiliating” among richer women.

Similes and personification are also employed to depict poverty unflatteringly. When faced with the idea of attending the ball without jewellery, Madame Loisel protests, “I’ll look like a church mouse” equating poverty with insignificance. Even the  hackney cabs in the street are described with shame, emerging only “after dusk, as if ashamed to parade their poverty in the full light of day”. These images heighten the humiliation and burden Madame Loisel associates with being poor.

Maupassant uses the borrowed necklace as the ultimate symbol of how possessions can deceive and destroy. Madame Loisel borrows a diamond necklace from her wealthy friend, which gives her the confidence and joy she has long desired. At the ball, she is “elegant, graceful, radiant”, her beauty enhanced not by who she is, but by what she wears. However, when she loses the necklace, her life descends into “grim poverty” and “heavy domestic work”. The irony is that the necklace was a worthless imitation. This cruel twist reveals how her obsession with appearances led her to suffer needlessly for a symbol of wealth that had no real value.

Maupassant contrasts Madame Loisel’s emotions through his choice of adverbs. When she has nothing, her reactions are marked by frustration. She behaves “peevishly”, “irritably” and responds “shortly”. In contrast, when she has the chance to possess luxury, even temporarily, her emotions shift dramatically. She opens the invitation “eagerly”, borrows the necklace “extravagantly”, and dances “wildly” and “ecstatically” at the ball. These contrasting adverbs reflect how her mood is shaped entirely by her relationship to wealth.

In conclusion, The Necklace presents money and possessions not only as measures of social class but as powerful forces that shape identity, perception and destiny. Maupassant uses Madame Loisel’s tragedy to critique a society obsessed with wealth and appearance and to expose the devastating cost of this illusion.
