> **Indicative content**
> 
> - **Formula**: Budgeted cost per hire = Total cost / Total hires **[1 mark]**
> - **Budgeted cost per hire** = \$1 260 000 / 450 = **\$2800** **[1 mark]**
> - **Actual cost per hire** = Budgeted + adverse variance = \$2800 + \$500 = **\$3300** **[1 mark OFR]**
> 
> *Alternative phrasing*: 1260 / 450 = 2.8 (\$000), then 2.8 + 0.5 = 3.3 (\$000) = \$3300
> 
> **Own figure rule (OFR)** applies.
> 
> Accept all valid responses

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two-step process: (1) divide total budgeted cost by total hires (the *budgeted* cost per hire), (2) ADD the adverse variance of \$500 to get *actual* cost per hire. Adverse = costs went UP from budget, so you add. Watch the units: Table 1.1 totals are in \$000s, so 1260 / 450 = 2.8 in thousands = \$2800. Don't forget to add \$500 — forgetting it is the most common slip.

### Q2
> **[mark-scheme]**
> | Item | Budgeted (\$000) | Actual (\$000) | Variance | F / A |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Revenue | 500 | 600 | **100** | **Favourable (F)** |
> | Direct costs | 300 | 350 | **50** | **Adverse (A)** |
> | Indirect costs | 150 | 125 | **25** | **Favourable (F)** |
> 
> **1 mark for each correctly calculated variance** (including correct F / A label).
> 
> - **x** = 100 F **[1 mark]**
> - **y** = 50 A **[1 mark]**
> - **z** = 25 F **[1 mark]**
> 
> Ignore + / − signs. Allow 'unfavourable' instead of 'adverse'.
> 
> Accept all valid responses

> **[exam-tip]**
> F / A logic: revenue ABOVE budget is favourable; costs ABOVE budget are adverse (you spent more than planned); costs BELOW budget are favourable (you saved). Don't lose easy marks by forgetting to label F / A — the mark is for the calculation AND the label together.

### Q3
> **[mark-scheme]**
> | Level | AO1 Knowledge and understanding (2 marks) | AO2 Application (2 marks) | AO3 Analysis (2 marks) | AO4 Evaluation (6 marks) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **3** |   |   |   | **5–6 marks — Developed evaluation in context**: developed judgement / conclusion in the business context; developed evaluative comments which balance some key arguments in context |
> | **2** | **2 marks**: developed knowledge of relevant key term(s) / factor(s) | **2 marks**: developed application of relevant point(s) to the business context | **2 marks**: developed analysis identifying connections between causes, impacts and / or consequences | **3–4 marks — Developed evaluation**: developed judgement / conclusion; developed evaluative comments which balance some key arguments |
> | **1** | **1 mark**: limited knowledge | **1 mark**: limited application | **1 mark**: limited analysis | **1–2 marks — Limited evaluation**: judgement with limited supporting evidence; an attempt is made to balance the arguments |
> | **0** | No creditable response | No creditable response | No creditable response | No creditable response |
> 
> **Indicative content** — *AO1 Knowledge and understanding*
> 
> - Budgets — role and purpose
> - Variance from the budget
> - Business factors which may be important to success
> 
> **AO2 Application** (farming)
> 
> - Farming items — arable, crops, fruit, livestock, cattle, dairy, sheep, chicken, fertiliser, farm machinery
> 
> **AO3 Analysis**
> 
> - Budgets encourage forward thinking / planning so can plan for future financial needs
> - Provide discipline and a framework for planning and control
> - Many farmers unlikely to have financial knowledge of setting budgets but are still successful due to experience
> - Farming businesses may set budgets, but this may be informal as part of their normal seasonal planning
> - A farm may operate for survival and consider budgeting irrelevant, e.g. small subsistence farm
> - Profit-driven farm may see budgets as essential for achieving profit
> - External conditions are often unpredictable in farming making budgeting difficult
> - Even if variances are monitored, natural conditions may make changes to budget impossible or difficult
> - May give stakeholders of large commercial farms the profit they judge success by
> 
> **AO4 Evaluation**
> 
> A judgement / conclusion is made as to the importance of budgets for the success of a farming business. Possible evaluative points:
> 
> - Effectiveness of budget may only be seen at the end of the trading period, which can be a long time in some types of farming
> - How is success measured — survival / security for family members / better quality of produce / increased number of customers / increased revenue / amount of profit / ability to compete / market share / expansion / improved reputation?
> - It is meaningless to set budgets if the information included in them is badly researched or recorded — does the business have suitable employees and experience to produce budgets correctly?
> - Is the success of a farming business, especially a small one, more dependent on the experience of farming rather than knowledge of finance and budgeting?
> 
> **Evaluation mark band guidance**
> 
> - **Eval 6** — developed judgement / conclusion made in context (farming business) **[6 marks]**
> - **Eval 5** — developed evaluative comments given in context **[5 marks]**
> - **Eval 4** — developed judgement / conclusion made, not in context **[4 marks]**
> - **Eval 3** — developed evaluative comments, not in context **[3 marks]**
> - **Eval 2** — judgement / conclusion with little supporting evidence, not in context **[2 marks]**
> - **Eval 1** — an attempt is made to balance arguments **[1 mark]**
> - **Eval 0** — no evaluation
> 
> Accept all valid responses

> **[exam-tip]**
> Farming is the key context twist — weather, harvest timing and livestock disease make outcomes hard to forecast, undermining budget accuracy. Argue budgets are important for large commercial farms (profit-driven, multiple stakeholders) but less so for subsistence / family farms (experience-driven). Always name 'farming' in your judgement to secure Eval 5–6.
