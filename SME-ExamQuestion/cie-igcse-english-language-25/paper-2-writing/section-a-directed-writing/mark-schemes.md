### Q1
### Model answer

In this extract the writer's attitude to ambition is wary and quietly critical. Ambition is treated almost as a condition with a "side effect", a clinical word that frames it as something potentially harmful rather than admirable. The writer warns that it makes people "focus too stubbornly" on a single goal, so that anything else "may seem useless" and the "enjoyment of the 'journey'" is lost - a narrowing of life rather than an enriching of it. The criticism then turns moral: ambition makes people "inherently more selfish", pursuing personal goals "at the possible expense of your family, friends and community". By citing "experts" who rank "relationships" above ambition, the writer presents this scepticism as reasoned rather than merely personal.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Responses *might* use the following:
> 
> - wary / critical - ambition has a damaging "side effect"
> - sees it as narrowing - people "focus too stubbornly" and lose the "enjoyment of the 'journey'"
> - morally concerned - ambition makes people "inherently more selfish"
> - alert to social cost - goals pursued at the "expense of your family, friends and community"
> - measured / reasoned - draws on "experts" who value "relationships" more highly
> 
> **Why this answer would get full marks**
> 
> The response evaluates how the writer's cautionary attitude is constructed within this extract rather than simply labelling it "negative". It notes the clinical framing of "side effect", traces the move from a practical criticism (narrow focus) to a moral one (selfishness), and shows how the appeal to "experts" makes the scepticism feel reasoned. Short phrases are quoted verbatim and tied to specific points about tone, so the judgement stays grounded in the given extract throughout

### Q3
### Model answer

In this extract the writer's attitude to old buildings is dismissive and impatient. They are framed not as treasures but as problems that "get in the way" of better uses for the land, such as "parks, shops and recreational space". The blunt advice that if you "can't find any good reason to keep it" you "should consider demolishing" reveals an unsentimental, almost businesslike stance, with no concession to history or affection. The writer reinforces this by piling up the language of decay - buildings turn "shabby and unsightly", hide "poor ventilation, outdated wiring" and develop "weaker foundations" until they are "unstable or unsafe". By stressing that repair costs can be "prohibitive", the writer treats demolition as the obvious, common-sense conclusion rather than a loss.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Responses *might* use the following:
> 
> - dismissive / unsentimental - old buildings "get in the way" of more useful projects

> - critical of their condition - "shabby and unsightly", "weaker foundations", "unstable or unsafe"
> - pragmatic about cost - repair is "prohibitive"
> - progress-focused - the land could "offer more benefit to the community"
> 
> **Why this answer would get full marks**
> 
> The response goes beyond simply stating that the writer is "negative" to evaluate how the attitude is built within this extract. It tracks the movement from the buildings being an obstacle, to the blunt recommendation to demolish, to the accumulation of decay and cost. Short quoted phrases are woven in verbatim and are used to support a precise reading of tone (dismissive, impatient, pragmatic), rather than being dropped in without comment. ### Q4
### Model answer

Ranit's father is confident and wholehearted in his support, convinced that intensive training benefits his son with no real drawbacks. He "believes that his son benefits hugely from such opportunities", a strong claim that frames the training as a clear advantage rather than a gamble. He is aware that others doubt him - "I know some parents think I'm insane" - but is unshaken, defending his choice by arguing that "the relentless emphasis on academics" in his own childhood "wasn't healthy either". This suggests he sees sport as a healthier path than pure study. He also reframes the training as broadly beneficial, claiming the "determination" Ranit develops has "spilled over into his academic work". His insistence that "there's no downside for him" reveals an unreservedly positive attitude, though his admission of the "sacrifices we make as a family" shows he is not entirely blind to its cost.

### Q6
### Model answer

Paolo's attitude to tourism is ambivalent: appreciative of its benefits but quietly frustrated by its costs. He concedes a genuine gain when he notes "the city's facilities are open all year now", whereas "everything used to close in the winter" - a practical improvement he is willing to acknowledge. Yet his frustration is just as real. Calling the summer traffic "suffocating" suggests the influx feels oppressive, and his remark that events are "still organised around the visitors" implies locals come second. The word "still" hints at long-standing resentment. His ironic reference to "improving the visitor experience" reveals scepticism about the official language of tourism, and the narrator's note that he "betrays only a hint of irony" shows his criticism is measured rather than openly hostile - he is restrained, but unconvinced.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Responses *might* use the following:
> 
> - Appreciative of practical benefits: "the city's facilities are open all year now" where "everything used to close in the winter"
> - Frustrated by overcrowding: the summer traffic is "suffocating"
> - Resentful that locals come second: events are "still organised around the visitors"
> - Sceptical of official rhetoric: ironic reference to "improving the visitor experience"
> - Measured rather than openly hostile: he "betrays only a hint of irony"
> 
> **Why this answer would get full marks**
> 
> This response shows clear understanding of Paolo's attitude by capturing its central feature - its ambivalence - and weighing both sides with evidence drawn only from the extract. It does more than label the attitude: it explains what generates the appreciation (year-round facilities) and what generates the frustration (overcrowding, events arranged for visitors).
