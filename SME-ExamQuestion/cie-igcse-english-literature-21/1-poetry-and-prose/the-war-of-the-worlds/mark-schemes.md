### Q1
## Model answer

In The War of the Worlds, Wells strikingly conveys the narrator’s feelings about the Martians by charting a powerful emotional journey from curiosity and disbelief to horror and panic. Wells presents the Martians not simply as enemies, but as creatures so alien and powerful that they destroy the narrator’s sense of safety, certainty and human superiority.

At the start of the invasion, the narrator’s feelings are shaped by fascination rather than terror. He approaches the first cylinder with the mindset of a rational observer, sharing the public excitement as people gather to witness something extraordinary. Wells conveys this early attitude through the narrator’s detailed attention to what he can see and hear, as if careful observation will bring understanding and control. This creates dramatic tension because the narrator initially believes the situation can be explained logically.

However, Wells quickly replaces curiosity with unease by emphasising how mysterious and unnatural the Martians’ arrival is. The narrator becomes unsettled by strange mechanical sounds and movements coming from within the cylinder. Wells uses this gradual build-up to show the narrator’s growing suspicion as he realises the object is not simply a scientific wonder but a threat. Darkness and concealment become frightening to him, because he cannot see what is coming. This creates a powerful emotional shift: the narrator begins to sense that he is facing something beyond ordinary experience, and his feelings change from interest to dread.

When the Martians finally appear, Wells conveys the narrator’s horror through vivid and repulsive imagery. The Martians are not presented as heroic or human-like, but as grotesque and disturbing, with their bodies and movements designed to provoke revulsion. The narrator’s feelings are not just fear of violence, but fear of the unknown: he cannot relate to them as fellow beings. Wells emphasises this through language that focuses on unnatural texture, motion and behaviour, making the Martians seem both animalistic and alien. By making the narrator describe them in such detail, Wells shows him trying to process the unthinkable, even as his body responds instinctively with shock and disgust.

Wells also conveys fear through the narrator’s physical reactions, which make the terror more intense. Moments of shock are often presented as immediate sensations, such as chills, paralysis or breathless panic. This makes the narrator’s feelings believable because they are rooted in the body, not just the mind. Wells frequently shows the narrator frozen or overwhelmed before he forces himself to move, capturing the way real fear can disable someone. These moments create striking tension because the narrator knows he must escape, but his fear delays him, making danger feel even closer.

As the invasion continues, Wells conveys the narrator’s growing helplessness and despair. The narrator repeatedly witnesses the failure of human authority and defence, and this shapes his feelings about the Martians as unstoppable forces rather than opponents. Wells shows that human weapons and strategies barely matter against Martian technology, which makes the narrator’s fear deepen into hopelessness. His emotional state becomes dominated by survival instinct, as he begins to act not with confidence but with desperation. The Martians therefore represent not only physical danger but the collapse of human power, which is psychologically devastating for the narrator.

Wells also conveys the narrator’s feelings about the Martians through the way he describes crowds and society collapsing. He witnesses panic spreading as people lose their sense of order, and this amplifies his fear. The narrator is not only frightened of the Martians, but of what human beings become when fear takes over: selfish, irrational and desperate. Moments of stampede and chaos show that the invasion destroys civilisation from the inside as well as the outside. This makes the narrator’s emotional experience even more overwhelming, because he feels surrounded by danger in every direction: from the Martians and from the breakdown of human behaviour.

In conclusion, Wells strikingly conveys the narrator’s feelings  of curiosity, horror, panic and helplessness. By using first-person narration, grotesque imagery, intense sensory detail and scenes of collapsing order, Wells makes the narrator’s fear vivid and convincing.

In this extract from The War of the Worlds, Wells powerfully creates tension by turning public excitement into sudden fear as the Martian begins to emerge from the cylinder. Using first-person narration, disturbing sensory detail and a slow, suspenseful reveal, Wells makes the moment feel claustrophobic and dangerous.

Wells begins by presenting the crowd as unstable and uncontrollable. The opening line, “The crowd swayed a little”, suggests a mass of bodies moving unpredictably, as if panic could break out at any second. The narrator “elbowed” his way through, which makes the scene feel cramped and pressurised. Although the crowd is “greatly excited”, Wells quickly undercuts this with the “peculiar humming sound from the pit”. The adjective “peculiar” suggests something unnatural, and because the sound comes from below it creates dread, as if danger is rising towards them.

Tension increases through Ogilvy’s warning: “help keep these idiots back”. The word “idiots” shows how foolish and careless the crowd is, while “We don’t know what’s in the confounded thing” highlights uncertainty. Wells suggests the crowd treats the cylinder like entertainment, even though it is clearly threatening. This ignorance creates suspense because the reader senses that curiosity will be punished.

Wells then shows how quickly control collapses. A young shop assistant is “standing on the cylinder” because the crowd had “pushed him in”. This detail is disturbing because it reveals the crowd itself as dangerous, sacrificing someone through reckless excitement. Structurally, it also foreshadows the human cost of the invasion: the first victims are not soldiers, but ordinary people trapped by circumstances and stupidity.

The suspense intensifies as Wells describes the cylinder opening slowly from “within”. The end “was being screwed out”, and the “shining screw” projecting suggests mechanical force and inevitability. The narrator “narrowly missed” falling onto it, which adds immediate physical danger. When the lid falls with a “ringing concussion”, Wells uses sharp sound imagery to make the moment feel sudden and irreversible, as if a seal has been broken.

Wells delays the reveal to build further tension. The narrator sees a “perfectly black” cavity, and the darkness makes the threat more frightening because it hides what is coming. He admits he had “the sunset in my eyes”, suggesting blurred vision and vulnerability. This makes the reader share his fear, since he cannot clearly see the danger until it is almost too late.

When the Martian finally begins to appear, Wells’ language becomes increasingly unsettling. The narrator sees “greyish billowy movements”, vague and shapeless, making the creature feel unnatural. Then he describes “two luminous discs – like eyes”, a simile that creates terror because the darkness is suddenly alive and watching. The tentacles are compared to “a little grey snake”, and the verb “wriggled” suggests disgusting, uncontrolled movement. The fact the tentacle reaches “towards me” makes the threat personal, showing the narrator is no longer a spectator.

Wells also builds tension through the crowd’s reaction. A “shriek” from behind signals fear spreading instantly. The narrator sees “astonishment giving place to horror”, and the crowd moves backwards in panic. Crucially, the narrator becomes isolated: “I found myself alone”. This is terrifying because it leaves him exposed at the moment danger becomes real. Wells intensifies the drama as the narrator admits “ungovernable terror gripped me” and he stands “petrified”. These words suggest fear is so extreme it paralyses him, prolonging suspense because he cannot escape.

Finally, the Martian is described in horrifying physical detail. It rises “slowly and painfully”, making it seem real and unstoppable. Its body “glistened like wet leather”, a simile that creates revulsion through texture. Wells focuses on its face: “Two large dark-coloured eyes… regarding me steadfastly.” The adverb implies intelligence and intention, making it more frightening than a mindless animal. The mouth “panted” and “dropped saliva”, adding grotesque detail that turns fear into disgust. The final line claims its appearance creates “strange horror”, reinforcing that the threat is beyond normal experience.

In conclusion, Wells powerfully creates tension through first-person immediacy, suspenseful delay and increasingly disturbing imagery. The reckless crowd, the gradual emergence of the Martian and the narrator’s paralysing fear combine to make the moment intensely frightening and unforgettable.

### Q3
## Model answer

In The War of the Worlds, H. G. Wells strikingly depicts the Martian invasion of Earth as sudden, terrifying and unstoppable. Instead of presenting a fair fight or a heroic war, Wells shows humanity as helpless against a force with superior technology and inhuman detachment. Through the novel’s first-person form, its escalating structure and its vivid, unsettling language, Wells makes the invasion feel both physically destructive and psychologically overwhelming.

Wells structures the invasion so that it begins with curiosity rather than immediate horror. The strange arrival from space draws crowds who treat it like a spectacle, which creates dramatic irony because the reader quickly senses danger. This slow build makes the invasion more striking because it shows how unprepared humans are. Wells delays the full appearance of violence, allowing tension to rise as the cylinder is observed and its movement described in controlled, mechanical detail. The careful pacing makes the later chaos feel sudden and brutal, as the reader experiences the moment when ordinary life collapses.

Wells’ language also makes the Martians frightening because they are presented as unnatural and unfamiliar. He avoids describing them in ways that would make them seem human-like, instead emphasising their alien physicality and strange presence. This unfamiliarity creates fear because the narrator, and the reader, cannot rely on normal expectations. Wells often focuses on unsettling details and odd movement, suggesting something grotesque and difficult to comprehend. The invasion therefore becomes not just an attack by outsiders, but a disturbing reminder that humanity may not be the most advanced species in the universe.

The Martians’ weaponry is one of Wells’ most striking methods. The Heat-Ray destroys with instant force, giving humans no chance to defend themselves. Wells presents its impact as sudden and absolute, making the violence feel shocking and unfair. Crucially, he does not describe a “battle” in glorious terms. Instead, civilians panic, crowds break apart and destruction happens too quickly for resistance. This removes any sense of heroism and makes the invasion more disturbing because it feels brutally realistic: people are not fighting, they are simply trying to survive.

Wells intensifies the horror through imagery of fire and ruin. Familiar homes and towns are turned into burning wreckage, showing how quickly safety disappears. Fire becomes a repeated symbol of domination, suggesting the Martians can erase human life and civilisation with ease. Wells’ descriptive language often emphasises spread and scale, creating the impression that destruction multiplies too quickly to be controlled. The result is an invasion that feels apocalyptic, as if the landscape itself is being rewritten by violence.

The fighting-machines also make the invasion terrifying. Wells portrays these tripods as towering over the land, using their size to reduce humans to insignificant figures. Their movement is described as unnatural and fast, giving them the energy of predators rather than ordinary machines. Wells blurs the boundary between monster and technology, which makes them more frightening because they seem both mechanical and alive. Their dominance over the landscape reinforces the idea that humans are physically and intellectually outmatched.

Form plays a major role in making the invasion vivid. The novel is told through a first-person narrator, and this creates immediacy. Wells does not give the reader a calm, all-knowing overview: instead, we experience the invasion through a witness who is confused, frightened and overwhelmed. This limited viewpoint makes the invasion feel chaotic and unpredictable, because the narrator only understands events as they unfold. His fear becomes the reader’s fear, and the invasion feels more real because it is filtered through personal shock rather than distant reporting.

Wells also uses structure to show invasion as the collapse of authority and order. Human weapons and armies repeatedly fail to stop the Martians, undermining confidence in civilisation’s strength. As the invasion spreads, society breaks down into panic and flight, and the narrator becomes a witness to disorder replacing control. Wells shows that invasion is not only the destruction of buildings, but the destruction of stability itself.

Ultimately, Wells makes the invasion most horrifying through the Martians’ cold indifference. They show no pity and no moral restraint, treating humans as obstacles rather than individuals. This emotional detachment makes the invasion feel even more hopeless, because it suggests humans cannot appeal to reason or compassion.

In conclusion, Wells strikingly depicts the Martian invasion through suspenseful pacing, terrifying imagery and the immediacy of first-person form. By showing rapid destruction, failed resistance and human panic, Wells creates a powerful vision of civilisation collapsing.

In this extract Wells makes the moment powerfully dramatic by showing the Martian invasion erupting into ordinary life with terrifying speed.  The moment is powerful because the reader experiences the invasion as immediate, chaotic and overwhelming.

Wells begins by building tension through uncertainty. The “unseen batteries” across the river are described as “unseen because of the trees”, and the repetition of “unseen” emphasises confusion and vulnerability. The battle is “near us and yet invisible”, which makes it more frightening because neither the narrator nor the crowd can fully grasp what is happening. The short sentence “A woman screamed” breaks the descriptive flow, creating a sudden emotional jolt and signalling the first crack in public calm.

He then strengthens the moment through a striking contrast between peaceful countryside and violent warfare. The narrator describes “flat meadows”, “cows feeding unconcernedly” and “silvery pollard willows motionless in the warm sunlight”. This gentle, pastoral imagery creates a sense of safety and normality, which makes the invasion feel more shocking when it interrupts it. The cows being “unconcernedly” calm is significant because nature continues as if nothing is wrong, while humans are overwhelmed. Wells uses this contrast to show how fragile human security is.

When the Martians appear, Wells uses structure and pacing to intensify fear. The sequence “one, two, three, four” creates the effect of rapid multiplication, making the enemy seem overwhelming in number and speed. At first they appear as “little cowled figures”, which briefly makes them seem distant or even deceptively small. However, Wells quickly expands the threat by describing their “armoured bodies” and how they move “as fast as flying birds”. The simile suggests unnatural speed, making them feel less like living creatures and more like advanced machines. The invasion becomes powerful here because the reader sees the enemy grow from distant shapes into unstoppable attackers.

Wells adds further horror through the Heat-Ray. It is described as “ghostly” and “terrible”, language that suggests something supernatural and unstoppable rather than human technology. The verb “smote” gives the attack a violent, punishing force, as though the town is being struck down. The narrator’s memory that he has “already seen” the Heat-Ray intensifies tension because he understands what is coming before the crowd does. Wells creates power through anticipation: the reader shares the narrator’s dread.

The crowd’s reaction also makes the moment powerful because Wells captures shock turning into panic with realism. The people become “horror-struck”, followed by a moment of silence where there is “no screaming or shouting”. This pause is chilling because it shows fear becoming so intense it freezes movement. Then chaos begins with “a hoarse murmur and a movement of feet”, suggesting panic spreading like a wave. Wells includes disturbing detail, such as the man “too frightened to drop the portmanteau”, showing terror makes people irrational. The narrator is physically struck and “staggering”, which highlights how quickly humans become helpless bodies in a stampede.

The first-person narration increases the emotional intensity. The narrator’s thoughts become urgent and fragmented: “To get under water! That was it!” The exclamation marks convey panic, but also the desperate clarity of survival instinct. His warning “Get under water!” is “unheeded”, showing communication breaks down as fear takes over. Wells presents society collapsing into individual instinct, which makes the moment feel brutal and realistic.

Finally, Wells ends the extract with frantic movement that reinforces the powerlessness of humanity. The narrator “rushed” into the water, and the repetition of action verbs creates speed and desperation. The beach is “muddy and slippery”, suggesting even escape is dangerous. The Martian “towered overhead”, emphasising the terrifying difference in scale between invader and human.

In conclusion, Wells makes this such a powerful moment through contrast, sensory detail and the intensity of first-person fear. The countryside becomes a battlefield, panic spreads through the crowd and the Martians appear as unstoppable forces.
