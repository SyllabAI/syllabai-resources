### Q1
## Model answer

In ‘Hunting Snake’, Judith Wright powerfully conveys the speaker’s mixture of awe and fear through natural imagery and a subtle shift in tone. The poem captures a brief but intense encounter with a snake, and Wright transforms this moment into a reflection on the beauty, danger, and authority of the natural world.

From the outset, Wright creates a peaceful, almost idyllic atmosphere, making the snake’s arrival all the more shocking. The opening line describes the landscape as “Sun-warmed in this late season’s grace”, a gentle and harmonious image suggesting contentment. The phrase “grace” implies a blessing or gift, conveying the speaker’s initial sense of peace within nature. This tranquillity is strengthened by “autumn’s gentlest sky”, an image of safety and warmth. The setting lulls both the speaker and the reader into a false sense of security, heightening the contrast when the snake appears. Wright’s use of a peaceful setting emphasises how awe and fear burst unexpectedly into ordinary moments.

The sudden shift in tone when the snake arrives conveys immediate fear. The poet writes that “we walked, and froze half-through a pace”, communicating a moment of shock so intense that it literally halts movement. The verb “froze” conveys both sudden stillness and instinctive fear, suggesting that the speaker’s reaction is physical and uncontrollable. The snake’s movement is described as “reeling by”, a dynamic verb that gives it a swift, unstoppable momentum. The encounter is brief but powerful, and the speaker reacts instinctively, feeling overwhelmed by the snake’s presence.

Wright presents the snake as both dangerous and majestic, creating a complex blend of awe and fear. Its “Head down, tongue flickering on the trail” conjures stereotypical images of a predator: focused, precise, and hunting. The repeated sibilance in “sun glazed his curves of diamond scale” echoes the silent movement of the snake and gives the image a shimmering quality. Calling the scales “diamond” not only suggests their shape but also gives the snake a jewel-like beauty. This combination of threat and splendour evokes admiration alongside anxiety. The speaker admits, “we lost breath to watch him pass”, a phrase that can suggest fear, awe, or a mixture of both. Their breathlessness reflects both the snake’s beauty and its capacity to cause harm.

The speaker’s awe becomes even clearer as Wright highlights the snake’s power and purpose. The creature moves with “fierce intent”, indicating a natural authority that the humans instinctively recognise. Wright expresses the speaker’s astonishment through understatement: “we scarcely thought”. In this moment, rational thought is replaced by instinctive fascination. They track the snake’s movement with their eyes, unable to look away — “our eyes went with him as he went.” This mirrored movement reinforces the speaker’s mesmerised state, and the gentle repetition of “went” suggests quiet, almost reverent observation.

When the snake finally disappears, the poem shifts to a tone of quiet reflection. The speaker describes him as “Cold, dark and splendid”, a triad of adjectives that unites fear and beauty. “Cold” and “dark” suggest danger, mystery, and inhumanity, while “splendid” conveys admiration and awe. The juxtaposition captures how the natural world can evoke contradictory emotions: fascination mixed with fear, admiration mixed with vulnerability. The snake’s disappearance “into the grass that hid his prey” restores the quiet landscape, yet it also reminds us of the ongoing cycles of life and death happening unnoticed around the speakers.

The concluding lines return to the human perspective. The speaker notes that after the snake was gone, “We took a deeper breath of day”, suggesting relief but also renewed appreciation for the world around them. Their “deeper breath” may indicate that the encounter has left them shaken, aware of how thin the line between safety and danger can be. The moment of awe has heightened their sensitivity to life itself. When they “looked at each other, and went on”, the understated ending reflects how profound experiences can be absorbed quietly, leaving a subtle but significant emotional impact.

Through this brief encounter, Wright vividly conveys awe and fear by showing how nature’s beauty and danger are intertwined. The snake is not demonised; instead, it is presented as a powerful, self-contained force deserving of respect. The speaker’s reactions — physical shock, breathless admiration, silent watching — reveal a deep emotional humility before nature’s authority. The poem ultimately celebrates the mysterious and commanding presence of the natural world.

In ‘Sonnet 18’, Shakespeare presents one of the most memorable and striking comparisons in English poetry, contrasting the beauty of a lover with the instability and imperfection of a summer’s day. Through vivid imagery, carefully controlled rhythm, and a tonal shift in the volta, Shakespeare conveys both admiration for the lover and awe at poetry’s ability to preserve beauty against time.

The opening question appears gentle and contemplative, yet it is also playful. The speaker already knows the answer will be “no”: the lover surpasses summer. The line “Thou art more lovely and more temperate” immediately establishes the superiority of the lover. The word “lovely” suggests physical beauty, but “temperate” suggests emotional balance and gentleness. Summer may be beautiful, but it is inconsistent; the lover, by contrast, is presented as harmonious and constant. This subtle, precise contrast sets the tone for the rest of the poem.

Shakespeare then describes summer in a way that highlights its flaws. “Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May” presents a violent, almost disruptive force, with “rough winds” shaking fragile “darling buds”. The choice of “darling” emphasises tenderness, making the violence of the winds feel more striking, and showing that summer cannot protect beauty. Summer is temporary, too: “summer’s lease hath all too short a date”. This metaphor implies that its beauty is only borrowed, never permanent. The lover, however, is set up as someone who does not suffer from these natural limits.

The imperfections of summer continue in the next two lines: “Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines / And often is his gold complexion dimmed”. The “eye of heaven” metaphor for the sun elevates the image, but emphasises unpredictability — sometimes too bright, sometimes clouded, often unreliable. Shakespeare’s imagery highlights summer’s extremes: too hot, too dim, too brief. This creates a clear contrast with the lover, who is constant and even-tempered. The contrast is further strengthened by the universal truth stated in line 7: “And every fair from fair sometime declines”. Beauty fades not only because of natural causes, but also “by chance”. The word “untrimmed” emphasises the inevitability of decline.

The volta at line 9 introduces a striking shift: “But thy eternal summer shall not fade”. The lover is given an “eternal summer”, a season made permanent. The adjective “eternal” is crucial: it marks a move away from the natural world into something timeless and idealised. Shakespeare presents the lover not only as more beautiful than summer but also beyond the reach of decay. The line “Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow’st” uses the language of ownership to suggest that the lover’s beauty is secure, not borrowed like summer’s lease. This stability directly contrasts with earlier imagery of decline.

Shakespeare then goes further, defying death itself: “Nor shall death brag thou wander’st in his shade”. The personification of death as boastful makes the defiance more striking. Death cannot “brag” because the lover will never be claimed by him. The idea that the lover “grow’st” into “eternal lines” underscores the power of poetry itself. It is the poem — the “eternal lines” of the sonnet — that guarantees survival. Through this bold assertion, Shakespeare shifts the comparison: the lover is not merely better than summer; they are immortalised in art.

The final couplet confirms this idea with complete confidence: “So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.” The repetition of “so long” stretches time into the distant future, and the simplicity of the phrasing reinforces its certainty. Shakespeare is not simply praising his lover; he is asserting the eternal power of poetry. As long as readers exist, the lover will continue to “live”. The comparison with summer therefore becomes symbolic: summer fades, but art endures.

In conclusion, Shakespeare compares his lover to summer only to show that the comparison ultimately fails — not because summer is imperfect, but because the lover’s beauty and constancy surpass natural boundaries. Through vivid imagery of nature’s instability, a confident and uplifting volta, and the bold celebration of poetry’s immortalising power, Shakespeare creates a comparison that is as striking as it is memorable.

In ‘Carpet-weavers, Morocco’, Rumens offers a deeply moving portrayal of childhood shaped by labour, tradition, and sacrifice. Rumens intertwines innocence with exploitation, showing how childhood dreams and colours are woven into a future that does not belong to the children themselves.

From the opening line, Rumens places the children “at the loom of another world”, establishing both the physical reality of their labour and the symbolic distance between their lives and the futures they are helping to build. The phrase “another world” implies that the beauty, luxury, and spiritual richness their carpets will bring belong elsewhere — to customers, merchants, and mosques — not to them. This immediately creates a sense of sacrifice: their creativity is devoted to purposes beyond their own childhood enjoyment. Rumens’ use of “children” foregrounds innocence, making their labour seem all the more poignant.

The poet’s description of the children’s appearance — “their braids are oiled and black, their dresses bright” — initially paints an image of colour and vibrancy. However, the brightness contrasts with the seriousness of their work, reinforcing the tension between childhood and obligation. Their “assorted heights” that “would make a melodious chime” is a gentle, lyrical image, suggesting that the children together form something harmonious and beautiful. Yet the phrase “would make” hints at a possibility rather than a reality: their potential for play and childhood unity exists only as a metaphor, not an experience. Rumens subtly suggests that their childhood “melody” is imagined rather than lived.

The comparison “They watch their flickering knots like television” introduces a modern reference that highlights how different their lives are from those of children elsewhere. Instead of passively enjoying entertainment, they focus intently on “flickering knots”, the movements of their fingers replacing the movement on a screen. The simile emphasises how childhood imagination is channelled into repetitive work. The effect is both striking and sad: labour has replaced leisure. Rumens then shifts to religious imagery — “As the garden of Islam grows, the bench will be raised” — which suggests a connection between their work and cultural tradition. As they age or gain skill, they “will lace the dark-rose veins of the tree-tops”, creating increasingly intricate patterns. The beautiful metaphor of “dark-rose veins” reinforces the artistry of their work, but it also hints at sacrifice — veins carrying lifeblood. The children pour themselves into these carpets, giving up their time, energy, and youth.

The third stanza focuses on the carpet’s journey: “The carpet will travel in the merchant’s truck. / It will be spread by the servants of the mosque.” This emphasises how the children’s work serves people and purposes far removed from their everyday lives. Rumens uses the repeated “will” to show inevitability; the carpet moves forward into the world, while the children remain behind. The description “Deep and soft, it will give when heaped with prayer” highlights the comfort the carpet provides to those who worship on it, contrasting with the discomfort and repetitiveness of the children’s labour. The tenderness of “deep and soft” reinforces the idea that the beauty they create will be appreciated by others, not by them.

The final stanza is the most poignant and reflective. Rumens writes, “The children are hard at work in the school of days”, a metaphor that replaces formal education with life’s harsher lessons. Instead of learning letters and numbers, the children learn through physical labour. The phrase suggests that childhood itself is being sacrificed as they take on responsibilities too heavy for their age. Rumens then creates a powerful image of creation and loss: “From their fingers the colours of all-that-will-be fly / and freeze into the frame of all-that-was.” This encapsulates the central theme of sacrifice. The children weave “all-that-will-be” — the future beauty, faith, and value embodied in the carpets — yet those colours “freeze” into the past. Their creative potential is captured in objects that leave them behind. The interplay of “will-be” and “was” shows how their labour becomes history while their own futures remain uncertain.

In conclusion, Rumens movingly conveys childhood and sacrifice through vivid imagery, symbolic contrasts, and the tension between beauty and labour. The children’s creativity and innocence are woven into carpets that travel far beyond them, while their own childhoods are surrendered to work and responsibility.

In ‘An Essay on Man’, Pope offers a striking exploration of human nature and the limitations of human reason. Pope merges reason to present mankind as a “riddle”, a being trapped between extremes, and one who constantly overestimates his own wisdom.

The poem opens with a command: “Know then thyself, presume not God to scan”. By addressing the reader directly, Pope sets a moral and philosophical tone. His use of the imperative “Know” reflects the Enlightenment belief in rational self-inquiry, while “presume not God to scan” warns against human arrogance. The contrast between the achievable (understanding oneself) and the impossible (understanding God) immediately establishes Pope’s central idea: humans must accept their limits. The line also encapsulates two aspects of human nature Pope criticises: curiosity and pride. Humans want knowledge, but they aim too high.

Pope then asserts that “The proper study of mankind is man”. The repetition of the word “man” emphasises the self-contained nature of human inquiry: this is what we should understand, yet we frequently fail to do so. Pope presents humans as creatures caught in a “middle state”, poised between two extremes — ignorance and omniscience, mortality and reason. His description of humans as “A being darkly wise, and rudely great” illustrates this paradox. The oxymorons “darkly wise” and “rudely great” suggest that although humans possess flashes of intelligence, this wisdom is clouded by ignorance or roughness. The tension within these descriptions conveys Pope’s belief that human nature is contradictory.

Throughout the poem, Pope uses balanced structures. The repetition in lines “With too much knowledge for the sceptic side, / With too much weakness for the stoic’s pride” highlights how humans are ill-fitted for all extremes. Pope’s use of antithesis creates a rhythm of instability that mirrors the instability of human reason itself. Humans waver “in doubt to act or rest”, reflecting not only indecision, but also Pope’s deeper point that reason cannot fully guide us because it is itself unreliable.

Pope develops this further through a series of tight parallel structures: “In doubt to deem himself a god or beast; / In doubt his mind or body to prefer”. The balancing of “god” and “beast” conveys the arrogance and self-disgust that define different sides of human nature. Both extremes show irrational thinking — either too much pride or too much self-loathing. The pattern of “In doubt” reinforces the idea that doubt is a defining condition of humanity. Despite possessing reason, humans remain uncertain about the most fundamental questions about themselves.

The line “Born but to die, and reasoning but to err” summarises Pope’s bleak view of human limitation. The repeated structure, with “but to”, conveys inevitability: humans cannot escape mortality, and their reasoning cannot escape error. The phrase “Alike in ignorance, his reason such, / Whether he thinks too little or too much” is particularly striking. Here, Pope criticises both intellectual laziness and overthinking, suggesting that both lead to confusion. Human reason, rather than guiding us, often becomes a trap.

Pope goes on to describe humanity as a “Chaos of thought and passion, all confused”. This metaphor presents the human mind as a turbulent mixture of rationality (“thought”) and emotion (“passion”), suggesting that neither can dominate the other. The alliteration of “Chaos” and “confused” emphasises disorder. Yet, Pope also implies that this internal disorder is universal: humans are “Still by himself abused or disabused”, unable to reach stable truths. The repetition of “half” in “Created half to rise and half to fall” captures humans’ divided nature: capable of seeking improvement, yet continually dragged down by their own flaws.

The final lines convey Pope’s vision of humanity as a being of contradictions. Humans are “Great lord of all things, yet a prey to all”; this juxtaposition shows how humans exert power over their environment yet remain vulnerable to suffering, temptation, and error. The triple description — “The glory, jest, and riddle of the world!” — summarises Pope’s argument. “Glory” acknowledges human creativity; “jest” highlights human foolishness; “riddle” suggests the enigma of human nature that reason cannot fully solve. The exclamation mark ending the poem emphasises the intensity of Pope’s conclusion: humanity is fundamentally paradoxical.

In conclusion, Pope vividly conveys his ideas about human nature and the limitations of reason through direct address, balanced antithesis, and striking imagery. Humans are presented as creatures caught between extremes, capable of thought yet prone to confusion, and continually overestimating their abilities.

In ‘A Married State’, Katherine Philips powerfully expresses her views on marriage and independence by presenting matrimony as a life filled with burdens, anxieties, and emotional restriction, in stark contrast to the freedom and happiness she associates with virginity.

From the very opening line, Philips leaves the reader in no doubt about her position: “A married state affords but little ease”. The statement is delivered as a universal truth, creating an authoritative tone that shapes the rest of the poem. The bluntness of “little ease” suggests not only discomfort, but the constant, ongoing nature of a wife’s struggles. Philips intensifies this criticism by adding that “The best of husbands are so hard to please”, implying that even an ideal husband brings dissatisfaction. The superlative “best” is ironic; it exposes how deeply embedded male authority and female obligation were in seventeenth-century expectations of marriage. Through this early tone of confident certainty, Philips frames marriage not as a romantic union, but as a trap that reduces women’s happiness and independence.

Philips reinforces this negativity through vivid emotional imagery. She invites the reader to “spell” the suffering of wives in their “careful faces”, suggesting that the strain of marriage becomes physically visible. The verb “dissemble” conveys that women must hide their unhappiness to maintain social expectations, presenting marriage as something that forces emotional suppression. This idea of performance contributes to Philips’s wider critique: not only is marriage exhausting, but it demands a constant pretence of contentment. Through this, she expresses her belief that independence is preferable to the constraints of a married life shaped by duty, expectation, and secrecy.

In contrast, Philips presents the “virgin state” as a life defined by “much content”. The phrase “always happy” creates a sense of lasting peace, while “innocent” carries both moral purity and freedom from worldly burdens. Philips uses a list-like structure to emphasise this contrast. The accumulation of negatives associated with marriage, “No blustering husbands”, “No pangs of childbirth”, “No children’s cries”, “Few worldly crosses”, highlights the sheer number of demands placed on a wife. The repetition of “No” works almost like a chant of liberation, stripping away each expectation one by one until independence appears calm, uncluttered, and enviably peaceful. This structural opposition between the two states is one of Philips’ most powerful devices in the poem.

Philips also focuses on the physical and emotional toll that marriage exacts. Childbirth “extorts” tears, a verb associated with force and violence, suggesting suffering imposed on women rather than chosen. Children’s cries “offend your ears”, implying constant disruption and the loss of personal peace. The phrase “worldly crosses” carries religious connotations of burden and sacrifice, reinforcing the idea that wives are expected to endure hardship as part of their role. In presenting these pains so starkly, Philips makes independence seem not only desirable but necessary for preserving a woman’s sense of self.

Towards the end of the poem, Philips adopts a more advisory tone as she addresses the “Madam” directly. The imperative “be advised by me” positions her as a wise counsellor, strengthening her argument through direct persuasion. The repeated “Turn, turn apostate to love’s levity” is striking: by choosing the religious word “apostate”, she suggests abandoning romantic love as though renouncing a misguided faith. Calling love “levity” reduces it to a trivial, unreliable force, far removed from the stability and dignity that Philips seems to value. This choice of language underlines her belief that emotional independence is far more beneficial to women than the uncertainty of marriage.

She ends the poem with the dismissive reassurance that unmarried women need not fear the old superstition of “leading apes in hell”, a phrase used historically to shame women who remained single. By rejecting this idea, Philips not only defends singlehood, but directly challenges patriarchal attempts to control women through fear and ridicule. The final line therefore becomes a bold declaration of independence: singleness is not a punishment but a source of freedom, unburdened by marriage’s emotional and physical strains.

Overall, Philips conveys her views on marriage and independence with a powerful, unwavering voice, using contrast, vivid imagery, and direct persuasion to present independence as a path of peace and freedom, and marriage as a life of burdens and constraint.

Overall, Mungoshi vividly conveys nature’s beauty and energy by presenting it through a child’s delighted senses. Through rich imagery, playful personification, and a tone of freshness and freedom, the poem celebrates the vibrancy of the natural world and the joy of being fully alive within it.
