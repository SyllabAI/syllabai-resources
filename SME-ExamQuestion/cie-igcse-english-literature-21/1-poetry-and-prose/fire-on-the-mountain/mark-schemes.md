### Q1
## Model answer

In Fire on the Mountain, Anita Desai presents memory and the past as powerful forces that continue to shape the characters’ lives, despite their attempts to escape or suppress them. Rather than offering comfort or nostalgia, memories in the novel are intrusive and emotionally destabilising, revealing that the past cannot be neatly abandoned.

For Nanda Kaul, memory is something she actively resists. She retreats to Carignano believing that physical isolation will free her from her former life as a wife and mother. However, Desai repeatedly shows that Nanda’s present is shaped by memories of emotional exhaustion and repression. When Nanda reflects that she has “discharged” all her duties, the language suggests a burden she has carried for too long. Her desire to live without obligation is directly linked to her remembered experience of a crowded house, constant noise, and unrelenting responsibility. Memory here motivates withdrawal, but it does not offer peace.

Desai uses extended flashbacks to expose the truth of Nanda’s past gradually. Memories of her time as the Vice Chancellor’s wife reveal a life of performance and emotional denial. She recalls presiding over a bustling household with apparent authority, yet these memories are filled with irritation and suppressed resentment. The repetition of sound — crying children, servants, guests — contrasts sharply with the silence she craves at Carignano. This contrast emphasises that Nanda’s identity has been shaped by endurance rather than fulfilment, and that her present detachment is a reaction to long-term emotional strain.

Crucially, memory also undermines Nanda’s belief that she has achieved independence. The past intrudes unexpectedly, often through sensory triggers such as sound or heat. The ringing telephone, Ila Das’s “hideous” voice, or even the cicadas in the heat of the afternoon provoke intense reactions, suggesting that Nanda’s emotional defences are fragile. By aligning the narrative closely with Nanda’s consciousness, Desai shows how memory operates involuntarily, resurfacing even when Nanda tries to remain detached.

Raka’s relationship with memory is very different but equally damaging. Although she rarely speaks about her past, her behaviour reveals the lasting impact of trauma. Raka’s withdrawal from people and fascination with desolate, burned, or dangerous spaces reflects her memories of violence at home. When she sees drunken adults at the club, she is immediately reminded of her father “stumbling and crashing” through the house before beating her mother. This moment reveals that Raka’s past is not distant but ever-present, shaping her fear of adult relationships and her preference for solitude.

Unlike Nanda, Raka does not attempt to intellectualise or deny the past. Instead, she responds instinctively, fleeing situations that echo her memories. Desai suggests that childhood trauma imprints itself deeply, influencing behaviour even when it is unspoken. Raka’s final act of setting the forest on fire can be read as an unconscious repetition of the violence she has witnessed, showing how unresolved memory can erupt destructively.

Ila Das is another character whose life is shaped by the past. Raised in privilege, she has internalised a sense of entitlement and pride that no longer matches her social reality. Her memories of childhood status prevent her from asking for help, even when she is poor and vulnerable. Ila’s cheerful recounting of the past contrasts tragically with her present hardship, highlighting how memory can trap individuals in outdated identities. Desai shows that Ila’s inability to adapt — to let go of who she once was — ultimately contributes to her isolation and death.

Throughout the novel, Desai presents memory not as a coherent narrative but as fragmented and sensory. Heat, sound, and landscape repeatedly trigger recollections, blurring the boundary between past and present. This technique reinforces the idea that memory is not something the characters control; instead, it shapes their emotional responses and choices.

In conclusion, Desai presents the past as an inescapable presence in Fire on the Mountain. Through Nanda, Raka, and Ila, she shows that memory continues to influence behaviour, relationships, and identity, even when characters attempt to deny or suppress it. The novel suggests that without confronting the past honestly, its impact becomes destructive rather than healing.

### Q2
## Model answer

Anita Desai makes Raka a striking and important character in Fire on the Mountain by presenting her as silent, watchful, and profoundly shaped by trauma. Although Raka is physically small and speaks very little, her presence has a powerful impact on both Nanda Kaul and the direction of the novel. Through Raka, Desai challenges assumptions about childhood innocence and exposes the darker consequences of isolation and violence.

Raka is striking from her first appearance because she does not behave like a conventional child. She arrives at Carignano frail and sickly after recovering from typhoid, described in terms that make her seem almost insect-like — Nanda compares her to a “mosquito” — or skeletal. This physical weakness contrasts with her remarkable independence and fearlessness. Rather than seeking comfort or attention, Raka immediately begins exploring the garden and the surrounding hills alone. Desai uses this contrast to make Raka memorable: she is vulnerable in body but resilient in spirit.

One of the most important ways Raka stands out is through her intense desire for solitude. Unlike Nanda, who claims to want isolation but is disturbed by interruption, Raka genuinely prefers to be alone. She ignores Nanda “so calmly, so totally” that it leaves Nanda unsettled. This calm indifference makes Raka striking because it reverses expected power dynamics between adult and child. Raka does not seek approval or affection, and her independence forces Nanda to confront the falseness of her own detachment. In this way, Raka becomes a mirror that exposes Nanda’s emotional neediness.

Raka’s importance also lies in how Desai uses her to explore trauma. Although Raka rarely speaks about her past, the narrative reveals that she has grown up witnessing her father’s violent abuse of her mother. This trauma shapes her instinctive mistrust of adults and her rejection of social spaces. When she sees drunken adults dancing at the club, she is immediately reminded of her father “stumbling and crashing” through the house. Desai shows that Raka’s withdrawal is not coldness, but self-protection, making her silence deeply meaningful.

Desai further makes Raka striking through her fascination with desolate and dangerous places. She is drawn to the ravine, the burnt cottage, and stories of forest fires. These spaces reflect her inner world — damaged, scarred, and unpredictable. Unlike Nanda, who seeks control and stillness, Raka is intrigued by chaos and destruction. This fascination culminates in the novel’s final moment, when Raka proudly announces that she has set the forest on fire. The act is shocking, but Desai presents it as a natural extension of Raka’s experiences rather than a sudden change.

The forest fire gives Raka enormous symbolic importance. Fire represents destruction, but also exposure and truth. As Nanda collapses under the weight of her own suppressed past and the news of Ila Das’s death, Raka’s fire rages uncontrollably. This parallel suggests that the violence and trauma Raka has witnessed are now erupting outwards into the world. Raka does not understand the consequences of her action, which reinforces Desai’s message about the long-term effects of untreated trauma on children.

Raka’s limited dialogue also contributes to her impact. Desai often presents her through observation rather than speech, making her actions more significant than her words. This narrative choice forces the reader to interpret Raka carefully, emphasising her psychological depth despite her young age. Her silence contrasts sharply with characters like Ila Das, whose constant speech masks vulnerability, further highlighting Raka’s uniqueness.

Overall, Desai makes Raka a striking and important character by using her to challenge romantic ideas of childhood, solitude, and innocence. Raka is not merely a secondary character; she reshapes Nanda’s understanding of herself and embodies the destructive potential of unresolved trauma. Through Raka, Desai suggests that silence and isolation may conceal deep emotional damage, making her one of the most powerful figures in the novel.

## Model answer

In this passage from Chapter 6, Desai presents Nanda Kaul as defensive, deeply uncomfortable with human contact, and struggling to maintain the illusion of control she has built around her solitary life. Although Nanda outwardly appears composed and self-sufficient, the extract reveals how fragile and reactive she is when her isolation is threatened.

From the outset, Nanda’s reaction to the telephone highlights her hostility towards intrusion. The ringing is described as “extraordinary” and “ominous”, suggesting that contact from the outside world feels threatening rather than neutral. The fact that she considers “punishing” the phone by letting it ring reveals her desire for dominance and control, even over inanimate objects. Desai presents Nanda as someone who equates silence with power, and communication with vulnerability.

Nanda’s physical response to the ringing phone exposes the strain beneath her rigid composure. She sits “bolt upright” and trembles, pressing her hands together as though bracing herself. This involuntary reaction contrasts sharply with her belief that she has withdrawn calmly and deliberately from emotional entanglements. Desai uses these physical details to suggest that Nanda’s retreat into solitude is not peaceful, but anxious and defensive, driven by fear of emotional exposure.

When Nanda finally answers the phone, her resentment is intensified by sensory discomfort. She resents the “uncomfortable pressure” of the receiver against her ear, a detail that emphasises how acutely she experiences physical and emotional intrusion. The language implies violation rather than inconvenience, presenting Nanda as hypersensitive and resistant to even minimal contact. Her irritation suggests that she has not grown accustomed to solitude, but rather become increasingly intolerant of interruption.

The arrival of Ila Das’s voice provokes an especially strong reaction, revealing Nanda’s lack of compassion and emotional detachment. Desai describes the voice as “shrill and strident”, so unbearable that it makes Nanda “gasp and shrivel”. The verb “shrivel” implies something drying up or retreating inward, reinforcing the idea that Nanda responds to others by contracting emotionally. Although Nanda recognises that this voice is “Ila Das’s tragedy in life”, her response is not sympathetic. Instead, she moves the receiver away “a few safe inches”, as though Ila herself is something dangerous or contaminating.

At this point in the novel, Desai presents Nanda as someone who views herself as a victim of other people’s demands. Her inability to follow what Ila is saying because of “the shock of that hideous voice” suggests an unwillingness to listen rather than genuine incapacity. The adjective “hideous” is harsh and judgemental, exposing Nanda’s cruelty and lack of patience. This moment reinforces the sense that Nanda’s isolation is not simply a preference, but a rejection of others based on irritation and emotional exhaustion.

The narrative perspective is closely aligned with Nanda’s consciousness, encouraging the reader to experience the call as she does. However, Desai also subtly invites us to question Nanda’s reliability. The intensity of her reaction appears disproportionate, and her certainty that Ila is unaware of her own “hideous” voice hints at Nanda’s sense of superiority. At this stage, she still sees herself as someone entitled to peace, unwilling to recognise her responsibility towards others.

Nanda’s response can be understood as the result of a life spent suppressing her own needs within marriage and motherhood. Having been expected to perform emotional labour for decades, she now reacts violently to even small demands. Desai presents her not as serene, but as brittle — her composure easily fractured by a single phone call.

Overall, at this point in the novel, Desai presents Nanda Kaul as controlled on the surface but deeply unsettled beneath. Her hostility towards contact, lack of empathy, and physical distress reveal that her solitude is a fragile defence rather than a source of genuine peace. This moment foreshadows the emotional collapse that will follow later in the novel.

### Q4
## Model answer

In Fire on the Mountain, Anita Desai presents relationships as deeply fragile, shaped by repression, trauma, and emotional neglect. Rather than offering comfort or stability, relationships in the novel are shown to be burdensome, hollow, or quietly damaging. Through Nanda Kaul’s past and present connections, Desai explores how traditional family structures often fail to provide genuine emotional support.

Nanda Kaul’s relationship with her family is fragile because it is rooted in duty rather than affection. Although she has spent her life as a wife, mother, and grandmother, she feels no sense of fulfilment from these roles. When she reflects that she has “discharged all [her] duties”, the language suggests obligation and exhaustion rather than love. Desai reveals that Nanda’s identity within relationships has been defined by expectation, particularly within a patriarchal society that demanded silence and obedience from women. As a result, once these duties end, Nanda retreats entirely from emotional connection, exposing how little genuine intimacy existed in her family relationships.

This fragility is most clearly revealed through Nanda’s marriage. Although she outwardly appeared to be a dignified Vice Chancellor’s wife, it later emerges that her husband was emotionally distant, unfaithful and dismissive. The relationship was sustained by social performance rather than mutual respect. Desai’s delayed revelation of this betrayal mirrors Nanda’s own repression of pain, emphasising how such relationships can appear solid while being emotionally empty. The damage of this marriage lingers, shaping Nanda’s inability to form close bonds later in life.

The relationship between Nanda and her great-granddaughter Raka further highlights the fragility of familial bonds. Despite their biological connection, there is little warmth between them. Nanda initially takes pride in Raka’s silence and independence, believing it mirrors her own desire for solitude. However, when Raka ignores her “so calmly, so totally”, Nanda is left unsettled. This moment exposes Nanda’s loneliness and her need for recognition, even as she denies it. Desai shows that Nanda’s emotional withdrawal has left her ill-equipped to form meaningful connections, even when she desires them.

Raka’s side of the relationship is shaped by trauma rather than choice. Her withdrawal stems from witnessing her father’s violence towards her mother, which has taught her to associate adult relationships with fear and chaos. When Raka sees drunken adults at the club, she is reminded of her father “stumbling and crashing”, prompting her to flee in terror. Desai suggests that Raka’s rejection of relationships is a survival instinct, highlighting how fragile trust becomes when childhood safety is destroyed. This makes any potential bond with Nanda extremely challenging.

Nanda’s relationship with Ila Das is another example of emotional fragility. Though they share a long history, Nanda finds Ila irritating and overwhelming, engaging with her only out of obligation. Ila, on the other hand, seeks companionship and support, yet never receives it. Desai uses Nanda’s metaphor of herself as a worm being torn apart during Ila’s phone call to show how even mild social contact feels threatening to her. The tragedy of Ila’s death underlines how dangerous isolation can be, particularly for vulnerable women, and exposes Nanda’s failure to offer compassion when it was needed most.

Throughout the novel, Desai uses silence, sparse dialogue, and isolated settings to reinforce emotional distance. Carignano, with its stark landscape and bare garden, symbolises the emotional barrenness of the characters’ lives. Rather than healing wounds, isolation exposes the weakness of relationships built on repression and fear.

In conclusion, Desai portrays relationships in Fire on the Mountain as fragile because they are shaped by duty, trauma, and emotional denial. Through Nanda, Raka, and Ila, she reveals how unspoken pain and social expectation undermine human connection, leaving characters isolated even when they are not alone.
