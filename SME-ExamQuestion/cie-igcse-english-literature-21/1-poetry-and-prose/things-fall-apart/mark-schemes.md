### Q1
## Model answer

Achebe makes this a shocking and powerful moment by letting us share Ikemefuna’s innocent hopes right up until the blow falls, and then showing Okonkwo kill him out of fear of looking weak.

The moment is powerful because Achebe puts us inside Ikemefuna’s mind and makes us like him. As he walks, he plays a childhood game, singing a song to himself: “If the song ended on his right foot, his mother was alive.” This small, innocent detail shows how young and trusting he still is and makes him seem vulnerable, and it is painful for the reader because we know something he does not, that he is being led to his death. The line “Ikemefuna felt like a child once more” is heartbreaking, because his happiest, most trusting moment comes just before he is killed. This gap between what he believes and what is really happening creates a strong sense of dramatic irony that makes the scene almost unbearable.

Achebe then builds fear through the change in the men around him. One of the men “cleared his throat” and “growled at him to go on”, and “the way he said it sent cold fear down Ikemefuna’s back.” The physical description of his fear, his “hands trembled” and his “legs melting under him”, shows us how terrified he is and makes us feel the danger with him. The most frightening detail is the question “Why had Okonkwo withdrawn to the rear?”, because we understand the terrible reason even though Ikemefuna is only beginning to sense it. Achebe makes us dread what is coming without stating it directly, so the innocent boy becomes a helpless victim in front of us.

The killing itself is shocking because of how quickly and plainly it is described. When the first man raises “his machete”, Achebe writes simply that “Okonkwo looked away. He heard the blow.” Not seeing it makes it worse, and the sound of “The pot fell and broke in the sand” suggests the violence in a horrible way. Then comes Ikemefuna’s cry, “My father, they have killed me!”, as he runs to Okonkwo for protection. The word “father” is devastating, because he is running to the very man who is about to kill him.

The most powerful part is the final sentence and the reason behind it. Achebe writes, “Dazed with fear, Okonkwo drew his machete and cut him down. He was afraid of being thought weak.” The short, flat sentences make the act feel sudden and cold. What is most disturbing is that Okonkwo does not kill out of anger or belief, but out of fear, fear of seeming weak in front of the other men. This one line in the narrative reveals the flaw that drives Okonkwo throughout the novel: his terror of weakness is stronger than his love for the boy who called him father.

Overall, Achebe makes this moment shocking and powerful by building our sympathy for Ikemefuna, using dramatic irony to fill us with dread, and then describing the killing in short, plain sentences. The final line, “He was afraid of being thought weak”, turns a personal tragedy into the key to Okonkwo’s whole character.

### Q2
## Model answer

In Things Fall Apart, Chinua Achebe vividly conveys the tension between individual desires and community values by presenting Umuofia as a society where personal ambition must constantly be reconciled with rigid ancestral laws. Through the protagonist Okonkwo, Achebe explores how an individual's "passionate" desire for status can lead to the subversion of communal harmony. By contrasting Okonkwo’s performative masculinity with the more nuanced spiritual demands of the clan, Achebe reveals that the centre of the community is often destabilised as much by internal individual failings as by external colonial forces.

Initially, Achebe establishes this tension through Okonkwo’s violation of the "Week of Peace". While the community values this sacred period as a time when "a man does not say a harsh word to his neighbor" to honour the "great goddess of the earth," Okonkwo’s individual fiery temper takes precedence. His desire to exert domestic control leads him to beat his youngest wife, an act that the priest Ezeani identifies as a "great evil" that could "ruin the whole clan". Achebe uses this incident to show that Okonkwo’s individual desire for dominance is fundamentally at odds with the communal need for spiritual equilibrium. The tension is vividly conveyed through the dialogue of the elders, who note that even in cases of extreme individual provocation, the community value of "peace" must remain inviolable.

The author further explores these tensions through the tragic sacrifice of Ikemefuna. The "Oracle of the Hills and the Caves" decrees the boy's death, representing a communal and spiritual mandate that Okonkwo individualistically chooses to carry out personally, despite the warning from Ezeudu: "That boy calls you father. Do not bear a hand in his death". Here, the tension lies between Okonkwo’s individual "fear of failure and of weakness" and the community’s expectation of balanced justice. By showing Okonkwo "dazed with fear" as he "cut him down," Achebe reveals that Okonkwo is not motivated by communal duty, but by a private, desperate need to prove his own "manliness". This act alienates him from his son Nwoye and creates a "snapping" of the familial bond that mirrors the eventual fracturing of the clan itself.

Finally, Achebe reveals the tension through the character of Nwoye, whose individual "desire" for a more compassionate spiritual path leads to a total rejection of Umuofian values. Nwoye is haunted by the communal practice of leaving twins to die in the "Evil Forest," a custom that fills his soul with a "vague and persistent question". When the missionaries arrive, it is the "poetry of the new religion" that captures Nwoye, offering him an individual "relief" from the "heavy hand" of his father’s and the community’s expectations. Achebe’s concluding metaphor for this tension is strikingly bleak: "Living fire begets cold, impotent ash". The community’s insistence on a hyper-masculine ideal—Okonkwo’s "fire"—ultimately produces an individual reaction that destroys the very continuity the community sought to preserve.

In conclusion, Achebe conveys these tensions by presenting individuals who are unable to find a middle ground between their internal drives and the external laws of their people. Whether it is Okonkwo’s "inflexible will" or Nwoye’s quiet "conspiracy" of dissent, Achebe suggests that a community which cannot accommodate the complex desires of its members is one that is destined to fall apart.

### Q3
## Model answer

In Things Fall Apart, Chinua Achebe reveals that the pressures and expectations placed on boys and young men are defined by an uncompromising ideal of masculinity that demands the total suppression of emotion and the pursuit of material success. Through the domestic tyranny of Okonkwo and the internal struggle of his son, Nwoye, Achebe portrays Umuofia as a society where "manliness" is a performative burden. The author suggests that these expectations, rooted in the fear of being seen as "weak" or "lazy," ultimately alienate the next generation and contribute to the fracturing of the clan.

Achebe initially establishes that the primary expectation for a young man is to distance himself from any resemblance to a failure. For Okonkwo, this pressure stems from his father, Unoka, who was "lazy and improvident" and "incapable of thinking about tomorrow". Consequently, Okonkwo "ruled his household with a heavy hand," and his children lived in "perpetual fear of his fiery temper". This pressure is most visible in his treatment of Nwoye. At only twelve years old, Nwoye causes his father "great anxiety for his incipient laziness," leading Okonkwo to "correct him by constant nagging and beating". Through this, Achebe reveals that childhood for an Umuofian boy is not a period of innocence, but a rigorous apprenticeship in "manliness," where any perceived softness is met with violence.

The author further explores these expectations through the symbolic division of labour and the celebration of physical prowess. Success for a young man is measured by his ability to provide, specifically through the cultivation of "yam, the king of crops". Boys are expected to master "difficult and masculine tasks," such as "splitting wood" or "pounding food". Achebe shows that Nwoye learns to "feign annoyance" when asked to help with "women's troubles," because he knows it pleases his father to see him "grumbling about women". This performance of misogyny is presented as a necessary survival mechanism. By showing Nwoye pretending that he "no longer cared for women's stories" in favour of Okonkwo’s "masculine stories of violence and bloodshed," Achebe highlights the psychological cost of these societal expectations.

Finally, Achebe reveals the tragic consequences when these pressures become unsustainable. The expectation that "to show affection was a sign of weakness" leads Okonkwo to participate in the killing of Ikemefuna, the boy who "called him father". This act of performative strength is the breaking point for Nwoye, who feels "something seemed to give way inside him, like the snapping of a tightened bow". Achebe’s use of the "Roaring Flame" metaphor to describe Okonkwo’s masculinity provides a definitive critique of these pressures: "Living fire begets cold, impotent ash". The expectation for Nwoye to be a "flaming fire" like his father results instead in his total conversion to a new faith, suggesting that the rigid expectations of Umuofian manhood are what truly cause the "center" to fail.

In conclusion, Achebe reveals that the expectations placed on young men in Umuofia are rooted in a destructive cycle of fear and performance. By presenting masculinity as a "heavy hand" that crushes individual sensitivity, he shows how the clan’s insistence on toughness and achievement creates a spiritual vacuum. Ultimately, the novel suggests that a society which demands the murder of affection in its sons is one that has already begun to fall apart from within.

Initially, Achebe builds drama by establishing a sense of anticipation through the soundscape of the village. Through the use of rhythmic percussion, the depiction of communal panic, and the horrific imagery of the smoking ancestral leader, he captures the absolute authority of the egwugwu. The passage reveals that in Umuofia, justice is a public, spiritual, and deeply frightening performance that binds the clan together through shared awe.

In Things Fall Apart, Chinua Achebe encourages a deep admiration for Ekwefi by presenting her as a woman of profound spirit and maternal devotion who refuses to be entirely broken by her environment. While she is subject to the same "heavy hand" that governs Okonkwo’s entire household, Ekwefi is distinguished by her agency and her capacity for enduring love. Through her history of trauma and her defiant protection of her daughter, Ezinma, Achebe presents her as a character whose internal strength often eclipses the performative masculinity of her husband.

Initially, Achebe encourages admiration through Ekwefi’s independent spirit and her history of following her heart. Unlike other wives who may have been assigned to their husbands, Ekwefi "ran away" from her first husband because she was captivated by Okonkwo when he "threw the Cat" in his youth. This decision illustrates a level of personal autonomy that is rare in her society. Furthermore, she possesses a courageous tongue that few others in Umuofia dare to use against Okonkwo. When he beats her for a perceived slight against a banana tree, she has the "audacity" to murmur a taunt about "guns that never shot," even after he has already used his fists. Achebe uses this incident to show that while Okonkwo can control her body through violence, he cannot suppress her defiant personality.

The most striking reason to admire Ekwefi is her fierce maternal instinct, which Achebe portrays as a form of heroic endurance. Having borne ten children and lost nine of them in infancy, Ekwefi’s life is defined by a "grim resignation" that eventually transforms into an intense, companionable bond with her only surviving child, Ezinma. Achebe describes their relationship as one of "equals," strengthened by "little conspiracies" such as eating eggs in the secrecy of the bedroom. This bond reaches a climax when Chielo, the priestess of Agbala, carries Ezinma away into the night. Despite her "terror" of the dark and the voice of the priestess which was "clear as metal," Ekwefi follows them through the forest, driven by a sudden strength that makes her "like a hen whose only chick has been carried away by a kite". Her vow to "defend her against all the gods in the world" and to "die with her" if necessary provides the most admirable image of courage in the novel.

However, Achebe does not sugar coat her character, noting that her suffering has made her "a very bitter woman". Her inability to rejoice with Nwoye’s mother over the birth of sons reveals the deep psychological toll of her "evil chi". This complexity makes her more human and, arguably, more admirable, as her love for Ezinma persists despite this internal "cloud on her brow".

In conclusion, Achebe encourages the reader to admire Ekwefi not because she is a saintly figure, but because she is a resilient survivor. By contrasting her genuine, selfless courage during the night of the priestess with Okonkwo’s later "manly" and "reasonable" arrival at the cave, Achebe suggests that true strength is found in those who protect what they love, regardless of the personal cost.
