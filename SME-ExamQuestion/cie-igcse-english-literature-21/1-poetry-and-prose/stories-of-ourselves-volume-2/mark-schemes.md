### Q1
## Model answer

In the opening of ‘Nick’, Rossetti introduces her main character as a man who has everything he could want but is still miserable because of his envy, and she does it in a light, fairy-tale way that makes his fault clear and even a little funny.

Rossetti begins by playing with the word “poor”. She calls Nick “a poor man”, but then spends a whole paragraph listing everything he owns: his “stone house, with its green verandah and flower-garden”, and a farm that gives him “milk, eggs, mutton, butter, poultry, and cheese in abundance”. This long list of good things makes us expect a happy man, so when she explains that he is only “poor” because he is “discontented and envious”, the word is turned on its head. I think this is a clever way to introduce him, because it tells us straight away that his real poverty is inside him, not in his circumstances.

Rossetti then shows exactly how his envy works. She writes that “it was in vain that his apples were the largest for miles around, if his neighbour’s vines were the most productive by a single bunch.” The small detail of “a single bunch” is important, because it shows that even the tiniest advantage in someone else ruins all his own success. The narrative voice here feels gently mocking, especially in the line that he sat “grumbling and bemoaning himself as if every other man’s riches were his poverty.” By comparing other people’s wealth to his own “poverty”, Rossetti makes his envy sound both childish and a bit ridiculous, which sets up the comic tone of the story.

The protagonist is brought to life through his own words. Leaning over “Giles Hodge’s gate, counting his cherries”, Nick mutters, “I wish I were sparrows to eat them up, or a blight to kill your fine trees altogether.” Letting us hear him speak makes his spite feel real and personal, and the wish to become a “blight” shows how far his envy goes, because he would rather destroy good things than let anyone else enjoy them. This wish, spoken in the first person, also matters because, as readers soon learn, his wishes are about to come true.

Finally, Rossetti introduces the fairy-tale danger that will punish him. The “little rosy woman, no bigger than a butterfly” appears with her “fist clenched”, and her curse that he “shall straightway become everything you wish” is the turning point of the whole story. Even here the narrative tone stays light: the slap that makes “his cheek tingle as if a bee had stung him”, and her leaving “with just so much sound as a dewdrop makes in falling”, are gentle, delicate images which keep the story feeling like a fable rather than something frightening.

Overall, Rossetti vividly introduces Nick as a comic but flawed character whose envy is his real problem. Through the twist on the word “poor”, the mocking narrative voice, his own spiteful wish and the arrival of the fairy, she makes us understand his fault clearly and prepares us for the punishment that is coming.

In Fluke, Romesh Gunesekera strikingly portrays deception and misunderstanding through irony, layered narration and the contrast between appearance and reality. By filtering events through the calm, observant voice of the driver-narrator, Gunesekera exposes how deception is normalised and even celebrated, while misunderstanding becomes a comfortable state people choose rather than challenge.

From the opening, deception is embedded in surfaces and performance. Mr Weerakoon’s appearance is meticulously described: his tight blue suit, matching briefcase and “smart silvery laptop” all signal modern success and expertise. However, the narrator subtly undercuts this image by noting the case is made of “pretend leather”, immediately suggesting artificiality beneath the polished exterior. This early detail establishes a key theme: Weerakoon’s authority rests on image rather than substance, and those around him accept this deception because it reassures them.

Misunderstanding is reinforced through language, particularly business jargon. Weerakoon’s presentation is full of “bullet points”, “bell curves” and “market segmentation”, which the narrator admits sounds like “hot air”. Despite this, the audience appears impressed. Gunesekera uses this gap between meaning and reception to show how misunderstanding thrives when people are unwilling to admit confusion. The narrator’s honest admission that he cannot fully follow the presentation contrasts with the delegates’ silent compliance, highlighting how deception relies on collective pretence.

The narrator himself becomes complicit in deception, though in a quieter, more troubling way. His claim to have a “knack with machines” borders on superstition, involving magnetism and “good chi”, yet it successfully fixes the projector. This moment is striking because it blurs the line between genuine skill and coincidence. Weerakoon’s enthusiastic response, “Bravo! How did you do that?”, shows how easily success is mythologised. Gunesekera suggests that misunderstanding often begins with chance, then hardens into belief once it proves useful.

National and political deception is explored through casual conversation rather than dramatic exposition. The discussion of the navy’s shift from war to whale-watching tourism reveals how violent histories are repackaged as commercial opportunity. Weerakoon’s language is disturbingly flippant, treating war, tourism and profit as interchangeable strategies. His references to Pearl Harbor and military “lessons” expose a mindset that disguises aggression as strategic thinking. The narrator’s discomfort, expressed through his silence and eventual withdrawal, shows an awareness that such deception depends on forgetting inconvenient truths.

The most striking portrayal of misunderstanding comes at the end, when Weerakoon panics over his missing phone. The device, supposedly essential and secure, is found easily in the van, its PIN still the “usual four zeros”. This detail is quietly damning. It reveals that the man who sells “Marketing: The Secret of Success” cannot even protect his own information. The misunderstanding here is profound: Weerakoon believes he is in control, yet everything about him suggests carelessness masked as confidence. The narrator’s reflection that “Marketing is a doddle. Dealing with a cock-up is the real problem” delivers the story’s central insight with understated force.

The final image of “sleep” and “amnesia” broadens deception into a human condition. Gunesekera suggests that people actively choose misunderstanding because it allows them to live comfortably with past mistakes, moral compromises and unresolved guilt. The narrator’s awareness of this temptation, and his recognition of how easily one can “float on our great comfortable sea of amnesia”, gives the ending its unsettling power.

Overall, Gunesekera portrays deception and misunderstanding as systemic rather than exceptional. Through irony, symbolic detail and a restrained narrative voice, *Fluke* exposes a world where appearances replace truth and misunderstanding becomes a form of survival. The story is striking because it does not condemn outright; instead, it quietly reveals how willingly people deceive themselves, and how easily others collude in that deception.

In Haywards Heath, Aminatta Forna movingly explores love and memory by presenting love as something enduring yet vulnerable to time, and memory as both sustaining and destructive. Through Attila’s reflective journey, Forna shows how memory preserves intimacy even as reality erodes it, creating a quiet but devastating emotional tension between what was and what can no longer be reclaimed.

From the outset, Forna frames the story through Attila’s consciousness, allowing memory to shape his experience of the present. His journey is meticulously planned, yet emotionally driven, suggesting that memory operates beneath rational control. Although he appears calm, his physical reactions betray suppressed emotion: his heart “beat wildly” and his scalp “tightened in alarm”. This physical response shows that Rosie remains deeply embedded in him, even after decades apart. Forna makes love feel involuntary and persistent, something that resurfaces despite time, distance and logic.

Memory is often triggered by place, and Forna uses Haywards Heath itself as a symbolic landscape of the past. The town name, once a private joke between Attila and Rosie, instantly pulls him back into shared intimacy. His recollection of her teasing overseas students about its pronunciation shows how memory preserves not grand moments but small, personal details. These remembered gestures are tender and precise, conveying a love rooted in familiarity and shared humour rather than sentimentality.

When Attila finally sees Rosie, Forna heightens the emotional impact by focusing on restraint and silence rather than overt declaration. Their conversation is fragmented and understated, and the line “They sat in silence and the silence felt comfortable already” suggests that their bond still exists without words. This moment is deeply moving because it implies that love survives even when language fails. Forna avoids melodrama, instead allowing emotional weight to build through pauses and what remains unsaid.

However, memory is also shown as painful and destabilising. Attila’s regret surfaces when he recalls leaving Rosie for his scholarship, asking himself “Which one of them had been naïve?” The rhetorical question exposes his lingering guilt and self-reproach. Love here is inseparable from loss; memory forces Attila to confront the consequences of his past choices. Forna presents memory as something that cannot be controlled or edited, only endured.

The most moving exploration of love and memory occurs when Rosie begins to forget Attila. Her gentle confusion, asking him to repeat his name and later mistaking him for someone else, is devastating precisely because it is delivered without cruelty or drama. When she says, “I’m afraid you’ll have to tell me your name again, dear,” Forna shows how memory’s erosion threatens love’s survival. Yet Rosie’s warmth remains unchanged, suggesting that love can persist even when its object is no longer fully remembered.

The final scene crystallises Forna’s exploration of love with painful clarity. Rosie dances with the young carer while Attila watches silently, recognising that her happiness now exists independently of him. The repetition of Rosie’s earlier phrase, “Another turn, Attila?”, now addressed to someone else, emphasises the passage of time and Attila’s displacement. Yet the young man’s response, “Whatever makes you happy, Rosie” mirrors Attila’s own selfless love. In this moment, love becomes an act of letting go. Attila’s decision to sit down and watch, rather than intervene, demonstrates a mature, compassionate love that prioritises Rosie’s present joy over his own longing.

Overall, Forna movingly explores love and memory by showing love as enduring but not possessive, and memory as fragile yet powerful. Through quiet scenes, restrained dialogue and a deeply reflective narrative voice, the story captures the ache of remembering someone who can no longer fully remember you. The emotional power lies in Forna’s refusal to resolve this tension neatly, leaving the reader with a profound sense of love’s persistence in the face of inevitable loss.

Gardam reinforces his isolation by contrasting Philip’s silent endurance with the adults’ conversation about him, which he cannot hear but which reduces him to an object of discussion. Gardam powerfully captures Philip’s inner life through free indirect discourse, allowing his thoughts to flow without clear separation from the narration. His observations of the seagulls, for example, initially appear innocent, yet quickly reveal his anxiety about foreignness and belonging. This thought is poignant and revealing: Philip’s fear of language barriers and exclusion is projected onto the birds, showing how deeply his apprehension about France troubles him. Gardam uses this moment to underline his childish logic and emotional confusion, making his fear both understandable and moving.

The Union Jack functions as the central symbol through which Gardam explores Philip’s emotional state. Initially, the flag represents safety, order and his mother’s controlling presence, as he repeatedly checks the “small brass tin” to reassure himself it is still there. Gardam slows the description, tracing the flag’s movement “up and down” until it becomes “a little bright speck”, mirroring Philip’s sense of losing his last connection to home. This loss intensifies his panic and sense of abandonment, particularly as he enters France without the symbol that was meant to guarantee his recognition and protection.

His growing resentment towards his mother is expressed through increasingly extreme and paranoid thoughts, culminating in the shocking belief that she “wanted him lost”. Gardam does not soften these thoughts; instead, she presents them directly, allowing the reader to experience the raw intensity of a child’s anger and grief when he feels unloved.

However, Gardam also shows Philip’s emotional resilience. Despite his fear, he physically defends his suitcases against the ruffians, acting with “a vehemence that astonished” everyone, including himself. This moment marks a subtle shift in his self-perception: he is no longer merely enduring but actively resisting. Gardam conveys this through dynamic verbs such as “flung” and “cried out”, which contrast with his earlier passive suffering and suggest a growing sense of agency.

The ending powerfully resolves Philip’s emotional journey. After convincing himself that his mother does not love him, the discovery of her note, “Oh Philip, my darling… I do so love you”, abruptly undercuts his anger and despair. The final image of the “spare Union Jack” pinned to the letter transforms the flag’s symbolism: it no longer represents rigid control or national identity but reassurance and care. Gardam’s restraint here is crucial; she avoids sentimentality, allowing the simple gesture to carry emotional weight. The delayed revelation makes the ending all the more powerful, as it exposes how easily Philip’s fear led him to misinterpret love as rejection.

Overall, Gardam conveys Philip’s experiences and emotions with striking power by immersing the reader in his inner world and using symbolism, imagery and narrative voice to reflect his shifting emotional state. The story is compelling because it does not diminish the intensity of a child’s feelings, treating Philip’s fear, anger and relief as profoundly real. This sensitive and controlled portrayal ensures the reader fully shares his journey from abandonment to understanding.

Initially, Rossetti presents Nick’s guilt as a form of spiritual lack rather than material need. This striking paradox sets the stage for his punishment. Nick’s guilt is not a feeling of remorse, but a moral failure that the fairy woman punishes by granting him his wishes. The animalistic physical transformations Nick undergoes serve as a striking presentation of how malice reduces human dignity.
