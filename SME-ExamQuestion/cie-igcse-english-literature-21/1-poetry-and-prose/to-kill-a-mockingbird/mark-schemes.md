### Q1
## Model answer

Lee makes this a tense and revealing moment by slowing everything down as the mad dog gets closer, and by showing us a completely new side of Atticus through the eyes of his amazed children.

The tension is built first through the way the dog is described moving towards the family. Tim Johnson is “advancing at a snail’s pace”, but he seems “dedicated to one course and motivated by an invisible force that was inching him toward us”. The slow, unstoppable movement is frightening because there is nothing anyone can do to hurry it or stop it. Lee adds small physical details, like the way the dog’s “jaw opened and shut”, which remind us he is sick and out of control. Because we see all this from Scout’s perspective, the fear feels immediate and childlike, and it makes the children seem very vulnerable.

Lee then draws the tension out through the adults’ hesitation. Heck Tate hands the rifle to Atticus, and Scout says she and Jem “nearly fainted”, which shows how shocking this is to them. The short, urgent dialogue adds to the pressure: “Don’t waste time, Heck,” and “For God’s sake, Mr Finch, look where he is!” The danger is made worse by the risk of missing and hitting the “Radley house”, so a lot depends on this one shot. When Atticus admits, “I haven’t shot a gun in thirty years”, we, like the children, are not sure he can do it, and this uncertainty is the centre of the tension.

The moment is revealing because it shows a version of Atticus his children have never seen. Lee slows time right down as he walks “out into the middle of the street”: the simile that he “moved like an underwater swimmer” suggests how strange and dreamlike the moment feels, and Scout says “time had slowed to a nauseating crawl.” The careful way he takes off his glasses, so that when they drop we “heard them crack”, keeps us holding our breath. Then everything speeds up in a single sentence: “With movements so swift they seemed simultaneous, Atticus’s hand yanked a ball-tipped lever as he brought the gun to his shoulder.” The flat line “The rifle cracked” and the dog crumpling “in a brown-and-white heap” shows how skilled he really is.

What makes it most revealing is Atticus’s calm afterwards. When Heck Tate says he was “a little to the right”, Atticus simply answers “Always was”, as if hitting a moving target is nothing to him. His modesty is clear when he “ground the broken lenses to powder under his heel” and says almost nothing. Because the story is told in the first person by Scout, we feel her shock directly: for children who thought their father was old and boring, this reveals a hidden courage and skill they never suspected, and it changes how they see him.

Overall, Lee makes this such a powerful moment by building slow, physical tension as the dog approaches and then revealing Atticus as “One-Shot Finch”, a brave and modest man. Seeing it through Scout’s eyes makes both the fear and the surprise feel real, and it teaches the children, and us, that real courage is often quiet.

In this passage from Chapter 6 of To Kill a Mockingbird, Harper Lee creates a tense and exciting moment by using vivid description, pacing, and the children’s fear of the unknown. The episode shows Scout, Jem and Dill sneaking around the Radley house at night, and Lee builds suspense by placing the reader directly inside their dangerous adventure.

Lee immediately establishes an eerie and threatening atmosphere through her description of the Radley house. The back of the house is described as “less inviting than the front” and the porch is “ramshackle”, suggesting decay and neglect. Details such as the “dark windows” and the “hat-rack mirror” that “caught the moon and shone eerily” create a sinister setting. The moonlight, instead of being comforting, makes the house feel unnatural and exposed, heightening the sense that the children are somewhere they should not be. This unsettling imagery prepares the reader for danger and builds tension before any action occurs.

The tension is increased through Lee’s use of sound and silence. Small noises, such as the squeaking step, become significant because the children are desperate not to be discovered. Jem testing the step “by degrees” slows the pace and mirrors the children’s fear. The whispered dialogue, such as “Sh-h” and “Hurry”, reinforces how quiet and cautious they must be. These moments stretch out the scene and make the reader anticipate something going wrong, which adds to the excitement.

Lee also builds suspense through the children’s fear of the unseen. The reference to “dodg[ing] the unseen from all directions” suggests that danger could come from anywhere. Dill spelling “G-o-d” in a whisper shows how frightened he is, while also reminding the reader that they are still children. Their fear feels genuine and contagious, drawing the reader into the moment and increasing tension.

The appearance of the shadow is the most dramatic and frightening part of the extract. Lee delays revealing what it is, describing it first as “the shadow of a man with a hat on”. Scout’s uncertainty — “At first I thought it was a tree” — heightens suspense, as the reader realises the danger before she fully does. The simile “crisp as toast” makes the shadow seem sharply defined, while the fact that it “moved across the porch toward Jem” creates a sense of imminent threat. The slow, deliberate movement of the shadow contrasts with the children’s panic, making the moment even more tense.

The children’s reactions further emphasise the danger. Jem “went rigid”, Dill covers his face, and Scout watches helplessly. Their fear is silent and frozen, which makes the scene more intense. When the shadow finally moves away, there is only brief relief before chaos breaks out. Jem’s sudden escape and the verbs “galloped”, “flung”, and “shooed” dramatically speed up the pace, turning suspense into frantic excitement.

The tension reaches its climax when the “roar of a shotgun shattered the neighbourhood”. This loud, violent sound contrasts sharply with the earlier silence. Jem’s panic, shown through his “sobs” and broken speech, reminds the reader again that he is still a child facing adult consequences. The discovery that Jem is stuck in the fence prolongs the danger and keeps the reader anxious until he finally escapes.

In conclusion, Lee makes this moment tense and exciting through eerie setting, careful pacing, vivid imagery, and sharp contrasts between silence and noise. By presenting the scene from Scout’s narrative perspective and focusing on the children’s fear of the unknown, Lee immerses the reader in their experience and transforms a childhood adventure into a genuinely frightening episode.

## Model answer

The law is central to To Kill a Mockingbird, functioning not only as a plot device but as a lens through which Harper Lee explores justice, morality and social prejudice in 1930s America. However, the novel also suggests that moral justice can exist outside of legal boundaries, showing that while the law is crucial, it is sometimes not always enough.

Atticus Finch, the moral centre of the novel, is a lawyer who believes in justice and equality before the law. He defends Tom Robinson, a black man falsely accused of raping a white woman, Mayella Ewell. Although Atticus does not usually take on criminal cases, he agrees after being asked by Sheriff Heck Tate, knowing that he will lose. This decision reflects his belief in the importance of giving Tom the best possible defence, regardless of race. He tells Scout: “The one thing that doesn’t abide by majority rule is a person’s conscience”. This  shows that, for Atticus, the laws of morality must sometimes guide legal duty.

Tom’s trial is the clearest demonstration of the limits of the law in a racially segregated society. Despite clear evidence that Tom could not have committed the crime as his left arm is useless, he is still found guilty. The all-white jury’s decision reflects the deep racial prejudice ingrained in Maycomb and in the legal system of the southern states of America.

Yet, there are small signs that the law is beginning to change. One juror in Tom’s trial hesitates, which indicates that Atticus’ argument made some impact. Jem, devastated by the outcome, asks, “How could they do it, how could they?” Jem’s emotional response shows a child’s growing understanding of the law and his disappointment in its failure to deliver justice. Harper Lee uses the trial to show how legal institutions can shape young people’s moral education.

Scout also gains awareness of the law throughout the novel. At first, she sees Atticus’ job as just part of normal life, especially when Walter Cunningham pays legal fees with produce rather than money. But by the end of the novel, she has developed a deeper understanding. When Sheriff Tate insists on protecting Boo Radley by claiming that Bob Ewell “fell on his knife”, Scout agrees, saying that exposing Boo would be “like shootin’ a mockingbird”. This moment symbolises Scout’s growth as she recognises that sometimes moral justice must outweigh legal procedure, especially when the law might harm the innocent.

Atticus himself struggles with this dilemma. When he thinks Jem may have killed Bob Ewell, he insists that the law must take its course. However, when it is revealed that Boo Radley saved the children, Atticus accepts Tate’s fabricated account, agreeing that subjecting Boo to the spotlight of the legal system would do more harm than good. This moment reflects a mature understanding that justice is not always best served by strict legal action. As Tate states, “Let the dead bury the dead”, implying that the community’s sense of morality can sometimes override legal truth.

The law is also shown to be intertwined with prejudice and power. The legal system fails Tom Robinson not because of a lack of evidence, but because of his race. Atticus’ defence is logical, ethical and legally sound but it cannot overcome Maycomb’s racist values. This highlights the limits of a law that is meant to be blind but is shaped by social bias. The novel critiques the way legal systems reflect the flaws of the societies they serve.

In conclusion, the law is central to To Kill a Mockingbird, not just in the literal sense of the courtroom scenes, but in how it shapes the characters’ understanding of fairness, truth and justice. However, Lee also shows that legal justice does not always align with moral justice. Through Atticus, Scout and Jem, the novel encourages readers to consider when following the law is right and when it might be more ethical to challenge or interpret it differently.

Dill Harris is a vital character in To Kill a Mockingbird, acting as both a source of humour and a contrast to Scout and Jem. Harper Lee uses Dill to explore the key ideas of childhood, family, imagination and moral sensitivity. Though a relatively minor character, Dill plays a major role in helping the reader understand both the innocence of childhood and the moral awareness that the trial of Tom Robinson provokes.

When Scout first meets Dill, she describes him as a “curiosity” because of his unusual appearance and style: “He wore blue linen shorts that buttoned to his shirt, his hair was snow white and stuck to his head like duckfluff”. From the start, Dill is different from the children of Maycomb, both in appearance and background. This difference reflects the context of 1930s Southern America, where communities were often closed and suspicious of outsiders. Maycomb is depicted as a microcosm of this world and Dill’s status as an occasional visitor from Meridian sets him apart, though he quickly becomes part of Scout and Jem’s inner circle. Scout says, “summer was Dill”, which captures how essential he becomes to their shared adventures and emotional growth.

Dill fuels the children’s imagination, particularly their fascination with Boo Radley. He invents “eccentric plans, strange longings, and quaint fancies” which Scout lovingly compares to a “pocket Merlin”. His enthusiasm contrasts with Jem’s increasing maturity and Scout’s practical nature. Dill’s love of make-believe and tendency to tell tall tales, like claiming his father is dead, adds humour, but it also suggests a deeper emotional need. His lies about his father’s identity, including once saying “I haven’t got one,” hint at his unstable home life and desire to be accepted and valued.

Dill’s emotional vulnerability becomes more apparent when he runs away from home and hides under Scout’s bed. He tells Scout that his mother and stepfather “do get on a lot better without me, I can’t help them any”. Dill’s confession highlights his loneliness and need for attention. His arrival at the Finch house is welcomed by Atticus, who treats him with kindness and responsibility, which contrasts sharply with the emotional neglect Dill experiences at home. In this way, Lee uses Dill to explore the differences in parenting styles and the importance of moral role models during childhood.

Dill’s most significant moment comes during Tom Robinson’s trial, which marks a shift from playfulness to moral seriousness. When Mr Gilmer cross-examines Tom with cruelty and disrespect, Dill begins to cry. He says, “It was the way he said it made me sick, plain sick”. Dill’s sensitivity highlights the impact of racism on a young, innocent mind. Unlike Scout, who is beginning to understand how Maycomb works, Dill is horrified by the normalisation of racial injustice. This response positions him as a character who still reacts emotionally to cruelty while others have become numb. Dill could also be considered a mockingbird figure because he has an unhappy home life. He loses his childish innocence when he sees the ugliness of adult prejudice.

Raymond Dolphus comforts Dill by suggesting that children “cry about the simple hell people give other people — without even thinking”. This reinforces the idea that Dill, though imaginative and naïve, sees the truth more clearly than many adults. Dill’s inability to accept this injustice signals both his decency and his loss of childhood innocence.

In his relationship with Scout, Dill also reveals his softer side. He declares that Scout is “the only girl he would ever love” and becomes her “permanent fiancé”. Their innocent affection adds warmth to the novel, but also reflects traditional gender expectations of the time. Dill and Jem sometimes exclude Scout from their adventures, which frustrates her and reminds readers of the gender roles that even young children internalise.

In conclusion, Harper Lee uses Dill Harris to explore the emotional effects of broken families, the transition from innocence to awareness and the moral complexity of childhood in a deeply prejudiced society. Through Dill’s eyes, the reader is reminded that justice and empathy often come more readily from children than adults.

This shows her need for order and her desire to establish authority within the Finch household. The use of prison imagery illustrates Scout’s resistance to Alexandra’s attempts to impose traditional femininity. Lee presents Alexandra as a product of her time: conventional, proper and deeply concerned with reputation.

Aunt Alexandra is obsessed with family heritage and social status. She believes that “fine folks” are those who have lived on the same land for generations, asserting that “the longer a family had been squatting on one patch of land the finer it was”. This narrow view reflects the rigid class system of the 1930s Deep South, where family history determined social worth. Scout observes that her aunt would “point out the shortcomings of other tribal groups to the greater glory of our own”, which highlights Alexandra’s snobbery and blind loyalty to her social circle.

Her views on class and race are also closely linked. When Scout wants to invite Walter Cunningham to their house, Aunt Alexandra is horrified, saying “he — is — trash” and warning that associating with such people will cause Scout and Jem to pick up their “habits”. This moment reveals Alexandra’s class prejudice and her belief that children should be protected from inferior influences. Likewise, she is disturbed by the children’s close relationship with Calpurnia. Alexandra insists that Calpurnia is no longer needed and is shocked to learn the children have attended her church.

Despite her prejudice, Aunt Alexandra is also presented as caring. She eventually allows Calpurnia to serve her missionary guests after recognising her grace and competence. This small concession signals a softening of her rigid views. Additionally, although she initially believes Atticus’ defence of Tom Robinson brings shame to the family, she shows genuine concern when Tom is killed. Her asking Atticus if the town’s criticism upsets him reveals her inner conflict and perhaps guilt. This emotional moment highlights how even those steeped in prejudice can experience doubt and change.

Aunt Alexandra’s character development is especially visible in her relationship with Scout. Initially, she tries to make Scout behave like a “young lady” criticising her dungarees and tomboy habits. This change suggests that Alexandra begins to value Scout’s individuality over outward appearances.

Scout describes her aunt as having “river-boat, boarding-school manners” and being “an incurable gossip”, yet she also acknowledges that Alexandra “let any moral come along and she would uphold it”. Lee uses these observations to present Alexandra as both comic and serious, as she is someone who clings to outdated customs but also possesses strength and care. She is described as the last of her kind, symbolising the dying values of the Old South that the novel critiques.

In conclusion, Aunt Alexandra is a character who represents the Southern Belle ideal, shaped by a culture of class consciousness, gender expectations and racial prejudice. Through her, Lee examines how these values can be both upheld and questioned within a single individual. Alexandra’s development over the course of the novel, from rigid enforcer of tradition to caring family member, shows that even deeply ingrained beliefs can be challenged by empathy and love.

In conclusion, To Kill a Mockingbird explores growing up as a process of gaining moral awareness, developing empathy and questioning societal norms. Through Scout and Jem, Lee shows how children grow not just physically, but emotionally and ethically, especially when faced with the harsh realities of injustice.

Mayella Ewell is a central character in To Kill a Mockingbird despite her limited appearance, as her actions catalyse the major conflict in the novel: Tom Robinson’s trial. Through her character, Harper Lee explores themes of prejudice, isolation, class, and injustice.

Mayella is important because she falsely accuses Tom Robinson of raping her, which sets the novel’s main courtroom drama into motion. This accusation drives the tension in the second half of the novel and provides Lee with a powerful lens through which to examine the racism embedded within the legal system and wider society. Without Mayella’s claim, there would be no trial to challenge Atticus Finch’s moral courage or to reveal the extent of Maycomb’s racial prejudices. The trial forces the children, especially Scout and Jem, to confront uncomfortable truths about their community, shifting their understanding of morality and justice.

Lee also uses Mayella to complicate the theme of prejudice by showing that she is not only a perpetrator but also a victim. As the eldest daughter of the violent and abusive Bob Ewell, Mayella is responsible for caring for her many younger siblings after the death of their mother. Her life is defined by poverty, isolation, and fear. Scout, despite her dislike of Mayella, recognises this, saying, “Mayella Ewell must have been the loneliest person in the world.” This insight shows how Lee encourages readers to view Mayella with a degree of sympathy, even though her actions are morally indefensible. She is a young woman trapped by circumstance, gender expectations, and social class, which adds complexity to her character and emphasises how prejudice operates across different levels.

Mayella’s role in the trial highlights the rigid racial and social hierarchies in 1930s Southern America. Although the Ewells are seen as "absolute trash" and are among the lowest in Maycomb’s social order, Mayella’s whiteness still places her above Tom Robinson, a black man. This power imbalance enables her accusation to be taken seriously despite clear evidence of Tom’s innocence. Lee uses Mayella to show how deeply racial prejudice is embedded in society — so much so that a white girl’s word automatically outweighs the truth told by a black man. In To Kill a Mockingbird, Harper Lee explores the theme of prejudice in many forms — racial, social, and gender-based — showing how deeply embedded it is within the community of Maycomb. Through the experiences of Scout, the trial of Tom Robinson, and the treatment of Boo Radley, Lee critiques the injustice and irrationality of prejudice in 1930s America.

The most obvious and devastating form of prejudice in the novel is racial discrimination. Tom Robinson, a black man, is falsely accused of raping Mayella Ewell, a white woman. Although Atticus presents strong evidence that Tom is innocent, the all-white jury convicts him purely because of the colour of his skin. Tom’s only “mistake” is saying that he felt sorry for Mayella, an expression of empathy that Maycomb’s white community views as offensive and presumptuous. This reaction exposes the rigid racial hierarchy of the town in the Jim Crow era, where black people are expected to remain not only separate (due to segregation) but also subservient. As Scout later reflects, “Atticus had used every tool available to free men to save Tom Robinson, but in the secret courts of men’s hearts, Atticus had no case.” Lee’s use of irony here underlines the tragic consequences of racial bias.

Lee further explores the consequences of racial prejudice through characters like Dolphus Raymond. He pretends to be an alcoholic to explain his choice to live with a black woman and mixed-race children. In reality, he drinks Coca-Cola from a paper bag and only pretends to be drunk to give people an excuse they can understand. Raymond tells Scout, “they could never, never understand that I live like I do because that’s the way I want to live.” His actions highlight the pressure to conform to societal norms and the lengths people go to in order to protect themselves from judgement.

Social prejudice is also portrayed through the class divisions in Maycomb. Aunt Alexandra is deeply class-conscious and believes that people should only associate with those of the same social standing. She disapproves of Scout’s friendship with Walter Cunningham, declaring he is “not our kind of folks.” Through Aunt Alexandra, Lee criticises the rigid social hierarchy of the Deep South, where even white families are judged by their wealth and family history. Her hypocritical nature is further exposed during her missionary circle meeting. The ladies express sympathy for the Mruna tribe in Africa while simultaneously making derogatory remarks about the black community in their own town. This moment starkly reveals the disconnect between their public charity and private prejudice.

Another victim of prejudice is Boo Radley, who becomes the subject of fear and gossip simply because he lives a reclusive life. Children in the town, including Scout and Jem, initially see Boo as a “malevolent phantom” and he is blamed for any strange event in Maycomb. However, by the end of the novel, Boo is revealed to be gentle and kind, quietly protecting the children by leaving them gifts and eventually saving them from Bob Ewell. Scout’s growing empathy is captured when she finally meets Boo and says that “he was real nice” and Atticus responds, “Most people are, Scout, when you finally see them.” This moment encapsulates Lee’s central message that prejudice often stems from ignorance and fear of the unknown.

Gender prejudice is another key theme, particularly through Scout’s experiences. As a tomboy, she resists traditional expectations of femininity. She is often told to behave more “ladylike”, especially by Aunt Alexandra, and is excluded from playing with Jem and Dill simply for being a girl. Even as a child, Scout recognises the limitations placed on her because of her gender. Lee uses Scout’s narrative voice to expose the absurdity of such social expectations and to suggest that gender roles are another form of harmful prejudice in Maycomb society.

In conclusion, Lee presents prejudice as a destructive and pervasive force within Maycomb. By exploring racial, social, and gender discrimination, she challenges readers to confront their own biases and consider the harm caused by ignorance and injustice. Through the eyes of Scout, the reader learns that prejudice is not only wrong, but deeply ingrained — and that change begins with understanding and empathy.
