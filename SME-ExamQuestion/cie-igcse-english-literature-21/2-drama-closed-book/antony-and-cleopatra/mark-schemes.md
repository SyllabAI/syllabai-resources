### Q1
## Model answer

In this extract, Shakespeare makes the moment dramatic and important because it shows Cleopatra refusing to be controlled by Roman expectations and insisting on taking power in a public, political space

Shakespeare immediately creates drama through conflict and rapid questioning. Cleopatra declares, “I will be even with thee”, which sounds like a threat and establishes her authority. The phrase suggests she feels insulted and wants revenge, showing how quickly her emotions flare. Enobarbus responds with repeated questions: “But why, why, why?” This repetition builds intensity and makes the exchange feel urgent. It also shows Enobarbus’ disbelief at Cleopatra’s thinking, which increases tension because the audience senses that her decision could have serious consequences.

The moment is also important because it reveals Cleopatra’s pride and her desire to defend her reputation. She accuses Enobarbus of attacking her “being in these wars”, suggesting she takes criticism personally. When she argues, “Why should not we / Be there in person?”, she frames her presence as a matter of right and responsibility. Shakespeare uses the plural “we” to emphasise her royal identity, as though she speaks not just as a woman but as the state itself. This makes her sound powerful and determined.

However, Enobarbus’ aside adds dramatic irony and highlights the risks of Cleopatra’s involvement. His metaphor about “horse and mares together” is insulting, suggesting that women weaken and distract men in battle. Shakespeare includes this aside to show that even Antony’s supporters hold sexist beliefs, and it reminds the audience that Cleopatra’s reputation is judged differently because she is a woman. The aside also creates tension because Cleopatra senses something is being hidden from her.

The extract becomes even more dramatic when Rome is introduced as a hostile force of gossip and judgement. Enobarbus explains that Antony is “Traduced for levity”, suggesting that his seriousness as a leader is questioned. He adds that people claim an “eunuch” and Cleopatra’s “maids / Manage this war”, which is humiliating because it suggests Antony is controlled by women and outsiders. Shakespeare makes this important because it exposes Roman fear of female power, and it shows reputation as a weapon that can destroy authority. The audience understands that even if Cleopatra’s intentions are loyal, her presence will be used as proof that Antony is weak.

Cleopatra’s response is fiery and memorable, increasing the drama through strong, violent language. Her curse, “Sink Rome, and their tongues rot”, is intense and emotional, showing her refusal to accept criticism. Shakespeare uses this outburst to highlight her passion and pride, but also her impulsiveness. This makes the moment dramatic because it shows Cleopatra’s strengths and flaws at the same time.

Finally, Shakespeare makes the moment important by showing Cleopatra claiming a traditionally male role. She insists that “as the president of my kingdom” she will “Appear there for a man”. This is significant because it reveals how Cleopatra understands leadership in gendered terms: to be taken seriously, she must be seen as “a man”. Shakespeare makes this a powerful declaration of authority, showing her courage and refusal to be sidelined. At the same time, the insistence “I will not stay behind” is ominous because it foreshadows how her decision to join the battle will contribute to Antony’s defeat at Actium, shaping the tragedy of the play.

In conclusion, Shakespeare makes this moment dramatic through tense dialogue, repetition and conflict, and makes it important because it exposes the pressures of honour, reputation and gender.

### Q2
## Model answer

In Antony and Cleopatra, Shakespeare vividly presents honour and reputation as powerful forces that shape characters’ decisions and identities. Honour is closely linked to public image, so reputation becomes something characters fight to protect, even when it destroys them.

Shakespeare presents Roman honour as public and political, based on how a man is judged by the state. Characters like Octavius Caesar treat honour as something measured through control and achievement. Antony’s reputation matters not just to himself but to Rome, because he is expected to represent Roman greatness. When he stays in Egypt and prioritises Cleopatra, this threatens the honour associated with leadership and military responsibility. Shakespeare makes this vivid through the way Roman characters constantly discuss Antony’s behaviour as shameful, suggesting that in Rome, honour is not private but something society monitors and punishes.

Antony’s honour is also tied to masculinity and warfare. Early in the play, he is remembered as a heroic soldier, and his name carries status. However, Shakespeare shows how quickly this reputation can be damaged. Roman voices interpret Antony’s love for Cleopatra as weakness, implying that emotional attachment is incompatible with Roman honour.  Caesar mocks Antony's current state in Egypt as effeminate, claiming he is "not more manlike / Than Cleopatra". This creates a sharp contrast between Antony’s heroic image and his “fallen” identity.

Cleopatra’s reputation is presented as equally important but far more complicated. Roman characters try to reduce her to a stereotype, describing her as a seductive threat, a "strumpet", who has “bewitched” Antony. This shows how reputation can be shaped by prejudice and political hostility rather than truth. However, Shakespeare gives Cleopatra depth through her own language and stage presence. She is witty, intelligent and commanding, which complicates the Roman judgement of her.

Shakespeare also explores how honour creates insecurity and rivalry. Antony becomes increasingly obsessed with how he appears, especially after his military failures. When his authority begins to weaken, his reactions become emotional and extreme, showing that honour is tied to identity. He cannot accept the idea of being laughed at or replaced. Shakespeare presents this vividly through Antony’s intense shifts in mood, suggesting that honour is not just social respect but self-worth. Once his reputation is shaken, Antony starts to unravel.

A key way Shakespeare presents honour and reputation is through the conflict between private desire and public expectation. Antony’s relationship with Cleopatra offers personal happiness, but it damages his Roman image. Shakespeare makes this tragic because Antony cannot satisfy both roles: if he chooses Rome, he sacrifices love and freedom; if he chooses Cleopatra, he sacrifices political reputation and military honour. This tension makes honour feel like a trap, because Antony is judged whichever path he takes. Shakespeare shows that reputation controls people even when it harms them.

Shakespeare also makes honour and reputation vivid through his language choices, especially the contrast between Roman political speech and the emotional, image-rich language linked to Egypt. Roman characters often speak in a controlled way, using formal and evaluative language to measure Antony’s “worth”, which suggests honour is something public and measurable. By contrast, Cleopatra’s language is often metaphorical and theatrical, such as when she describes Antony not as a soldier but as a cosmic entity: "His face was as the heav'ns, and therein stuck / A sun and moon". This turns reputation into something performed and celebrated, not just earned.

Cleopatra’s final act becomes Shakespeare’s most striking presentation of honour. She refuses to be humiliated by Caesar and chooses death rather than allowing herself to be turned into a public spectacle. This shows that honour can also mean dignity and self-control, not just public approval. Shakespeare presents Cleopatra’s death as powerful because she claims ownership over her reputation.

In conclusion, Shakespeare vividly presents honour and reputation as central forces in Antony and Cleopatra. Honour is shown as public, unstable and closely linked to power, masculinity and national identity.

## Model answer

Shakespeare makes this ending powerful and memorable by presenting Cleopatra’s death as both deeply emotional and dramatically controlled. Through intense imagery, repeated references to Antony, and the theatrical staging of her suicide, Shakespeare turns the final moments into something tragic but also strangely beautiful.

One way Shakespeare makes the ending powerful is through Cleopatra’s unusual attitude towards death. Instead of fearing it, she treats it as something gentle and even desirable. She compares “the stroke of death” to “a lover’s pinch”, which is shocking because it mixes pain and pleasure. This metaphor suggests Cleopatra sees death as a final reunion with Antony, not a defeat. The fact that it “hurts and is desired” reflects the intensity of her emotions and makes the ending feel romantic as well as tragic. Shakespeare uses this language to show Cleopatra dying on her own terms, which gives her death dignity.

Cleopatra’s grief for Antony also makes the scene memorable. She repeatedly speaks to him, crying “O Antony!”, which keeps him emotionally present even though he is already dead. This repetition makes it clear that Cleopatra cannot imagine life without him. Her language suggests she is still acting like a lover rather than a ruler, showing that her personal feelings are stronger than political concerns. When she says that if Charmian dies first then Antony might “spend that kiss / Which is my heaven to have”, Shakespeare shows Cleopatra’s jealousy is still alive even at the point of death. This makes her seem human but also complex, because she is thinking about love and rivalry at the same time as dying.

Shakespeare also makes the ending powerful through imagery of nature and the gods, which makes Cleopatra’s death feel significant. Charmian’s line “The gods themselves do weep” personifies the heavens, suggesting Cleopatra’s death is so important that even nature reacts. This raises the moment to a tragic, almost mythic level. Cleopatra’s own language is rich in softness and calmness: she describes the death as “As sweet as balm, as soft as air, as gentle.” The triple structure makes her words sound soothing and peaceful, creating a slow, quiet atmosphere that contrasts with the violence and conflict earlier in the play.

Shakespeare also makes this ending memorable because Cleopatra’s death becomes an act of defiance against Caesar. She states, “great Caesar ass / Unpolicied!”, using insulting language that shows she still rejects Roman control. Even at the end, she refuses to submit or allow Caesar to display her as a trophy. This gives the scene political power as well as emotional weight: Cleopatra’s death is her final way of keeping her dignity.

Cleopatra’s final images are also striking because they blur reality and dreamlike fantasy. She asks Charmian, “Dost thou not see my baby at my breast, / That sucks the nurse asleep?” This tender image makes her seem calm and almost childlike, as if she is drifting into a peaceful vision before death. It contrasts strongly with the earlier tension of war and betrayal, showing how far Cleopatra has moved from political struggle into personal escape.

Finally, the ending is powerful because it happens quickly and decisively. Cleopatra says, “What should I stay —” and then dies mid-thought. The dash creates a sudden stop, emphasising how final death is. It also suggests that Cleopatra has no reason to remain in a world without Antony, so her death feels inevitable. The audience is left with a sharp sense of loss, but also admiration, because Cleopatra dies with courage, passion and control.

In conclusion, Shakespeare makes this a powerful and memorable ending through poetic language, emotional intensity, symbolic staging and Cleopatra’s defiant control. Her death feels both tragic and beautiful, combining love and loss, softness and strength. By ending the play with Cleopatra choosing dignity over humiliation, Shakespeare ensures her final moments stay in the audience’s mind.

### Q4
## Model answer

Cleopatra is one of the most fascinating and complex characters in Antony and Cleopatra because Shakespeare makes her powerful, dramatic and unpredictable.

Shakespeare makes Cleopatra fascinating through the way she speaks. Her language often feels theatrical and exaggerated, which makes her seem larger than life. In the opening scene, she challenges Antony's declaration of love with the high-stakes demand: "If it be love indeed, tell me how much." She uses strong commands and bold emotional statements, making her sound like someone who expects to be listened to. Even when she is flirting or teasing, she still feels like a queen who controls the room. Shakespeare often gives her lively dialogue, which adds energy and excitement to her scenes. This makes the audience interested in her because she is never dull or quiet.

However, Shakespeare also makes Cleopatra complex by showing how quickly her mood changes. In some scenes she moves from affection to anger within a few lines. This creates tension because Antony, and the audience, never know what version of Cleopatra they will get next. Shakespeare’s use of sharp shifts in tone makes her feel human and emotional, rather than calm and predictable. Her jealousy and dramatic reactions suggest she is afraid of being abandoned or replaced, which adds vulnerability under her confident surface. This makes her complex because she is not just powerful, she is also insecure.

Structure is also important in how Shakespeare presents Cleopatra. He often places her Egyptian scenes next to scenes in Rome, creating a strong contrast. Egypt feels passionate, colourful and emotional, while Rome feels cold, controlled and focused on politics. Cleopatra stands out even more because her world seems full of life and feeling. This makes her fascinating because she represents freedom and excitement. At the same time, it makes her complex because the audience is encouraged to question whether Rome’s “discipline” is actually better, or just more ruthless.

Shakespeare also uses Cleopatra’s relationship with Antony to show her complexity. Cleopatra often tests Antony’s loyalty, and her language can sound manipulative, as if she wants to control him. This could make her seem selfish. However, Shakespeare also shows that she genuinely loves Antony and fears losing him. When Antony dies, her manipulation vanishes, replaced by grief: "Noblest of men, woot die?" Her emotional intensity suggests she does not just want power, she wants security and devotion. This makes her complex because her behaviour can be interpreted in different ways: she is both a passionate lover and a clever woman who understands influence.

Another way Shakespeare makes Cleopatra complex is through how other characters speak about her. Roman characters often describe her as dangerous and corrupting, almost like she is a threat to Rome itself., such as Philo describing Antony as a "strumpet's fool". This creates a biased image of Cleopatra before the audience fully understands her. Shakespeare uses this to show how Cleopatra is judged unfairly. Caesar claims that Antony has given his empire up to a "whore". Structurally, this makes the audience see two Cleopatras: the version Rome wants to create and the real Cleopatra who is witty, emotional and vulnerable.

Cleopatra becomes most powerful and memorable at the end of the play. As tragedy builds, Shakespeare shows her choosing dignity rather than humiliation. She refuses to be paraded as a trophy by Caesar, which proves she still has control over her identity. Her language becomes more serious and determined, such as "My resolution's placed and I have nothing / Of woman in me", showing she understands how she will be remembered. This final structural shift makes her seem more noble, because she takes charge of her ending instead of letting Rome control it. Shakespeare presents her death as tragic, but also as an act of pride and strength.

Overall, Shakespeare makes Cleopatra fascinating because her language is bold and dramatic, her emotions are intense and her presence dominates the play. He makes her complex because she is full of contradictions: powerful yet insecure, loving yet controlling, playful yet tragic.
