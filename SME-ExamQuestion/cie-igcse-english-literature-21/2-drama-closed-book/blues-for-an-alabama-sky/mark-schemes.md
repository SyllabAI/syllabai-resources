### Q1
## Model answer

In this moment from Act 2, Scene 1 of Blues for an Alabama Sky, Cleage builds tension and shock by allowing a light-hearted conversation to unravel into a fierce confrontation that exposes irreconcilable values. Through confrontational dialogue, interruptions and sudden shifts in power, the scene reveals deep divisions around sexuality, religion and belonging, making the clash both unsettling and inevitable.

At the start of the extract, Cleage deliberately creates a relaxed and playful atmosphere. Guy’s storytelling is extravagant and humorous, filled with glamour, exaggeration and teasing references to Langston’s party. His language celebrates diversity and freedom, presenting romance as beauty, desire and openness. His repeated questions — “The men were looking at another man?” and “Men flirting with men?” — slow the rhythm of the scene and expose his disbelief. Cleage uses repetition here to show Leland’s inability, or refusal, to accept what he is hearing. Each interruption tightens the atmosphere, turning curiosity into judgement. The audience becomes aware that this difference in values cannot, and will not, remain politely unspoken.

The moment of greatest shock comes when Leland labels homosexuality an “abomination.” The word is loaded with religious authority and moral condemnation, and Cleage places it abruptly within otherwise conversational dialogue. This creates a jarring effect, exposing the violence of Leland’s beliefs. By invoking God, Leland attempts to assert moral dominance, positioning himself as righteous and unquestionable. The audience recognises that this language dehumanises Guy and threatens the safety of the space.

Cleage intensifies the confrontation through Guy’s reaction. His response is immediate and uncompromising: “Get out.” The sudden shift from witty narrator to defender marks a dramatic turning point. For the first time, Guy asserts physical and moral authority, refusing to tolerate Leland’s intolerance. The brevity of the imperative contrasts with Leland’s long, moralising speech, making it feel decisive and powerful. This moment shocks because it breaks the group’s usual pattern of tolerance and humour, revealing how high the stakes have become.

Angel’s position in the scene further exposes the clash of values. Her attempts to minimise the incident — “It was just a party” — show her desire to avoid conflict at all costs. Cleage uses Angel’s pleading tone and divided loyalties to heighten tension, as the audience can see she is torn between the safety of her chosen family and the promise of stability Leland offers. Her request that Leland wait downstairs symbolises this emotional split, placing her literally and morally between two worlds.

The scene ends without resolution, which sustains tension beyond the moment itself. Leland’s stiff politeness as he exits contrasts with the emotional damage he has caused, leaving the audience unsettled. Cleage uses this unresolved ending to foreshadow later violence, suggesting that such rigid beliefs will not remain contained within words alone.

Overall, Cleage builds tension and shock by allowing incompatible values to collide in an intimate space. Through interruption, charged language and sudden reversals of power, the scene exposes the deep fault lines between progressive Harlem culture and conservative morality, making this moment one of the play’s most revealing and disturbing confrontations.

However, Cleage does not present him as cruel or dismissive. His concern is quiet and restrained, shown through his silences and measured responses. This contrast reveals a subtle imbalance in their friendship: Guy often acts as Angel’s protector, but he cannot force her to accept the truth.

The exchange about Paris further exposes Angel’s insecurity. Her bitterness suggests that she believes dreams are a luxury reserved for men like Guy, while she must rely on charm, men and opportunity to survive. Cleage uses this line to expose the social pressures that limit Angel’s sense of agency.

At the same time, the argument about clothing appears trivial but is highly revealing. Angel demands that Guy make her a dress, and when he resists, she accuses him of prioritising “those Hole in the Wall floozies.” This moment exposes Angel’s dependency and entitlement, but also her fear of being invisible. Her insistence on appearance reflects how survival for her is tied to being seen, desired and noticed. Guy’s eventual compromise — altering the suit and making a hat — reinforces their unhealthy dynamic, where he repeatedly rescues her rather than allowing her to face reality alone.

Finally, the scene’s ending with alcohol is revealing in its normality. The casual decision to “have a drink” reinforces escapism as a coping mechanism. The stage direction showing Guy noticing the bottle’s level hints that Angel has already been drinking alone, suggesting a quiet decline beneath her bravado. Cleage presents this not as melodrama, but as routine, making the moment feel disturbingly authentic.

Overall, Cleage makes this moment revealing by stripping back Angel’s glamour to expose fear, denial and dependency. Through naturalistic dialogue, pauses and contrasts with Guy, the scene reveals the emotional and social forces that push Angel towards destructive choices, foreshadowing the tragedy that follows.

### Q3
## Model answer

In Blues for an Alabama Sky, Pearl Cleage presents friendship and chosen family as a vital source of survival, comfort and identity in a hostile and unstable world. Through the relationships between Guy, Angel, Delia and Sam, Cleage shows how bonds formed by choice rather than blood offer emotional refuge, while also revealing how fragile these connections become under economic and social pressure.

From the opening of the play, Cleage establishes the central group as a chosen family through shared space and routine. Guy’s apartment is not simply a living arrangement but a communal home, where Angel sleeps on the sofa, Delia comes and goes freely, and Sam is treated as part of the household. This constant movement between apartments reinforces a sense of collective life. Cleage’s stagecraft emphasises this intimacy, as the audience can often see both living spaces at once, visually linking the characters and suggesting that their lives are intertwined.

Guy’s relationship with Angel most clearly illustrates the idea of a chosen family. He repeatedly acts as her protector, providing housing, money and emotional reassurance after she loses her job and her partner. His affectionate language and playful teasing, such as calling her “Sweetie” and “Big Daddy’s gonna keep everything fine and mellow,” create a sense of parental care rather than romantic attachment. Indeed, Guy insists he will look after Angel “just like always”, the line repeated to underline his commitment to her. Cleage presents this bond as genuine and loving, but also subtly unbalanced. Angel’s dependence on Guy highlights how chosen family can blur into emotional reliance, foreshadowing later tensions.

Delia and Sam represent a different model of friendship within the group, one grounded in shared values and quiet support. Their relationship grows through mutual respect rather than flamboyance. Sam supports Delia’s ambition to open a family-planning clinic, attending meetings with her and helping her prepare for opposition. Cleage uses their calm, thoughtful dialogue to show a partnership based on trust and intellectual connection. This contrasts with Angel’s more chaotic relationships, suggesting that chosen family can take many forms, not all of them dramatic or loud.

Cleage also uses humour and casual conversation to reinforce the warmth of these friendships. Teasing exchanges, shared meals and drinks, and late-night conversations give the audience a sense of authenticity. These moments are not idealised; instead, they feel natural and lived-in. This realism strengthens the emotional impact of the play, as the audience comes to see the group as a real family unit rather than a symbolic one.

However, Cleage does not present chosen family as invincible. The arrival of Leland exposes the vulnerability of this community. His conservative beliefs and insistence on traditional authority clash sharply with the group’s progressive values. This contrast comes to the fore when the group are discussing a party where men are openly flirting with other men. Before storming out, Leland exclaims: “Don’t put God’s name in the stuff you’re talking about!” As Angel begins to prioritise Leland’s promise of security over her friendships, the chosen family starts to fracture. Cleage reveals how economic desperation can force individuals to abandon supportive relationships in favour of stability, even when that stability is illusory.

The ultimate breakdown of the chosen family comes with Sam’s death. His murder is not only a personal tragedy but a symbolic collapse of the group’s moral and emotional centre. In the aftermath, Delia’s decision to leave Harlem underscores the impossibility of maintaining the chosen family without safety or hope. Guy’s departure to Paris further disperses the group, while Angel is left isolated and broken. Cleage uses this fragmentation to show that while chosen families can provide strength, they remain vulnerable to violence, poverty and intolerance.

Overall, Cleage presents friendship and chosen family as both essential and fragile. Through everyday interactions, shared ideals and mutual care, the central group creates a family that sustains them when traditional structures fail. Yet the play ultimately reveals how external pressures can tear even the strongest chosen bonds apart, deepening the tragedy at the heart of Blues for an Alabama Sky.

### Q4
## Model answer

In Blues for an Alabama Sky, Pearl Cleage’s portrayal of Sam contributes powerfully to the dramatic impact of the play by presenting him as a calm, principled figure whose moral clarity highlights both the hope and the tragedy at the heart of Harlem in 1930. Through Sam, Cleage embodies the progressive ideals of the community, making his eventual death deeply shocking and emotionally devastating for the audience.

From his first appearances, Sam is shown as dependable and compassionate. As a doctor at Harlem Hospital, he represents rationality and care in a world increasingly shaped by hardship and fear. His dialogue is often measured and thoughtful, especially in contrast to Angel’s emotional volatility and Guy’s flamboyance. This steadiness gives the audience a sense of reassurance, making Sam seem like a figure of safety within the play’s close-knit community. Dramatically, this sense of stability makes later events far more painful, as the audience comes to trust Sam just as the characters do.

Sam’s relationship with Delia is particularly important in establishing his dramatic role. He supports her ambition to open a family-planning clinic, asking challenging questions not to undermine her, but to help her prepare for opposition. Cleage uses this dialogue to show Sam as intellectually engaged and quietly progressive. His belief in women’s right to make choices about their own bodies aligns him with Delia’s ideals and reinforces the play’s exploration of women’s autonomy. This shared purpose creates warmth and tenderness between them, encouraging the audience to invest emotionally in their future together.

Cleage also uses Sam to highlight ideological conflict within the play, especially through his contrast with Leland. While Leland’s worldview is shaped by rigid religion and patriarchal authority, Sam’s beliefs are grounded in science, ethics and compassion. Importantly, Sam never treats Leland with hostility. His calm politeness and willingness to engage respectfully increase the dramatic tension, as the audience can see the danger that Sam himself does not fully recognise. This dramatic irony heightens suspense, as Sam’s trust and openness make him vulnerable.

Sam’s decision to perform Angel’s abortion is a crucial turning point. Initially hesitant, he ultimately respects Angel’s right to choose, stating that people must “kill their own snakes”. Cleage’s language here presents Sam as morally reflective rather than reckless. He does not act impulsively, but thoughtfully, which reinforces the injustice of his fate. Dramatically, this moment positions Sam as a moral centre in the play, acting out of principle rather than self-interest.

The dramatic impact of Sam’s portrayal reaches its peak in his death. Because Cleage has consistently shown him as gentle, responsible and non-violent, his murder is both shocking and deeply unsettling. The audience understands that Sam poses no threat, making Leland’s act seem especially brutal and senseless. Sam’s death symbolises the destruction of Harlem’s progressive potential, as a figure committed to care, reason and community uplift is violently silenced.

In the aftermath, Sam’s absence is keenly felt. Delia’s decision to leave Harlem underscores the devastating consequences of his loss, both personally and socially. Cleage uses this outcome to show how fragile progress is in a world marked by economic hardship and rising conservatism. Sam’s death is not only a personal tragedy but a wider commentary on how hope and reform are crushed during this period.

Overall, Cleage’s portrayal of Sam contributes to the dramatic impact of the play by making him a figure of moral stability whose destruction exposes the play’s central tensions. Through his compassion, restraint and ultimate vulnerability, Sam embodies what Harlem could have been, making his loss one of the most powerful and tragic elements of Blues for an Alabama Sky.
