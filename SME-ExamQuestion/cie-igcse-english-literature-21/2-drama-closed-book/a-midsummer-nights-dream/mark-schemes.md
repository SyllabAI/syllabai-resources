### Q1
## Model answer

In A Midsummer Night's Dream, Shakespeare presents love as a powerful but irrational force. It is blind, changeable and often ridiculous, and only the play's comic ending finally settles it into harmony.

From the opening, Shakespeare shows that love is rarely smooth or free. Lysander's famous line, "The course of true love never did run smooth", acts almost as a motto for the play, and Shakespeare immediately proves it true by setting love against authority. Hermia is ordered by her father and by Athenian law to marry Demetrius or die, so that genuine love is treated as disobedience. Even Theseus's love is rooted in violence, since he reminds Hippolyta that he "wooed thee with my sword". By opening with these obstacles, Shakespeare makes the audience see love as something fragile that must struggle against power, law and pride before it can flourish.

Shakespeare also presents love as blind and irrational. He even turns this idea into real stage action. Helena observes that "Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind", and that this is why "winged Cupid" is "painted blind". The love-juice that Oberon uses literalises this blindness: a single drop makes a sleeper adore whoever they first see, so that the young men switch their affections in moments. Through this device Shakespeare suggests that desire is arbitrary and not based on reason, since the lovers cannot explain why they love, only that they suddenly do. The confusion of their night-time quarrels lets the audience laugh through dramatic irony, because we can see the magic that the lovers cannot, but it also makes a serious point about how little control people have over who they want.

Love becomes most absurd of all in the figure of Titania. Under the spell, the dignified fairy queen dotes on Bottom even though he wears an ass's head, and when she wakes she is horrified: "Methought I was enamoured of an ass". Shakespeare uses this grotesque pairing as a comic symbol of how love can blind even the powerful to what is really in front of them. The same idea runs through Bottom's cheerful remark that "reason and love keep little company together", and through Helena's painful declaration that she will be Demetrius's "spaniel" if only he will keep her near. Across these moments Shakespeare presents love as something that humbles and even humiliates people, turning queens and lovers alike into fools.

Shakespeare also contrasts this chaotic young love with a calmer, more mature kind. While the four Athenians chase each other through the wood in breathless rhyming couplets, Theseus and Hippolyta look forward steadily to their wedding, and their relationship, though it began in conquest, has settled into mutual respect. This contrast invites the audience to see the lovers' frenzy as a phase to be grown out of, a wildness that marriage will calm.

Yet Shakespeare does not leave love in chaos. The play's structure moves from disorder back to harmony, as Oberon lifts the spell, the right couples are finally paired, and the lovers wake as if from a dream to find their feelings settled. The three weddings, blessed by the fairies at the close, present love as something that can be reconciled with social order rather than destroying it. Even the clumsy "Pyramus and Thisbe" play, performed for the wedding, defuses the tragic potential of love into laughter, reminding the audience how easily passion could have ended in death. By resolving the confusion so neatly, Shakespeare suggests that love's madness, although real, can be survived and finally celebrated.

In conclusion, Shakespeare presents love as blind, changeable and absurd, but also as joyful and ordered in the end. Through the magic of the flower, the comedy of Titania and Bottom, and the contrast between wild and settled couples, he makes the audience feel both the danger and the delight of love, before guiding it safely into the harmony of marriage.

The moment becomes even more comical when the argument shifts to a focus on height. Hermia becomes obsessed with the idea that Helena has used her "tall personage" to win over Lysander. The audience finds entertainment in seeing these "beauteous" ladies abandon their "maiden shame" to engage in a "fray".

In conclusion, Shakespeare makes this moment entertaining by using the crazed energy of the lovers to strip away their dignity. ### Q3
## Model answer

In A Midsummer Night’s Dream, Shakespeare makes Oberon a complex and often contradictory character. While he is initially presented as a "jealous" and possibly rash ruler, his character is made more likeable through his genuine concern for the unhappy Athenian lovers. By the end of the play, the audience’s view of him is balanced between his manipulation of Titania and his essential role in restoring "peace" to the "mazed world".

At the start of the play, Shakespeare reveals the less likeable aspects of Oberon’s character, particularly his "fell and wroth" temper. His conflict with Titania over a "lovely boy stol’n from an Indian king" shows him to be possessive and petty. Rather than seeking a peaceful compromise, he vows to "torment thee for this injury" and uses the magic of "love-in-idleness" to humiliate her. Shakespeare makes this moment quite disturbing: Oberon wants Titania to "wake when some vile thing is near" so that she will "pursue it with the soul of love". This desire to see his wife dote on a "lion, bear, or wolf" purely for his own advantage makes him seem more like a domestic tyrant than a likeable king.

However, Oberon becomes much more sympathetic through his interaction with the mortal world. When he overhears Demetrius being "hard-hearted" towards Helena, his reaction is one of immediate compassion. He calls Helena a "nymph" and promises that "ere he do leave this grove / Thou shalt fly him, and he shall seek thy love". Here, Oberon acts as a manipulator who uses his power to help those who are suffering. Unlike his "shrewd and knavish" servant Puck, Oberon takes the human "jangling" seriously. When he discovers Puck’s mistake, he is genuinely concerned, noting that "of thy misprision must perforce ensue / Some true love turned". This desire to fix the "fruitless vision" of the lovers' errors makes the audience forgive his earlier cruelty.

Ultimately, Oberon’s likeability is secured by his role as the restorer of order and "all prosperity". Once he has the "changeling boy" he desired, he chooses to "undo / This hateful imperfection" of Titania’s eyes. His declaration that "now thou and I are new in amity" suggests that his "debate" and "dissension" were temporary. In the final scene, he leads the fairies to "bless" the marriage beds, ensuring the children of the couples are "fortunate" and free from the "blots of nature’s hand". By concluding the play with this religious hush and a promise of safety, Shakespeare reminds us that Oberon’s power is fundamentally benevolent.

In conclusion, Oberon is not a simple hero, but a powerful spirit whose actions are driven by both ego and empathy. While he is presented as rash and jealous at the start, his efforts to ensure that "all shall be well" for the mortals make him a character the audience eventually respects. Shakespeare reveals that Oberon is likeable because he is the only character with the power to turn heaven into hell and back again, choosing peace in the end.

In this passage from Act 1 Scene 2, Shakespeare makes the introduction of Nick Bottom highly entertaining by presenting him as a man with a massive ego and no self-control. Through Bottom’s constant interruptions and his absurd belief that he can play every role in the play better than anyone else, Shakespeare creates a sense of "tragical mirth". Bottom is the ultimate comic "construct" who turns a simple casting meeting into a chaotic performance, showing the audience that his "chief humour" is to be the centre of attention.

Shakespeare initially makes Bottom entertaining by showing his desire to dominate the scene. As soon as Quince calls his name, Bottom asks, "What is Pyramus? A lover or a tyrant?" This reveals his over-confidence; he does not care what the part is, as long as it allows him to show off. When told it is a lover, he claims it will "ask some tears in the true performing of it", but then immediately admits his "chief humour is for a tyrant". To prove this, he recites a hilariously bad poem about "The raging rocks / And shivering shocks" which "Shall break the locks / Of prison gates". This is entertaining because Bottom believes this "lofty" verse is high art, while the audience can see it is just noisy, empty shouting.

The comedy increases as Bottom tries to steal the roles assigned to his friends. When Francis Flute is told to play Thisbe, Bottom immediately interrupts, "An I may hide my face, let me play Thisbe too". He offers to speak in a "monstrous little voice" to prove he can handle the female lead. Later, when Snug is cast as the lion, Bottom again demands, "Let me play the lion too". He promises to roar so well that the Duke will say, "Let him roar again". This is funny because it shows Bottom has no understanding of how a play works; he thinks a play is just a series of chances for him to be noticed. His claim that he will "roar you as gently as any sucking dove" is a ridiculous contradiction that highlights his stupidity.

Finally, Shakespeare uses the interaction between Bottom and Quince to reveal Bottom’s foolishness. Bottom is so difficult to manage that Quince has to resort to flattery to get him to cooperate. Quince tells him that he "can play no part but Pyramus," describing him as a "sweet-faced man" and a "most lovely, gentlemanlike man". Bottom is so vain that he falls for this instantly, replying, "Well, I will undertake it". This is an entertaining end to the introduction because it shows that despite his "tyrant’s vein," Bottom is easily controlled by anyone who strokes his ego. Shakespeare reveals that Bottom is not a threat to the play’s order, but a harmless, lovable fool.

In conclusion, Shakespeare makes this an entertaining introduction by using Bottom’s arrogance as a source of slapstick comedy. Through his "monstrous" voices, his "gentle" roaring, and his desperate need for praise, Bottom is established as the heart of the play’s humour. The scene proves that while the "mechanicals" might be "slow of study," Bottom, is fast to turn any situation into a hilarious disaster.

In A Midsummer Night’s Dream, Shakespeare’s portrayal of Puck, also known as Robin Goodfellow, is essential to the play’s dramatic impact because he acts as the engine of the plot and the source of its unpredictable comedy. As a "shrewd and knavish sprite", Puck represents the volatile nature of magic, moving between the fairy and mortal worlds to create the "jangling" conflict that defines the play. Through his mischievous personality, his transformative powers, and his direct relationship with the audience, Puck ensures that the play remains a vision of entertaining chaos rather than a dark tragedy.

Shakespeare initially uses Puck to establish a sense of supernatural energy and mischief. Puck describes himself as the "merry wanderer of the night" who enjoys playing tricks on humans, such as "Mislead night wanderers, laughing at their harm?" This contributes to the dramatic impact by setting a tone where anything can happen. His presence makes the woods feel dangerous yet funny; he is a spirit who would hide in a drink or pretend to be a "three-foot stool" just to see someone fall. This portrayal reveals that the magic in the play is not always dignified or helpful, but is often driven by a "knavish" desire for a laugh at the expense of mortals.

Puck’s negligence is the main structural device that creates the play’s middle-act complications. When Oberon orders him to find an "Athenian" man, Puck’s mistake in anointing Lysander instead of Demetrius triggers the "sport" of the lovers’ quarrel. Puck does not feel guilty about this error; instead, he famously declares, "Lord, what fools these mortals be!" This contributes to the dramatic impact by providing a cynical, comedic perspective on the lovers’ passion. To Puck, the intense emotional suffering of Hermia and Helena is merely a "fond pageant," and his enjoyment of their "prepost’rous" situation encourages the audience to laugh at the absurdity of young love.

The transformation of Bottom is another way Puck creates a striking dramatic impact. Puck views the Mechanicals as "hempen homespuns" and "rude mechanicals," showing his contempt for their low social status. By fixing an "ass’s nole" on Bottom’s head, he creates a visual spectacle that is both monstrous and strange. This action bridges the two subplots, as it leads to the extreme situation of the Fairy Queen doting on a "monster". Puck’s commentary on how the others "fly" in fear from Bottom adds to the excitement of the scene, proving that he is the 'Captain' of the woods who controls everyone’s reality.

Finally, Puck is responsible for the play’s resolution and its lasting impact on the audience. He is the one who applies the "remedy" to the lovers' eyes so that "all shall be well". In his famous Epilogue, he addresses the audience as "shadows," suggesting that if they are "offended," they should simply think they have "slumbered" and seen a "dream". By framing the entire experience as a "weak and idle theme," Puck softens the "injury" of the play’s earlier cruelty. He concludes the drama by offering amends, leaving the audience with the feeling that the chaos was just a harmless vision.

In conclusion, Puck contributes to the dramatic impact by being the most active and mad spirit in the play. He is the character who forces the seasons of the plot to change, moving from the bitter chiding of the lovers to the peace of the wedding. Shakespeare’s Puck is not just a servant to Oberon, but a powerful force who reminds us that "reason and love keep little company together," making the play a truly memorable exploration of human folly.
