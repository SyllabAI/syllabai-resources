# Mark Schemes — How To Answer The Unseen Questions
**4. Unseen** · Cambridge (CIE) IGCSE english-literature (0475 & 0992)


## Exam Questions (4 questions)


### Q1
## Model answer

The writer builds a strong sense of tension by making us feel every moment of Nadia’s fear on the high board, and by holding back the ending so we are left, like her, on the edge.

The writer creates tension straight away by contrasting how easy the dive looked with how frightening it becomes. “The ladder had seemed like nothing from the ground” is a short, blunt opening, but it sets up the gap between watching and doing. When Nadia climbs, “the rungs seemed to narrow under her wet feet”, and the detail of the “wet feet” makes us imagine slipping, which adds to the fear. The board is described as “white and dry and utterly still, over a very long way of nothing”, and the phrase “a very long way of nothing” makes the empty air below feel huge and threatening.

The writer then uses what Nadia sees to build the tension. The pool “had shrunk to a bright blue rectangle” and the people “had become small and busy and far away”, which shows how high up she is and how alone she feels. The simile that their voices are “flattened by distance into a single soft roar, like the sea heard through a shell” is effective, because it cuts her off from everyone below and makes the moment feel strange and dreamlike. Even the “breeze she had not felt on the ground” adds to the sense that she has entered a different, more dangerous world.

The most tense moment comes when she looks down. “That was the mistake” is a short sentence that lands like a warning. The water “now looked hard as a tabletop, a flat blue lid over something she could not see the bottom of”, and this simile is frightening because it turns something soft into something solid and deadly. The writer then makes the fear physical: “Her heart was doing something complicated and painful against her ribs, and her legs had turned to something that was not quite bone.” I think describing her legs like this is clever, because it shows she is losing control of her own body.

The people around her make the tension worse. The instructor’s voice is “kind and bored at the same time”, and the fact that he has said it “a thousand times” reminds us that this is huge for Nadia but nothing to him, which makes her feel even more alone. Her little brother, watching from below, adds pressure too, because “he thought she could do anything” — she does not want to let him down, and this raises what is at stake.

The ending is where the writer uses tension most cleverly. Nadia realises “being ready was not a thing that arrived; it was a thing you decided”, and then “before the part of her that wanted to live could stop her, she let her weight tip forward into the empty air —”. The writer ends on a dash, right at the moment she jumps, so we never see her hit the water. This leaves the tension unresolved and holds us in the same frightening moment as Nadia. I found this a powerful way to end, because the fear is left hanging in the air, exactly like she is.

Overall, the writer creates tension through the contrast between watching and doing, the frightening images of the water and the height, the physical description of Nadia’s fear, and the unresolved ending. By stopping at the very moment of the jump, the writer makes us feel the tension of the leap without ever letting it go.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> This is a 25-mark question. This answer would be placed in the top band (Level 8) because it analyses closely how the writer’s methods build tension, uses short embedded quotations, tracks the tension as it rises through the passage, and gives a genuine personal response to the unresolved ending.
> 
> **Commentary**
> 
> The question is about atmosphere, so the response keeps its focus on how tension is made: the contrast between watching and doing, the frightening similes (“hard as a tabletop”, “like the sea heard through a shell”), the physical description of fear, the isolating instructor and the watching brother, and the dash that ends the passage mid-jump. Each point names a method and explains its effect on the reader. Quotations are short and never become retelling. The reading of the unresolved ending as leaving the reader “on the edge” shows the evaluative, personal response expected at the top band.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Keep your focus on the key word “tension” — for every quotation, explain how it makes the reader tense
> - Choose short quotations and embed them; do not retell the story
> - Track how the tension builds through the passage, from the climb to the final dash
> - Analyse the similes (“hard as a tabletop”) and the physical descriptions of fear closely
> - Comment on how the writer uses other people (the bored instructor, the watching brother) to raise the stakes
> - Say something about the ending: the dash leaves the moment unresolved — explain that effect
> - It is unseen prose, so work only from the passage; do not invent what happens next

### Q2
## Model answer

Sowerby vividly conveys the speaker’s mixed feelings about turning into his father — a slow, almost frightened realisation that moves from resistance to a kind of sad acceptance.

At the start, the poet shows the change creeping in through tiny habits. “It starts with the small things. The way / I check the locks twice, then a third time” — the flat, everyday opening makes the feeling ordinary and real, and the list of “back door, front door, the bolt on the shed” shows the speaker copying his father’s exact routine, “same circuit he walked every night of his life”. I think the word “same” is important here, because it shows the speaker noticing he has become a copy without ever choosing to.

The poet then makes the inheritance physical. The speaker says “I’ve got his hands now — the same blunt thumbs, / the nail he lost to a car door in ’91 / grown back wrong on me too, somehow”. The specific detail of the lost nail makes it feel true, and the idea that his father’s old injury has “grown back wrong on me too” suggests the speaker has inherited not just habits but damage, as if the past has printed itself onto his body. This makes his feelings seem uneasy — he is watching himself become someone else.

The speaker’s discomfort grows when he hears his father in his own voice. “I say his sayings without meaning to. / ‘We’ll see.’ ‘Money doesn’t grow on trees.’” The ordinary phrases show how deep the inheritance goes, and the simile that “The words come up like water from a well / I never knew I’d sunk, or he’d dug first” is the cleverest moment in the poem. It suggests the father’s influence is buried deep inside the speaker, and that he is drawing on it without realising, which makes becoming his father feel unavoidable rather than chosen.

The turning point is when the speaker admits how he used to feel. “For years I swore I’d not turn out like him. / Now here I am” — the short, blunt sentence “Now here I am” shows the gap between what he wanted and what has happened, and I feel there is a quiet defeat in it. He has lost the argument with himself.

The ending is what makes the poem so striking. The speaker is “at the mirror, half-past six, / shaving the exact same patch I always miss — / and there he is, looking back, missing it too.” The poet saves the biggest moment for the last line, and it works because the father appears in the mirror as the speaker’s own reflection. It is sad and a little funny at the same time — the small failure of missing the same patch of stubble becomes proof that the two men are, in the end, the same person. The word “there” makes it feel as if the father is really present, alive in the speaker’s face.

Overall, Sowerby conveys the speaker’s feelings as a mixture of resistance, unease and, finally, acceptance. Through the everyday habits, the physical detail of the hands, the simile of the well and the reflection in the mirror, he shows a man realising he has become his father — and by the end I feel that this is something the speaker has stopped fighting, even if he is not quite happy about it.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> This is a 25-mark question. This answer would be placed in the top band (Level 8) because it analyses the poet’s methods closely, traces the speaker’s changing feelings across the whole poem, uses short embedded quotations, and shows a personal, critical understanding of the poem’s deeper idea about inheritance.
> 
> **Commentary**
> 
> The response follows the speaker’s feelings from resistance to acceptance rather than listing techniques: it reads the flat, everyday opening, the physical inheritance of the hands and nail, the well simile, and the mirror image, and each time explains the effect and how it conveys the speaker’s mixed feelings. Quotations are short and well chosen. The reading of the final line — the father appearing as the speaker’s own reflection — is precise and shows the individual response expected at the top band. It addresses “vividly” throughout and never drifts into narrative.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Trace the speaker’s feelings as they change — here from swearing he won’t become his father to accepting that he has
> - Name the method (list, simile, the final mirror image) and explain its effect, not just its presence
> - Keep quotations short and embedded, and keep linking them to how the speaker feels
> - Look hard at the last line — poems like this often save their biggest effect for the ending
> - Notice the physical details (the hands, the lost nail) and what they suggest about inheritance
> - Give a genuine personal response to the tone — is it sad, funny, resigned, or all three?
> - It is an unseen poem, so write only about the words on the page, not the poet’s life

### Q3
## Model answer

The writer makes me feel a deep, quiet sadness for Amara, showing how much it hurts to leave a home so that the loss almost feels physical — but also giving a gentle comfort by the end.

From the start, small ordinary details make the loss feel real. The kettle “had already been packed”, so they drink “the last tea standing up because the chairs were gone”. The phrase “the last tea” makes an everyday thing feel final, and having to stand shows the home is already being taken apart around them. I think this feels honest rather than over the top, because the feeling comes through the objects instead of being stated.

The writer then makes the empty flat seem alive. Stripped bare, “the place seemed to stretch and echo, as if it had been holding its breath for fourteen years and had finally let it out.” This is a powerful bit of personification: the flat is given a body and a breath, so leaving feels like the end of a living relationship. The “pale rectangles where the pictures had hung” are like ghosts of the family’s life, and they made me realise how much of Amara’s past is being wiped away.

As she moves through the rooms, the exact, sensory memories make us feel more sympathy. The “faint brown ring” left by “the steam of a thousand dinners” puts years of family life into one small stain, while the pencil height-marks “each one dated in her father’s careful hand” show her whole childhood. When she presses her thumb to “the highest mark” and feels that “if she rubbed hard enough she could keep it”, the writer shows how much she wants to hold on; the word “absurdly” shows she knows it is impossible, which makes it sadder.

The removal men make the loss feel worse by contrast. The flat “was suddenly full of strangers and the squeal of tape”, and the harsh word “squeal” breaks the tender mood. Watching them “lift her mattress as though it weighed nothing at all” is really sad in a quiet way — to them it is just an object, but to her it is part of her life — and her stillness, when “she could not move”, shows how overwhelmed she is.

The ending is made important by a change towards understanding and comfort. Looking at “the particular grey square of sky” and the swifts that “screamed round the rooftops at dusk”, Amara realises “a place could be full of you without your ever noticing… you only felt the shape of it when it was taken away.” This feels true to me. But the writer does not leave her alone in her sadness: her mother’s hand “found Amara’s shoulder and stayed there, warm and certain”, and the simple words “We carry it with us” give real comfort. The last image — Amara stopping “as though she had heard her name” — is sad and hopeful at the same time, as if the home is calling to her even as she leaves.

Overall, the writer makes me feel Amara’s loss strongly through personification, small tender details and the way the passage moves from sadness to comfort. By the end I feel that leaving a home is not just losing a place but carrying it inside you — and that Amara, held by her mother, will do exactly that.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> This is a 25-mark question. This answer would be placed in the top band (Level 8) because it is a sensitive, well-organised response that analyses how the writer’s methods shape the reader’s feelings, uses short embedded quotations, and tracks the passage’s emotional movement from loss to comfort with a genuine personal response.
> 
> **Commentary**
> 
> The question asks how the writer makes the reader feel, so the response keeps returning to its own reactions (“made me realise”, “This feels true to me”) while always tying them to method — the personification of the flat, the sensory detail of the brown ring and pencil marks, the harsh “squeal”, the mother’s steadying hand. It selects short quotations and never retells the story. It notices the structural shift towards comfort at the end and reads the final image as both sad and hopeful, which shows the individual, evaluative response expected at the top band. As an unseen response it works only from the passage.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - The question is about your feelings, so keep saying how each detail makes you feel — but always anchor it to a specific word or image
> - Choose short quotations and embed them; do not copy out long chunks
> - Track how the mood changes across the passage — here it moves from loss to comfort
> - Look closely at the personification of the flat (“holding its breath”) and explain its effect
> - Notice small, precise details (the brown ring, the pencil marks) and why they carry so much feeling
> - Comment on the ending separately — examiners reward a response to how a passage is shaped
> - It is unseen prose, so do not guess at the writer or where it is from; work only from the passage

### Q4
## Model answer

Alleyne shows the natural world as something patient, alive and impossible to stop — a force that lasts longer than anything people build against it.

From the start, the poet makes nature feel active and deliberate. She tells us to “Give the land one wet season / and see how green come marching back”, and the word “marching” shows the green like an army moving forward, sure of itself. Using the single word “green” for the whole living world makes nature seem like one big living thing rather than lots of separate plants. I think this is effective because it makes something we usually see as passive seem like it has its own will. The list that follows — “vine over the fence, moss on the step, / the old road swallowed soft in leaf” — shows nature quietly taking back places people made, and the soft word “swallowed” suggests it does this almost gently.

The poet keeps telling us that nothing human stays still. “Nothing here stay still for long” is short and blunt, almost like a saying, and the Creole grammar — “Bush push up”, “lizard flick” — gives the poem a spoken voice, as if the speaker belongs to this place. The images make the world feel alive: “the lizard flick its blue-quick tongue” is sharp and quick, while the simile “the guava drop its fruit like small suns” makes the fruit sound warm and precious, falling “unafraid” of the town.

The turning point comes when the speaker admits, “I used to think the town was strong, / its concrete arms, its iron gate”. Here the town is given human parts too, but they are cold and hard next to the soft green. The poet then puts the two side by side — “but concrete crack, and green come through” — and the hard ‘c’ sounds in “concrete crack” almost sound like something breaking. The part that moves me most is the simile that nature is “patient as a grandmother's hand / smoothing, smoothing what the years undo”: the repeated “smoothing” slows the line down, and comparing nature to a grandmother links it to family and love, not just survival.

In the last stanza this quiet power turns into a personal statement. “So let them build their walls up high” sounds almost defiant, and the speaker is sure that “This wild will find the smallest seam, / will root itself in what they leave”. The final line — “and grow me back a home from air” — is the most powerful for me: nature does not just take back the land, it seems to grow the speaker a sense of belonging out of nothing. The word “home” makes it personal — the power of the natural world is also the power to give a person a place to belong.

Overall, Alleyne shows the natural world as gentle but impossible to beat. Through personification, the vivid images and the gentle simile of the grandmother, she makes the green feel alive, patient and stronger than concrete. By the end, I don't think the poem is really about plants taking over a town — it is about the quiet strength of belonging, the feeling that nature, and the speaker, will always find a way to grow back.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> This is a 25-mark question. This answer would be placed in the top band (Level 8) because it is a sustained, sensitive and detailed response that analyses the poet's methods closely, integrates well-chosen short quotations, and shows a genuine personal engagement with the poem's deeper meaning about resilience and belonging.
> 
> **Commentary**
> 
> The response works through the poem rather than listing devices: it names each method — the personification of “green come marching back”, the guava simile, the Creole voice, the grandmother simile, the contrast of “concrete crack” with the green — and each time explains its effect and links it back to the power of the natural world. Quotations are short and embedded. The reading of the final line as being about belonging, not just plants, shows the individual response and critical understanding expected at the top band. It addresses the intensifier “vividly” throughout and keeps a personal voice (“I think”, “the part that moves me most”) without ever slipping into narrative or losing focus on the writer's methods. As an unseen response it rightly uses no biographical or contextual material.

> **[exam-tip]**
> - Track the poem through to the turn at “I used to think the town was strong” — the shift is where the meaning deepens
> - Name the method (personification, simile, contrast) and then explain its effect; do not just spot it
> - Keep quotations short and embedded, and always link them back to the key word in the question — here, “power”
> - Respond to the word “vividly”: show how the poet makes the natural world feel alive
> - Notice what the Creole voice (“green come”, “lizard flick”) adds — a sense of a real speaker and a real place
> - Include a genuine personal response — how the poem makes you feel or what you think it is really about
> - It is an unseen poem, so do not write about the poet's life or context — focus only on close reading
