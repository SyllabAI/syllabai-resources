### Q1
## Model answer

While both texts are about British men overcoming dyslexia and achieving success, Carter in Text One succeeded despite his dyslexia, whereas Zephaniah in Text Two feels he succeeded because of it.

As young boys, both men seem to have had quite a difficult time. In Text One, some teachers suggested that Will Carter would end up “a criminal” and the writer states that he had been “miserable and lonely at primary school”. The writer uses the blunt, short statement “school was torment” to underscore what an unhappy and distressing time it was for Carter. Similarly, despite being written in the first person rather than the third person narrative used in Text One, Zephaniah conveys the difficulties he experienced in his early years through the equally blunt “as a child I suffered”.

In addition, in both texts the men had teachers who were not interested in hearing their opinions. In Text One, Carter’s teacher asks him if he was a “retard” and asks him to read out loud as a “way of silencing” him because she knew he couldn’t. In Text Two, Zephaniah was told to “shut up, stupid boy” and he reports that he was called “stupid for even thinking about it”. Both men were wrongly considered to have low levels of intelligence by ignorant teachers who spoke to them in a shocking and disrespectful way. Both were devalued and stereotyped by these teachers, with some thinking that Carter would end up a “criminal” and Zephaniah being told that he would “end up being a good sportsperson” because “we can’t all be intelligent”. The use of the direct speech in both texts emphasises the impact these teachers and what they said had on both men, shaping their views of themselves and their ability to succeed.

There is also a difference in the men’s experiences due when they were at school. In Text One we are told that Carter at age 13, knowing he had dyslexia, went to his teacher to “demand a teaching assistant and a laptop”. In contrast, Zephaniah did not even know what dyslexia was until he was 21 and his lack of understanding is conveyed through the question “Do I need an operation?”. The purpose of Text One is to inform readers about the tough challenges people with dyslexia face via Will Carter’s story, in which he and his grandparents had to fight and demonstrate that he was being discriminated against. He was able to use the assistance of technology which, for him, “changed everything”, but the tone is still cautionary as Carter believes the education sector has still been “too slow to embrace” all of the technology available. Carter believes he has been lucky to get the support he required and not everyone is so fortunate.

In contrast, technology and additional support were not available to Zephaniah and yet his message and tone are more positive overall. Zephaniah believes that dyslexia can help creativity and urges people to “use it to your advantage”, using his own success as an example. Text Two employs a more light-hearted, celebratory and reassuring tone, ending with the inclusive “us dyslexic people, we’ve got it going on” whereas Text One ends on a more downbeat note.

### Q2
## Model answer

The writer, Benjamin Zephaniah, uses a range of language and structural devices to convey both his negative and positive experiences of education, presenting his journey from struggle to success. He ultimately encourages others to feel empowered by their differences.

Firstly, Zephaniah opens with the blunt statement “As a child I suffered,” immediately setting a serious and emotive tone. The use of the verb “suffered” highlights the extent of his early difficulties, which is then juxtaposed with a tone of hope by using the contrasting conjunction “but” to mark a turning point as he “learned to turn dyslexia” to his advantage and to see the world “more creatively”. He uses a cyclical structure, as the phrase “We are the architects. We are the designers,” is repeated both at the beginning and end of the text, reinforcing his message of creativity and pride and creating an uplifting and positive tone. Within this paragraph, he also moves from the first person singular “I” to the more inclusive first person plural “we” to convey the fact that he is part of a wider community of people with dyslexia, something which he appears to be proud of.

Zephaniah also uses anecdotes to convey his experiences, especially the negative ones. He recalls being told to “shut up, stupid boy” and being called “stupid” for thinking creatively. He also recounts his experiences of being stereotyped through being told that he would simply “end up being a good sportsperson”. Although he didn’t realise that he was being stereotyped at the time, he uses the example to highlight how people with dyslexia were often underestimated, which is something he challenges throughout the text.

Furthermore, the writer structures the text using a chronological timeline, using time markers such as “when I was 10 or 11” and “when I was 13”. This allows the reader to follow Zephaniah’s development over time, mirroring the growth in his own confidence and self-acceptance, and to establish the fact that he has overcome the challenges he faced.

Towards the end of the text, Zephaniah adopts a more advisory tone, addressing the reader directly by saying “if you’re dyslexic” to offer reassurance and encouragement. His confident and self-assured tone continues to the end, advising those reading that “it’s not you”. He does not convey anger at the negative experiences he has had, but rather chooses to focus on his success and gives advice to parents through the imperative “don’t think of it as a defect” to reinforce his positive and hopeful message.

In Palin’s account, we are introduced to Prince Malik the day before the race, and in Levine’s text she describes the extended anticipation as she waits for the racers.

In addition, both writers experience and describe scenes of mayhem. In Text One, Palin describes how one of the team of bulls “plunge headfirst into the crowd” and “two more hurtle off towards a flagpole, taking out the flag”. Palin offers a humorous, observational account, while Levine plunges the reader into the wild, chaotic energy of the race. Together, they show how different writers can capture similar experiences in contrasting ways, using tone, structure, and language to shape their perspectives.
