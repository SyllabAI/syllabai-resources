### Q1
Headlands and bays are formed along discordant coastlines, where bands of hard and soft rock lie at right angles to the sea. **[KU] **Over time, these rocks are eroded at different rates due to their resistance. **[KU]**

The soft rock, such as clay or sandstone, is eroded more quickly by the sea. Processes like hydraulic action, where water is forced into cracks, and abrasion, where pebbles scrape against the rock, are stronger in these weaker areas. **[KU] **As a result, the softer rock is worn away, forming a bay. **[KU]**

In contrast, harder rock such as chalk or limestone is more resistant to erosion. It is left jutting out into the sea while the surrounding softer rock is eroded. These areas form headlands, which are more exposed to wave energy. **[KU]**

Wave refraction causes waves to concentrate their energy on the sides of the headland, increasing erosion over time. This can lead to further erosion of the headland into cliffs, caves, and arches. **[KU]**

In the bays, wave energy is weaker, so deposition happens, and beaches often form in these sheltered areas. **[KU]**

An example of this is seen at Swanage Bay in Dorset, where bands of clay, sandstone, and chalk have formed alternating bays and headlands like Ballard Point and Peveril Point. **[KU]**

This shows how geology and coastal processes work together to shape the coastline into headlands and bays. **[KU]**
