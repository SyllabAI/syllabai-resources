### Q1
Top-down strategies are large-scale and often government-led. **[KU]** They can provide quick results with big projects. For example, Brazil’s government invested in the Tucuruí Dam to generate hydroelectric power. **[KU]** This created electricity for industry and cities. **[KU]**

However, top-down strategies can be expensive and may harm the environment or displace communities. **[E]** Dams often flood farmland and force people to relocate. **[KU]** They may also prioritise industrial needs over local communities. **[E]**

Bottom-up strategies are smaller, community-led projects. **[KU]** These are often supported by NGOs. **[KU]** For example, hand pumps for clean water in African villages improve health and reduce disease. **[KU]** Solar cookers reduce deforestation and smoke-related illnesses. **[E]** Bottom-up strategies are usually cheap, locally appropriate, and sustainable. **[E]**

Yet, bottom-up projects often have limited reach. **[E]** They may not provide the large-scale infrastructure needed for national development, such as power plants or highways. **[KU]** Top-down schemes can achieve wider economic impacts, while bottom-up schemes focus on social and environmental needs. **[E]**

Overall, both approaches have strengths and weaknesses. **[E]** Bottom-up is more sustainable at the community level, but top-down can deliver large-scale change. **[E]** The best results come from combining both, depending on context. **[E]**
