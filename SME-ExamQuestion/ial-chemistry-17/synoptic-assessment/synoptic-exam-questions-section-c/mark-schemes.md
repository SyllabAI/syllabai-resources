# Mark Schemes — Synoptic Exam Questions (Section C)
**Synoptic assessment** · Edexcel International A Level (IAL) Chemistry (YCH11)


## Q1 — hard — 20 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 2 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two functional groups present in the glucose molecule:
> 
> - Hydroxyl group / –OH (group) / alcohol **[1 mark]**
> - Aldehyde group / –CHO
> **OR**
> Carbonyl at end of chain **[1 mark]**
> 
> Hydroxide / OH<sup>-</sup> is not accepted for the first mark.
> 
> "Carbonyl" alone and "ketone" are not accepted for the second mark.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The four common functional groups containing a C=O are:
> 
> - Aldehyde (–CHO, at a terminal carbon)
> - Ketone (C=O within the chain)
> - Carboxylic acid (–COOH)
> - Ester (–COO–)
> 
> Be specific as writing just "carbonyl" is too vague and will not score. Glucose has a terminal C=O (aldehyde) and several –OH groups (hydroxyls).
> 
> Do not confuse "hydroxide" (the OH<sup>-</sup> ion) with "hydroxyl" (the –OH group attached to a carbon).

### 2() — 3 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The equation for the fermentation of glucose is:
> 
> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub>→2CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>OH+2CO<sub>2</sub>
> **OR**
> C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> → 2C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub>OH + 2CO<sub>2</sub> **[1 mark]**
> 
> The required conditions are:
> 
> - Yeast / zymase / enzyme from yeast **[1 mark]**
> - Anaerobic / no oxygen / absence of oxygen
> **AND **
> An appropriate warm temperature (30 °C−40 °C) **[1 mark]**
> 
> Unbalanced equations and equations including H<sub>2</sub>O as a product are not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> A common slip is to include water as a product. Fermentation produces only ethanol and CO<sub>2</sub>. Check your equation: 1 mol glucose gives 2 mol ethanol + 2 mol CO<sub>2</sub>.
> 
> Always state "anaerobic" (or "no oxygen") in your conditions. If oxygen were present, the ethanol would be oxidised to ethanoic acid instead.

### 3() — 4 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Comparison of intermolecular forces in ethanol and hexane:
> 
> - Ethanol has London dispersion forces (LDF) 
> **AND **
> Hydrogen bonding **[1 mark]**
> - Hexane has London dispersion forces (LDF) only **[1 mark]**
> - Hydrogen bonds are stronger than London forces **[1 mark]**
> - Therefore more energy is required to break the intermolecular forces in ethanol, resulting in a higher boiling point for ethanol **[1 mark]**
> 
> Just "dispersion forces" and  van der Waals' forces are accepted instead of London dispersion forces (LDF)
> 
> "As *M*<sub>r</sub> increases, boiling point increases" without reference to electrons or intermolecular force is not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> This is a classic trap: smaller molecular mass does not always mean a lower boiling point. That only holds when the intermolecular forces are the same type. When one molecule can hydrogen bond and the other cannot, hydrogen bonding usually dominates, even if the other molecule has stronger London dispersion forces.
> 
> Structure your answer around four things:
> 
> 1. What IMFs are present in each molecule
> 2. Their relative strengths
> 3. Which type is dominant
> 4. The combined energy and boiling-point conclusion

### 4() — 5 marks
i) To calculate the atom economy:

- Write the atom economy formula:

percentage atom economy = $\frac{M_{r}\text{of desired product}}{\text{sum of}M_{r}\text{of all products}}$ x 100

- Using the fermentation equation (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> → 2CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>OH + 2CO<sub>2</sub>), calculate the molar mass of the desired product:

2 × 46.0 = 92.0

**[1 mark]**

- Substitute values with stoichiometric coefficients (coefficient of 2 on ethanol and CO<sub>2</sub>):

percentage atom economy = `\frac{2 \times 46 . 0}{\left(\right. 2 \times 46 . 0 \left.\right) + \left(\right. 2 \times 44 . 0 \left.\right)}` x 100

percentage atom economy = $\frac{92.0}{180.0}$ x 100

**[1 mark]**

percentage atom economy = **51.1% (to 3 s.f.)**

**[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for the correct molar mass of the desired / useful product (2 × 46.0 = 92.0)
> 
> 1 mark for correct substitution with stoichiometric coefficients (`\frac{2 \times 46 . 0}{\left(\right. 2 \times 46 . 0 \left.\right) + \left(\right. 2 \times 44 . 0 \left.\right)}` x 100) **OR** ($\frac{92.0}{180.0}$ x 100)
> 
> 1 mark for a final answer of 51.1% to 3 significant figures.
> 
> **Allow ECF from part (b)** — if the candidate's fermentation equation is wrong, apply the atom-economy method to their own equation and award marks accordingly.
> 
> Allow error carried forward for the final mark from a correct method with a wrong intermediate value.
> 
> Answers not to 3 significant figures or without the % symbol are not accepted.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> ii) Fermentation is considered a more sustainable method of producing ethanol because:
> 
> Any **two** from (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - Plant crops are renewable / sustainable / can be regrown (whereas crude oil is finite)
> - Growing plants absorb *CO*<sub>2</sub> from the atmosphere, making the fuel (closer to) carbon neutral
> 
> **OR**
> 
> Fermentation is carbon-neutral as there is no net increase in atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub>
> 
> - Fermentation operates at moderate temperatures (30–40 °C) with low energy input
> 
> **[2 marks]**
> 
> "Carbon neutral in principle" is accepted.
> 
> Generic "better for the environment" or "more eco-friendly" without specific reasoning is not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Remember to include the stoichiometric coefficients in the atom economy expression: (2 × 46.0) on top for two moles of ethanol, and (2 × 46.0) + (2 × 44.0) on the bottom for two moles each of ethanol and CO<sub>2</sub>.
> 
> There is an important subtlety here:
> 
> - Fermentation has only 51 % atom economy (half the mass is "lost" as CO<sub>2</sub>)
> - Hydration of ethene (C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O → C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub>OH) has 100 % atom economy.
> 
> Atom economy alone does not determine sustainability. You should think about the renewable source and the carbon cycle, not just efficiency.

### 5() — 4 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Advantages and disadvantages of bioethanol as a transport fuel compared with petrol are:
> 
> Advantages — any **two** from (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - Bioethanol is a **renewable** / sustainable resource
> - Bioethanol combustion produces fewer net emissions of greenhouse gases / lower carbon footprint / is carbon neutral in principle
> - Bioethanol produces **no sulfur dioxide emissions** on combustion
> - Bioethanol can be blended with petrol (e.g. E5, E10) and used in existing petrol engines with minimal modification
> - Bioethanol can be produced domestically from local crops, reducing reliance on crude-oil imports
> 
> **[2 marks]**
> 
> Disadvantages — any **two** from (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - Takes up land that could be used for food production (causing food shortages)
> **OR**
> There is insufficient arable land to meet global transport-fuel demand from bioethanol alone
> - Fermentation and distillation require **energy input** (heating, farm machinery, transport)
> - Bioethanol produces less energy per mole/gram than petrol
> - Destruction of habitats/deforestation to grow crops
> - Crop yields (and therefore bioethanol supply) are **variable**, affected by weather and crop failures, making supply less predictable than crude oil
> 
> **[2 marks]**
> 
> Generic statements ("cleaner", "greener", "better for the environment") without specific reasoning are not accepted.
> 
> The same idea re-stated in different words only gains one mark.

> **[exam-tip]**
> This is a balanced-argument question. You need **two advantages AND two disadvantages** to score full marks. Writing four advantages will cap you at 2 marks.
> 
> It is good practice to pair each point with a brief explanation, not just the assertion because sometimes examiners want a little more. For example:
> 
> - "Renewable because crops re-grow each year" should always earn the mark
> - "It is renewable" alone may not.
> 
> Avoid vague "cleaner" or "greener" without specific reasoning, as these score zero.

### 6() — 2 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> A suitable chemical test for ethanol with the expected observation:
> 
> 1 mark (M1) for a correct test:
> 
> - Add a carboxylic acid (e.g. ethanoic acid) **AND** a strong acid catalyst (e.g. concentrated sulfuric acid) **AND** warm
> 
> 1 mark (M2) for the matching observation:
> 
> - A fruity / sweet smell is produced (an ester is formed)
> 
> Partial credit — M2 only (the test does not uniquely identify ethanol but the observation is correct):
> 
> - Acidified potassium dichromate(VI), heated under reflux → orange to green colour change (M1 fails as ethanal also reduces dichromate)
> - Phosphorus pentachloride, PCl<sub>5</sub> → white / misty fumes of HCl (M1 fails as ethanoic acid also reacts with PCl<sub>5</sub>)
> 
> Reject (do not score either mark):
> 
> - Infrared spectroscopy (not a chemical test)
> - 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine / Brady's reagent (only rules out ethanal; does not positively identify ethanol)
> - Sodium carbonate / sodium hydrogencarbonate (only rules out ethanoic acid; does not positively identify ethanol)
> - Sodium metal alone (reacts with both ethanol and ethanoic acid; not selective)
> 
> Mismatched test/observation pairs are not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> For "prove this is X" questions, the answer must be a **positive, selective** test for X — not one that merely rules out other possibilities.
> 
> Esterification is the gold-standard chemical test for an alcohol:
> 
> - Only alcohols undergo esterification with a carboxylic acid + acid catalyst
> - The fruity / sweet smell of the ester is unmistakable in the lab
> 
> Common pitfalls flagged in Edexcel mark schemes:
> 
> - Acidified dichromate(VI): turns orange → green for **both** alcohols (ethanol) and aldehydes (ethanal), so it rules out ethanoic acid but not ethanal — earns the observation mark only.
> - PCl<sub>5</sub>: gives white misty fumes of HCl with **both** alcohols and carboxylic acids — earns the observation mark only.
> - 2,4-DNPH only tests for aldehydes/ketones (negative result here) — does not prove ethanol.
> - Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> only tests for carboxylic acids (negative result here) — does not prove ethanol.
> - Infrared spectroscopy is a spectroscopic technique, not a chemical test, so it scores zero on this style of question.

## Q2 — hard — 20 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 2 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The two functional groups are:
> 
> - Carboxylic acid  / carboxyl / –COOH **[1 mark]**
> - Alcohol / hydroxyl / –OH **[1 mark]**
> 
> "Aldehyde" or "ketone" is not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> 2-Hydroxybutanoic acid has **two** O–H environments:
> 
> - One from the –COOH group (broad, 2500–3300 cm<sup>−1</sup>)
> - One from the alcohol –OH (narrower, around 3400 cm<sup>−1</sup>).
> 
> Students often miss the alcohol if they stop after identifying the carboxylic acid.
> 
> Two diagnostic rules for distinguishing the two O–H absorptions:
> 
> - Carboxylic acid O–H: very broad, extending down into the C–H region below 3000 cm<sup>−1</sup>.
> - Alcohol O–H: narrower, sitting above 3200 cm<sup>−1</sup> and not overlapping the C–H region.
> 
> A sharp, strong absorption at 1720 cm<sup>−1</sup> confirms a C=O group. The type depends on context:
> 
> - Paired with broad 2500–3300 cm<sup>−1</sup> O–H: carboxylic acid.
> - Paired with C–H absorptions at 2700–2900 cm<sup>−1</sup>: aldehyde.
> - With no accompanying O–H feature: most likely a ketone.
> 
> "Aldehyde" and "ketone" are not supported because there is no C–H absorption at 2700–2900 cm<sup>−1</sup> in the spectrum, and the O–H evidence for a carboxylic acid is clear.

### 2() — 3 marks
To deduce the molecular formula:

- Calculate the moles of each element in 100 g:

moles = $\frac{\mathrm{percentage} \mathrm{composition}}{A_{r}}$

moles of C = $\frac{46.2}{12.0}$ = 3.85 mol

moles of H = $\frac{7.69}{1.0}$ = 7.69 mol

moles of O = $\frac{46.2}{16.0}$ = 2.89 mol

**[1 mark]**

- Divide by the smallest value (2.89) to find the simplest ratio:

C = $\frac{3.85}{2.89}$ = 1.33

H = $\frac{7.69}{2.89}$ =2.66

O = $\frac{2.89}{2.89}$ = 1

- Scale up to get the simplest whole number ratio:

C = 1.33 x 3 = 4

H = 2.66 x 3 = 8

O = 1 x 3 = 3

- State the empirical formula:

empirical formula = C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>3</sub>

**[1 mark]**

- Calculate the empirical formula mass:

empirical formula mass = 48 + 8 + 48 = 104

- The empirical formula mass (104) equals the molecular ion peak (*m* / *z* = 104), so the molecular formula equals the empirical formula.

molecular formula = **C**<sub>**4**</sub>**H**<sub>**8**</sub>**O**<sub>**3**</sub>

**[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for correct moles per 100 g: C = 3.85, H = 7.69, O = 2.89.
> 
> 1 mark for empirical formula C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>3</sub>, including the step of multiplying the ratio by 3.
> 
> 1 mark for molecular formula C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>3</sub>, linked to *m* / *z* = 104.
> 
> Presenting the empirical formula as the final answer without linking to *m* / *z* = 104 is not accepted.
> 
> Allow error carried forward from an incorrect mole step if subsequent method is consistent.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The empirical-to-molecular-formula method has four steps:
> 
> 1. Divide percentage composition by *A*<sub>r</sub> to find moles of each element.
> 2. Divide all mole values by the smallest to get the simplest ratio.
> 3. Multiply by a small integer if the ratio does not give whole numbers.
> 4. Compare the empirical formula mass to the molecular ion peak (*m* / *z*) to find the molecular multiplier.
> 
> Step 3 is the one most students rush. Here the ratio C : H : O = 1.33 : 2.66 : 1.00 is not whole numbers, but multiplying by 3 gives the clean whole-number ratio 4 : 8 : 3.
> 
> Always complete step 4 explicitly. Presenting the empirical formula as the final answer does not satisfy a "deduce the molecular formula" question, even when the empirical and molecular formulae happen to be identical.

### 3() — 5 marks
The structure of Compound **X** is:

![Skeletal formula of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid, with labels showing the 1H NMR peaks for certain protons](assets/011-skeletal-formula-of-2-hydroxybutanoic-acid-with-.png)

**[5 marks]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for identifying the peak at 11.3 (ppm) as the −COOH proton
> 
> 1 mark for identifying the peak at 3.00 (ppm) as the −OH / alcohol proton
> 
> 1 mark for identifying the peak at 0.95 (ppm) as a triplet **AND **that it is a CH<sub>3</sub> group adjacent to a CH<sub>2</sub> group
> 
> 1 mark for identifying the peak at 4.15 (ppm) as a triplet **AND **that it is a CH proton adjacent to a CH<sub>2</sub> group
> 
> 1 mark for the correct final structure of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid
> 
> Skeletal, displayed or structural formulae are accepted

> **[exam-tip]**
> Start every NMR problem by checking the H count. The integrations must sum to the total number of H atoms in the molecular formula. Here 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 8, matching C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>3</sub>.
> 
> For each peak, ask three questions:
> 
> 1. How many H atoms (integration)?
> 2. What environment (chemical shift)?
> 3. What neighbours (splitting pattern)?
> 
> Useful recognition patterns:
> 
> - Broad singlets above δ 10 ppm are almost always –COOH.
> - Broad singlets around δ 1–5 ppm are usually –OH (alcohol) or –NH, because these exchangeable protons lose coupling to their neighbours.
> - A triplet (3 lines) indicates exactly 2 neighbouring H atoms via the n+1 rule, pointing to a CH or CH<sub>3</sub> next to a CH<sub>2</sub>.
> - The ethyl group signature (–CH<sub>2</sub>CH<sub>3</sub>) is distinctive: a 3H triplet around δ 0.9–1.0 paired with a 2H multiplet around δ 1.5–1.8.
> 
> The central CH at C2 couples to **both** the CH<sub>2</sub> (2H) on one side **and** the exchangeable OH on the other. Because the OH proton exchanges rapidly, the CH effectively sees only the CH<sub>2</sub> neighbours and appears as a triplet rather than a more complex multiplet.
> 
> 2-Hydroxybutanoic acid has a chiral centre at C2, bonded to four different groups:
> 
> - CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>– (ethyl)
> - –OH
> - –COOH
> - –H
> 
> A racemic mixture is formed when the compound is synthesised from a planar precursor, so the synthetic product shows no net rotation of plane-polarised light.

### 4() — 4 marks
The mechanism for nucleophilic addition of CN<sup>−</sup> to propanal is:

![Chemical reaction diagram showing a cyanide ion attacking a carbonyl carbon, leading to the formation of a hydroxyl and cyanide group on the carbon chain.](assets/012-chemical-reaction-diagram-showing-a-cyanide-ion-.png)

**[4 marks]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for the lone pair on C of CN<sup>−</sup> **AND **curly arrow from the lone pair / negative charge to the carbonyl carbon
> 
> 1 mark for the dipole shown on the C=O bond (δ+ on C, δ− on O) **AND **curly arrow from the C=O double bond to the oxygen atom
> 
> 1 mark for the correct structure of the tetrahedral intermediate with a lone pair **AND **negative charge on the oxygen atom
> 
> 1 mark for a curly arrow from the lone pair on the O<sup>−</sup> of the intermediate to the H<sup>+</sup> ion (or H of HCN) **AND **a correct structure for 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, including the OH group
> 
> Accept a one-step mechanism where –OH is formed directly via proton transfer from HCN, provided all arrows are correct.
> 
> Curly arrows must start from lone pairs / negative charge or the centre of bonds.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Two curly-arrow rules that catch students out every time:
> 
> 1. The nucleophile arrow starts from a **lone pair**, not from the atom symbol. Draw the lone pair on CN<sup>−</sup> as two dots, then curl the arrow from those dots to the carbonyl carbon.
> 2. The arrow from C=O goes from the **π bond to the O atom**, not to empty space. The electrons end up on O as a new lone pair, giving the alkoxide intermediate.
> 
> Nucleophilic addition to an aldehyde like propanal produces a **chiral centre** at the former carbonyl carbon. The four different groups bonded to it are:
> 
> - H
> - CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>– (ethyl)
> - –OH
> - –CN
> 
> Attack from either face of the planar C=O is equally likely, so the product is a **racemic mixture** of the two enantiomers of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile. This contrasts with attack on propanone (a symmetric ketone), which does not give a chiral product because the carbonyl carbon has two identical CH<sub>3</sub> groups.
> 
> The chirality of the nitrile intermediate is inherited by 2-hydroxybutanoic acid after hydrolysis, so the synthetic product is also racemic and shows no net rotation of plane-polarised light.

### 5() — 3 marks
To calculate the percentage yield of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid:

- Calculate the moles of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile:

moles = $\frac{\mathrm{mass}}{M_{r}}$

moles of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile = $\frac{8.50}{85}$

moles of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile = 0.100 mol **[1 mark]**

- The molar ratio of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile to 2-hydroxybutanoic acid is 1:1:

theoretical moles of product = 0.100 mol

- Calculate the theoretical mass of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid:

theoretical mass = moles × *M*<sub>r</sub>

theoretical mass of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid = 0.100 × 104

theoretical mass of 2-hydroxybutanoic acid = 10.4 g **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the percentage yield:

percentage yield = $\frac{\mathrm{actual} \mathrm{mass}}{\mathrm{theoretical} \mathrm{mass}}$ × 100

percentage yield = $\frac{7.80}{10.4}$  x100

percentage yield =  **75.0%** **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> Answers can be given to 2 or 3 significant figures
> 
> Allow error carried forward from an incorrect mole calculation if subsequent steps are consistent.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Percentage yield calculations follow a fixed three-step method:
> 
> 1. Find the moles of the limiting reagent.
> 2. Use the molar ratio to calculate the theoretical mass of product.
> 3. Divide the actual mass by the theoretical mass and multiply by 100.
> 
> Always check the balanced equation for the molar ratio before calculating. Here the 1:1 ratio of nitrile to acid makes the calculation straightforward. A 1:2 or 2:1 ratio would require halving or doubling the moles.
> 
> A percentage yield above 100% indicates an error upstream, usually in the moles step. Industrial hydrolysis yields of 70–95% are typical, so 75% is a reasonable result here.

### 6() — 1 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> One environmental advantage of the biodegradable polyester compared with poly(ethene) for disposable packaging is:
> 
> Any **one **from:
> 
> - Polyester is biodegradable so it will not take up space in landfill / will not persist in the environment (unlike polyethene)
> - Does not produce toxic fumes when incinerated
> - Can be manufactured from renewable resources / plant crops (whereas polyethene is from finite crude oil)
> - Forming the polyeter is carbon-neutral in principle / CO<sub>2</sub> absorbed by the plant during growth is re-released on degradation.
> 
> **[1 mark]**
> 
> General statements without a specific mechanism are not accepted.
> 
> Non-environmental advantages are not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Environmental advantage questions want **specific** reasoning, not vague statements like "it is greener" or "better for the environment." Always tie your answer to one of three concrete angles:
> 
> 1. Biodegradability: which bonds break, and how do microorganisms degrade the polymer?
> 2. Feedstock: is the monomer from a renewable source (plants, fermentation) or a non-renewable one (crude oil)?
> 3. Emissions and degradation products: what does the polymer ultimately become, and is that toxic or harmless?
> 
> Biodegradable polyesters have an advantage over poly(ethene) on all three angles. Pick the most distinct point rather than giving two that overlap (for example, "it degrades" and "it does not persist" are the same point restated).

### 7() — 2 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> The equation for this reaction is:
> 
> - CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>COCl + 2CH<sub>3</sub>NH<sub>2</sub> → CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>CONHCH<sub>3</sub> + CH<sub>3</sub>NH<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup>Cl<sup>−</sup>
> **OR**
> CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>COCl + CH<sub>3</sub>NH<sub>2</sub> → CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>CONHCH<sub>3</sub> + HCl **[1 mark]**
> 
> The name of organic product is:
> 
> - *N*-methylpropanamide **[1 mark]**
> 
> "Propanamide" alone and "methylpropanamide" without the *N*- are not accepted.

> **[exam-tip]**
> Acyl chlorides react with amines to form **amides** via nucleophilic acyl substitution. The general pattern is:
> 
> R–COCl + R'NH<sub>2</sub> → R–CO–NHR' + HCl
> 
> A second equivalent of the amine usually neutralises the HCl to form an ammonium salt, as in the equation above.
> 
> When naming amides with a substituent on nitrogen, use the italic ***N*-** locant. The *N*- is non-negotiable: omitting it costs a mark. Write it in italic to show it is a locant, not a position number.
> 
> If the amine had been ammonia instead of methylamine, the product would be **propanamide** (CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>CONH<sub>2</sub>), which carries no *N*- prefix because the nitrogen only carries H atoms.

## Q3 — hard — 20 marks · exam-questions

### 1() — 5 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> For the synthesis of DEET from 3-methylbenzoic acid:
> 
> Step 1 (3-methylbenzoic acid to acyl chloride):
> 
> Any **one **of the following reagent and condition pairs (1 mark for each point):
> 
> - Reagent 1: Phosphorus(V) chloride / PCl<sub>5</sub>
> - Condition 1: Room temperature / dry / anhydrous
> 
> - Reagent 2: Thionyl chloride / SOCl<sub>2</sub>
> - Condition2: Dry / anhydrous 
> **OR**
> Heat / reflux
> 
> **[2 marks]**
> 
> Step 2 (acyl chloride to DEET):
> 
> - Reagent: diethylamine / (CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>)<sub>2</sub>NH **[1 mark]**
> - Condition: room temperature
> **OR**
> Heat / reflux **[1 mark]**
> 
> The intermediate is:
> 
> - 3-methylbenzoyl chloride **[1 mark]**
> 
> 3-methylbenzyl chloride (without the 'o' of -oyl) is not accepted as the intermediate name.

> **[exam-tip]**
> For step 1, anhydrous or dry conditions must be specified because acyl chlorides react immediately with water. Any aqueous solvent would hydrolyse the intermediate before Step 2 can occur.
> 
> For Step 2, the nitrogen in DEET has two ethyl groups. This means that the reagent must be a secondary amine, diethylamine ((CH<sub>3</sub>CH<sub>2</sub>)<sub>2</sub>NH). Using ammonia or a primary amine would give a product with the wrong number of substituents on the nitrogen atom.
> 
> The most common naming error is 3-methylbenzyl chloride instead of 3-methylbenzoyl chloride. The intermediate is an acyl chloride, which has a C=O group directly bonded to the chlorine atom (–COCl). A benzyl chloride has a –CH<sub>2</sub>Cl group with no carbonyl. In the name, the suffix -oyl indicates the presence of a carbonyl group.

### 2() — 2 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**
> Diethylamine is a stronger base than ammonia because its alkyl groups increase electron density on nitrogen:
> 
> - The ethyl / alkyl groups are electron-releasing
> 
> **OR**
> 
> The ethyl / alkyl groups have a positive inductive effect
> 
> **OR**
> 
> The ethyl / alkyl groups push electron density towards the nitrogen atom **[1 mark]**
> 
> - This increases the electron density on the lone pair of nitrogen, making it more available to accept a proton
> 
> **OR**
> 
> This makes the lone pair in diethylamine a better proton acceptor **[1 mark]**
> 
> References to delocalisation into a ring are not accepted. There is no benzene ring bonded to this nitrogen atom.

The implication of this value for industrial PET recycling is:

- The rate is too slow to be industrially / economically viable at 25 °C
**OR**
The rate would be too slow for a commercial process
**OR**
Elevated temperature is required for an industrial process **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for correct application of the two-point Arrhenius expression with 298 K and 333 K substituted
> 
> 1 mark for *k*<sub>298</sub> in the range 4–7 x 10<sup>-5</sup> mol<sup>-1</sup> dm<sup>3</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>
> 
> Allow error carried forward from an incorrect *E*<sub>a</sub> value in part (c)
> 
> 1 mark for a comment that the rate / rate constant at 25 °C is too slow to be industrially / economically viable

> **[exam-tip]**
> When extrapolating with Arrhenius, watch the sign convention. ln(*k*<sub>lower T</sub> / *k*<sub>higher T</sub>) is *negative* (the lower-*T* constant is smaller). Setting the equation up as ln(*k*<sub>298</sub>/*k*<sub>333</sub>) gives you a negative answer, confirming *k*<sub>298</sub> < *k*<sub>333</sub>.
> 
> Examiner reports consistently penalise vague "comment" marks here. Link the small *k* value to a real-world consequence: "industrial reactors operate at ~80 °C because residence time at 25 °C would be uneconomically long". Saying only "the reaction is too slow" without any industrial context earns no credit.

> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) The sign of Δ*S*<sub>system</sub> for the overall hydrolysis of PET is:
> 
> - Positive
> **AND**
> A solid giant polymer breaks down into (many) aqueous and liquid molecules (increasing the number of particles / disorder) **[1 mark]**
> 
> "Entropy is positive" is not accepted
> 
> ii) The alkaline hydrolysis of PET is impractically slow at room temperature because:
> 
> - The activation energy for the hydrolysis is (very) high / the reactants are kinetically stable **[1 mark]**
> - Very few particles / collisions have sufficient energy to meet or exceed the activation energy *E*<sub>a</sub> at room temperature **[1 mark]**
> 
> References to *p* (steric factor) or *A* (pre-exponential factor) in the Arrhenius equation are not accepted

> **[exam-tip]**
> This is the thermodynamic vs kinetic distinction clearly stated in the specification.
> 
> One of the most common examples is the Haber reaction at room temperature:
> 
> N<sub>2</sub> + 3H<sub>2</sub> ⇌ 2NH<sub>3</sub> - Δ*S*<sub>total</sub> > 0
> 
> It is thermodynamically feasible but the reaction is kinetically stable because the activation energy for breaking the N≡N triple bond is huge. So, it does not occur at a measurable rate without a catalyst and high temperature.
> 
> Examiners comment on two main errors:
> 
> 1. Saying a reaction "won't happen" because Δ*S*<sub>total</sub> is negative when the question is about kinetic stability.
> 2. Saying a reaction "is feasible, so it will happen quickly".
> 
>   - Feasibility tells you the direction, not the speed.
> 
> For PET hydrolysis, Δ*S*<sub>total</sub> > 0 and Δ*G* < 0, but the high *E*<sub>a</sub> (~70 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup> from part c) keeps the reaction kinetically stable until you raise *T* or use an enzyme catalyst.

### 1() — 3 marks
> **[mark-scheme]**

> ii) Phenol undergoes electrophilic substitution with this electrophile more readily than benzene because:

> **OR**
> This polarises the electrophile more **[1 mark]**
>

> **[exam-tip]**
> For paper 5 electrophilic aromatic substitution, examiners reliably award the activation mark for **lone-pair donation**, not just "electron-donating".

**[2 marks]**

> 1 mark for the correct organic product (4-aminophenol, HO-C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>4</sub>-NH<sub>2</sub>) **AND **H<sub>2</sub>O as the by-product
> 
> 1 mark for a fully balanced equation: 6 [H] on the left and 2 H<sub>2</sub>O on the right; M2 is dependent on M1 or a near-miss
>

> 
> Equivalent equations using H<sub>2</sub> in place of [H] are accepted, e.g.:
>

>

> The conversion -NO<sub>2</sub> → -NH<sub>2</sub> is the standard A-level synthesis route to aromatic amines. Sn / HCl is the common reducing agent.
>

>

> **[exam-tip]**
> Key features of paracetamol's <sup>1</sup>H NMR:
> 
> - **Two doublets** in the aromatic region (6.5–7.5 ppm)
> 
>   - This is the classic 2H + 2H pattern of a 4- or *para*-disubstituted benzene (each ring H couples to its single neighbour on the adjacent carbon)
> - **One singlet** at 2.0 ppm
> 
>   - This is the acetyl -CH<sub>3</sub>, with no -CH neighbours
> - **Two broad singlets** at high δ (>9 ppm)
> 
>   - These are the -OH (phenol) and -NH (amide), both H-bonded and labile (often disappear in D<sub>2</sub>O exchange)
> 
> Three common mistakes on paper 5 NMR questions:
> 
> 1. Confusing **integration** (how many equivalent H at a given environment) with **multiplicity** (how many *neighbouring* H, via *n* + 1).
> 
>   - A doublet always has 2 lines, regardless of whether it integrates for 1H, 2H, or 3H.
> 2. Forgetting that the **carbonyl carbon has no attached H**
> 
>   - So, an acetyl -CH<sub>3</sub> adjacent to C=O always appears as a singlet (*n* = 0).
> 3. Treating the four aromatic H of paracetamol as four separate environments.
> 
>   - *4- or para*-disubstitution gives only **two** aromatic environments (2H each) by ring symmetry, hence the classic two-doublet pattern.
> 
> Always state the *n* value explicitly when applying *n* + 1, and use chemical-shift tables in the data booklet to anchor your assignments.
