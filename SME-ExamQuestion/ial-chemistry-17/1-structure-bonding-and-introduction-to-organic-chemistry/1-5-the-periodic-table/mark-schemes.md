## Q3 — medium — 14 marks · structured-questions

### 1() — 3 marks
**i)** The first ionisation energy is:

- The energy required to remove one mole of electrons **[1 mark]**
- from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions **[1 mark]**

**ii)** Equation for the first ionisation energy of sodium:

Na (g) → Na⁺ (g) + e⁻ **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) 1 mark for "energy required / needed to remove one mole of electrons" — must reference "one mole of electrons" or equivalent ("a mole of electrons"); reject "an electron" alone
> 
> 1 mark for "from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions" — state symbol (g) or word "gaseous" required for both atoms AND ions
> 
> Reject definitions that omit either the "one mole" specification or the gaseous state
> 
> ii) 1 mark for Na (g) → Na⁺ (g) + e⁻
> 
> State symbols (g) on both species mandatory
> 
> Accept e⁻ or e on RHS
> 
> Reject: missing state symbols; Na₂(g) (refers to a molecule, not an atom); removing more than one electron in a single step; products without (g)

> **[exam-tip]**
> When you define first ionisation energy, two scoring components must appear: "one mole of electrons" and "one mole of gaseous atoms → one mole of gaseous 1+ ions". State symbols **(g)** on both sides of the equation are mandatory — omitting them is the most common cause of lost marks in this style of question. Remember: only **one** electron is removed per ionisation step (the second IE is a separate process), and the starting species is always a single gaseous atom — never write Na₂(g), which refers to a molecule.

### 3() — 3 marks
**i)** Electronic configuration of aluminium (atomic number 13):

1s²2s²2p<sup>6</sup>3s²3p¹** ****[1 mark]**

**ii)** Explanation:

- Magnesium's outermost electron is in the **3s** sub-shell; aluminium's outermost electron is in the **3p** sub-shell **[1 mark]**
- The 3p sub-shell is at a **higher energy** than the 3s sub-shell (and is more shielded by the filled 3s electrons), so less energy is required to remove aluminium's outermost electron, its first ionisation energy is therefore lower than that of magnesium **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> i) 1 mark for 1s²2s²2p<sup>6</sup>3s²3p¹
> 
> Correct superscripts; correct order; no missing sub-shells
> 
> Accept "1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p1" if superscripts are clearly intended
> 
> ii) 1 mark for identifying **3s for Mg AND 3p for Al** (both sub-shells must be named explicitly; "different sub-shell" alone scores zero)
> 
> 1 mark for **3p is at higher energy AND/OR more shielded than 3s**
> 
> Accept: "3p electron is further from nucleus / more shielded by 3s / at a higher sub-shell energy"
> 
> Reject: vague statements like "different orbital" without naming 3s and 3p explicitly

> **[exam-tip]**
> Always name both sub-shells (3s for Mg, 3p for Al), writing "different sub-shell" alone does not score.
> 
> The Group 2 → Group 3 anomaly hinges on the p sub-shell being at a higher energy and more shielded by the filled s sub-shell beneath it; this effect outweighs the small increase in nuclear charge from Mg to Al, which is why aluminium's first IE is lower despite having one more proton.

### 4() — 2 marks
The general increase in first ionisation energy across Period 3 from sodium to argon is because:

- **Nuclear charge increases** across the period (Na has 11 protons; Ar has 18) **[1 mark]**
- Electrons are being added to the **same quantum shell** (n = 3), so shielding by inner-shell electrons stays approximately the same, the outer electron experiences a greater attraction to the nucleus, so more energy is required to remove it **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for **nuclear charge increases / number of protons increases** across the period
> 
> Reject "atomic number increases" alone (does not explicitly link to attractive force)
> 
> 1 mark for **same quantum shell / similar shielding** linked to **greater attraction → higher IE**
> 
> Accept "electrons added to the same shell", "shielding stays (approximately) the same"
> 
> Reject "no shielding" or "shielding increases"
> 
> Both points required — simply stating "IE increases" is not enough

> **[exam-tip]**
> Edexcel mark schemes for "explain the trend across Period 3" award marks for: (1) what changes (nuclear charge increases), and (2) what stays the same plus its consequence (same shell / similar shielding → greater attraction → higher IE). Do **not** state "atomic radius decreases" as a cause, the decreasing radius is a consequence of the same underlying effect, not a separate reason.

### 5() — 2 marks
The group of the Periodic Table **Z** belongs to is:

- The largest jump in successive ionisation energies occurs between the 3rd and 4th IE (from 2740 to 11 580 kJ mol⁻¹, roughly a 4-fold increase) **[1 mark]**

- This indicates that the 4th electron is being removed from a new (inner) quantum shell, so Z has three electrons in its outermost shell, Z is therefore in Group 3 **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for identifying the largest jump between the 3rd and 4th IE
> 
> Accept any quantitative reference (e.g. "from 2740 to 11 580", "~4× increase")
> 
> 1 mark for concluding **Group 3**
> 
> Accept "3 outer electrons" if combined with the jump-rule reasoning
> 
> Reject Group 4 (mistakenly counting the large value as part of the outer shell)
> 
> Reject Group 1 (confusing "largest jump position" with element number)

> **[exam-tip]**
> The "jump rule" for successive IEs: if the largest jump is between the nth and (n+1)th IE, the element is in Group n. This is because the (n+1)th electron is being removed from a deeper (inner) shell, which takes much more energy. Always look for the biggest SINGLE jump, not the overall trend.

### 6() — 2 marks
i) Equation for the second ionisation energy of magnesium:

Mg⁺ (g) → Mg²⁺ (g) + e⁻ **[1 mark]**

ii)

- <sup>24</sup>Mg has a more stable nuclear configuration than ²⁵Mg or ²⁶Mg — its even number of protons and neutrons (12 of each) gives it a balanced neutron-to-proton ratio and greater nuclear stability, so it is the most abundant isotope. **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**

>

>

>

State symbols (g) on both sides remain mandatory, and only **one** electron is removed per ionisation step. For isotope abundance, the underlying reason is nuclear stability: isotopes with even numbers of both protons and neutrons (like <sup>24</sup>Mg with 12p + 12n) tend to be more abundant due to nuclear shell effects.

### 2() — 1 marks
**Correct answer: D** — Element C's outermost electron occupies a 3p sub-shell, which is at a higher energy and more shielded than the 3s sub-shell holding element B's outermost electron

**Final answer:** **The correct answer is D**

- Element B is magnesium (1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup>) — outermost electron is in the 3s sub-shell
- Element C is aluminium (1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup> 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>1</sup>) — outermost electron is in the 3p sub-shell
- The 3p sub-shell is at a slightly higher energy and is more shielded than the 3s sub-shell

**A** is incorrect as aluminium's 3p<sup>1</sup> electron is unpaired (pairing of 3p electrons would only occur from sulfur onwards)

**B** is incorrect as element C (Al) has MORE protons than element B (Mg); nuclear charge increases across the period

> **[exam-tip]**

### 3() — 1 marks
**Correct answer: D** — Element F has 3p<sup>4</sup> with one paired orbital — the spin-pair repulsion makes one of the paired electrons easier to remove than an electron from element E's 3p<sup>3</sup>

**Final answer:** **The correct answer is D**

- Element E is phosphorus (... 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>3</sup>) — three unpaired electrons in the 3p sub-shell (↑ ↑ ↑)
- Element F is sulfur (... 3s<sup>2</sup> 3p<sup>4</sup>) — one paired orbital (↑↓ ↑ ↑)
- The two electrons in the paired 3p orbital of sulfur repel each other (spin-pair repulsion)
- This extra repulsion makes one of the paired electrons easier to remove than any of P's unpaired 3p electrons → lower 1st IE for sulfur

**A** is incorrect as both elements have the same inner-shell electrons (1s<sup>2</sup> 2s<sup>2</sup> 2p<sup>6</sup>), so shielding is essentially the same

**B** is incorrect as atomic radius DECREASES from P to S (more protons, same shell)

**C** is incorrect as both P and S have their outermost electron in the same sub-shell (3p)

> **[exam-tip]**
> The Group 5 → Group 6 anomaly requires you to mention BOTH "paired" electrons AND "repulsion". Drawing electrons-in-boxes for the 3p sub-shells of P and S is the safest way to demonstrate the comparison.
