### 3() — 4 marks
To calculate the percentage by mass of calcium carbonate in the limestone sample:

- Calculate the initial moles of HCl added and the moles of NaOH used (equal to the moles of excess HCl)

moles = concentration x volume (dm<sup>3</sup>)

*n*(HCl<sub>start</sub>) = 0.500 x $\frac{50.0}{1000}$
*n*(HCl<sub>start</sub>) = 0.0250 mol

*n*(NaOH) = 0.200 x $\frac{25.0}{1000}$

*n*(NaOH) = 0.00500 mol = *n*(HCl<sub>excess</sub>) **[1 mark]**

*n*(HCl<sub>reacted</sub>) = 0.0250 − 0.00500

*n*(HCl<sub>reacted</sub>) = 0.0200 mol **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the moles of CaCO<sub>3:</sub>

  - Apply the 1:2 stoichiometric ratio from CaCO<sub>3</sub> + 2HCl → CaCl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O + CO<sub>2</sub>:

*n*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = $\frac{0.0200}{2}$ 
*n*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = = 0.0100 mol

- Calculate the mass of CaCO<sub>3</sub>:

mass = moles x *M*<sub>r</sub>

*m*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = 0.0100 x 100.0

*m*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = 1.00 g **[1 mark]**

- Calculate the percentage by mass:

percentage by mass = `fraction numerator m open parentheses CaCO subscript 3 close parentheses over denominator total space sample space mass end fraction` x 100

% CaCO<sub>3</sub> = $\frac{1.00}{1.25}$ x 100

% CaCO<sub>3</sub> = **80.0%** **[1 mark]**

> **[mark-scheme]**
> 1 mark for initial moles of HCl = 0.0250 mol, showing *c* x *V* with volume converted to dm<sup>3</sup>.
> 
> 1 mark for moles of NaOH = 0.00500 mol, correctly identified as the moles of excess HCl.
> 
> 1 mark for moles of HCl that reacted with CaCO<sub>3</sub> by subtraction: 0.0250 − 0.00500 = 0.0200 mol.
> 
> 1 mark for moles and mass of CaCO<sub>3</sub> using the 1:2 stoichiometric ratio: *n*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = 0.0100 mol, *m*(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = 1.00 g.
> 
> 1 mark for final answer 80.0% or 80%, including the % symbol.
> 
> Allow error carried forward across steps: if a step is wrong but the subsequent method is correct, later marks can still be awarded.

> **[exam-tip]**
> The core principle of back-titration is:
> 
> **n(reacted) = n(added) − n(remaining)**
> 
> The most common error is using the titre (moles of NaOH used) as the moles that reacted with CaCO<sub>3</sub>, skipping the subtraction step entirely.
> 
> The CaCO<sub>3</sub> + 2HCl reaction has a 1:2 ratio. Always write out the balanced equation and check the stoichiometry before dividing. A 1:1 ratio is the second most common error in this question type.
> 
> In percentage-by-mass questions, remember to divide by the **sample mass** (1.25 g), not by the mass of CaCO<sub>3</sub> alone.

## Q6 — medium — 1 marks · multiple-choice-questions

### 10() — 1 marks
**Correct answer: B** — reducing ability of the halide ions

**Final answer:** **The correct answer is ****B**** because:**

- Halide ions act as reducing agents as they can donate electrons to other species:

  - 2X<sup>-</sup> → X<sub>2</sub> + 2e<sup>-</sup>
- As you go down the group, the ionic radius increases and the attraction between the outer electrons and the nucleus decreases
- Therefore the larger the halide ions, the more readily they will lose their outer electron, so their reducing ability increases down the group
- This is demonstrated by the halogen displacement reactions where a more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from a halide solution of the less reactive halogen

  - Iodide ions lose their electrons more easily than bromide ions
  - Bromide ions lose their electrons more easily than chloride ions

**A** is incorrect as  the oxidising ability of the molecular halogens decreases down the group

**C** is incorrect as  the electrostatic attraction between nucleus and outer shell of electrons decreases down the group

**D** is incorrect as electronegativity decreases down the group

## Q10 — medium — 1 marks · multiple-choice-questions

### 1() — 1 marks
**Correct answer: D** — KI

**Final answer:** **The correct answer is D**

- Oxidising ability of halogens decreases down Group 7: Cl<sub>2</sub> > Br<sub>2</sub> > I<sub>2</sub>
- Cl<sub>2</sub> can therefore oxidise Br<sup>–</sup> and I<sup>–</sup>, but cannot oxidise F<sup>–</sup> (F is above Cl) or Cl<sup>–</sup> (same element, no redox)
- Cyclohexane (density 0.79 g cm<sup>–³</sup>) is less dense than water (1.00 g cm<sup>–³</sup>), so it forms the **upper** layer. Halogens are non-polar and far more soluble in cyclohexane than in water, so they concentrate in the upper layer
- Key cyclohexane-layer colours: Br<sub>2</sub> = orange; I<sub>2</sub> = purple/violet
- Upper layer purple/violet → I<sub>2</sub> → the original halide must have been KI:

Cl<sub>2</sub> + 2KI → 2KCl + I<sub>2</sub>

**A** is incorrect as Cl<sub>2</sub> **does** oxidise Br<sup>–</sup> to Br<sub>2</sub>, but Br<sub>2</sub> in cyclohexane is **orange** (not purple); this distractor catches students who stop at "displacement occurred" without checking the colour

**B** is incorrect as Cl<sub>2</sub> and Cl<sup>–</sup> are the same element, no redox is possible; the cyclohexane layer would stay colourless

**C** is incorrect as F<sup>–</sup> cannot be oxidised by Cl<sub>2</sub> (fluorine is above chlorine in Group 7); no reaction occurs and the cyclohexane layer would stay colourless

> **[exam-tip]**
> This question combines three pieces of Group 7 knowledge, all three must be correct to land the answer:
> 
> - which halogen can oxidise which halide
> - the colour of each halogen in a non-polar solvent
> - which layer cyclohexane forms when added to water
> 
> Colours to memorise exactly, the January 2022 examiner report specifically flagged candidates writing "iodine is brown in cyclohexane" as a recurring error:
> 
> - **I**<sub>2</sub>** in cyclohexane** → purple / violet (pink also accepted)
> - **I**<sub>2</sub>** in water** → brown / yellow / pale orange
> - **Br**<sub>2</sub>** in cyclohexane** → orange (yellow, brown or red-brown also accepted)
> - **Br**<sub>2</sub>** in water** → orange / yellow / brown
> - **Cl**<sub>2</sub>** in cyclohexane** → pale green / near-colourless
> 
> Cyclohexane (density ≈ 0.78–0.79 g cm<sup>–³</sup>) is less dense than water (1.00 g cm<sup>–³</sup>), so it floats on top as the **upper** layer, where the halogen concentrates and gives the cleaner, more distinctive colour.
> 
> In structured (Section B) questions asking you to describe what you'd see, mark schemes require a **two-part** answer: **layer + colour** (e.g. "the upper / cyclohexane layer turns purple"). Giving the colour alone loses the layer mark, and vice versa.
